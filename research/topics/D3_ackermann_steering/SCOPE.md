## Overview

Ackermann steering is the standard arrangement for wheeled vehicles that turn by pivoting one or more axles' wheels rather than by driving the left and right sides at different speeds. The mechanism traces to a 1816/1818 carriage-steering linkage (Lankensperger/Ackermann) that lets the inner and outer front wheels sweep different-radius arcs about a common centre, avoiding the tyre scrub that a parallel-steer axle would cause. For modelling and control, a car-like vehicle is almost universally reduced to the **kinematic bicycle model** (one virtual front wheel, one virtual rear wheel), which is exactly equivalent to the full four-wheel Ackermann geometry under the no-sideslip (rolling-without-slipping) assumption and is the basis of nearly all path-tracking and motion-planning work for such vehicles (Rajamani; Gillespie; Snider 2009). The kinematic model is known to degrade at higher lateral acceleration (commonly cited threshold a_y < 0.5·μg), where a dynamic bicycle model with tyre slip angles (understeer/oversteer) is needed instead. Minimum-turning-radius path geometry for forward-only motion follows Dubins' 1957 curvature-constrained shortest-path result, and with reverse gear allowed follows Reeds & Shepp (1990) — both underpin Nav2's Hybrid-A* planner's DUBIN/REEDS_SHEPP motion models for car-like robots. Path-tracking control for Ackermann vehicles is dominated by two lineages: geometric methods (pure pursuit, Coulter 1992; its Nav2 "Regulated" variant, Macenski et al. 2023, which explicitly supports Ackermann as well as differential bases) and the Stanford Racing Team's Stanley method (Hoffmann, Tomlin, Montemerlo & Thrun 2007), alongside MPC/MPPI approaches (Nav2's own `AckermannMotionModel` in nav2_mppi_controller enforces a `min_turning_r` constraint). ROS 2 represents Ackermann vehicles at the message level with `ackermann_msgs` (`AckermannDrive`/`AckermannDriveStamped`) and at the control level with `ros2_controllers`' `steering_controllers_library`, which implements bicycle (1 steer + 1 drive), tricycle and full Ackermann (2 steer + 2 drive) inverse kinematics and odometry. Separately from the control-theory side, a substantial literature compares Ackermann ("explicit steering") against skid-steering directly: the classic CMU comparison (Shamah 1999) found skid-steering needs roughly double the power of an explicit point turn because of larger sideslip angles, and odometry-calibration surveys (Sousa, Petry & Moreira) treat Ackermann as one of a small number of distinct steering geometries with its own calibration methods, separate from differential/skid drive. Off-the-shelf Ackermann platforms span three size classes relevant to a small-to-mid outdoor ground vehicle: 1/10–1/8-scale RC-car-based research platforms (F1TENTH, MuSHR, MIT RACECAR), purpose-built mid-size rovers (AgileX Hunter 2.0, AgileX LIMO's Ackermann mode), and full-size golf-cart/scooter-based platforms with drive-by-wire kits (Polaris GEM + Dataspeed, AutonomouStuff PACMod, OpenPodcar). IGVC's own AutoNav Challenge rules fix a concrete outdoor envelope this topic measures against: course lanes 10–20 ft wide with a required minimum turning radius of 5 ft, obstacles including drums and simulated potholes, and a 5 mph speed cap — giving the geometric constraints (wheelbase, track, max steering angle) against which any Ackermann design's minimum turning radius must be checked. Wider field robotics shows steering-mode plurality even on vehicles not usually thought of as "cars": agricultural robots are compared across Ackermann, differential and independent-steering configurations; and Mars/lunar rover mobility studies explicitly include an "Ackermann turn" (and "point turn") as one of several selectable locomotion modes alongside skid steering, with passive/Ackermann-like steering reported to need less power and give more precise dead reckoning than skid steering in at least one recent rover study.
