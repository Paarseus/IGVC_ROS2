## Summary

- Ackermann steering reduces a car-like vehicle to the **kinematic bicycle model** (one virtual front wheel, one virtual rear wheel) under a no-sideslip assumption; this model is suitable at low-to-moderate speed where inertial effects are small compared with the no-slip constraint, and loses accuracy as speed/lateral acceleration rise [D3-S31, p. 5 (§III)].
- Across three independent comparisons (a CMU wheeled test rig, a CMU planetary-rover testbed, and a JPL rover-mobility taxonomy), **explicit/Ackermann-type steering consistently needs less power and tracks arcs more precisely than skid steering** — skid steering drew roughly double the power of an explicit point turn on the CMU rig [D3-S05, Abstract], and on the Zoë2 rover skid steering used up to 30% more power at high steering angles and had on average over 100% drive-radius error vs 12% for passive/Ackermann-like steering [D3-S18, p. 61, p. 67]. The tradeoff is that single-axle Ackermann cannot turn in place and has a materially larger minimum turning radius than a dual-steered or skid/point-turn configuration (1.94 m vs 1.29 m dual-Ackermann vs 0 m point-turn on one agricultural robot) [D3-S19, Figure 4.5/Table].
- ROS 2 support for Ackermann vehicles is mature at the message level (`ackermann_msgs`, stable since the ROS 1 era) [D3-S07a; D3-S07b; D3-S07c] and at the control level (`ros2_controllers`' `steering_controllers_library`, with bicycle/tricycle/full-Ackermann inverse-kinematics controllers) [D3-S09–S12], and Nav2 explicitly supports Ackermann kinematics in its Regulated Pure Pursuit controller, Smac Hybrid-A* planner (Dubins/Reeds-Shepp motion models with a configurable minimum turning radius), and MPPI controller (a dedicated `AckermannMotionModel` that hard-clips angular velocity to respect a minimum turning radius) [D3-S02; D3-S14; D3-S15, D3-S16].
- IGVC's AutoNav Challenge fixes a concrete outdoor envelope — course lanes 10-20 ft wide, minimum turning radius not less than 5 ft, 5 ft minimum obstacle clearance, 5 mph speed cap, 1 mph minimum, 2-ft-diameter simulated potholes, ramps up to 15% grade [D3-S26, §II.2]; past IGVC teams that chose Ackermann steering explicitly recorded "large turning radius" as its main disadvantage even when choosing it anyway [D3-S27, p. 4], while a dual/four-wheel-steer ("double Ackermann") team reported it let them make tighter turns than single-axle Ackermann and perform strafing maneuvers a skid-steer chassis cannot [D3-S28, p. 2, p. 5].
- Off-the-shelf Ackermann platforms span three size/cost classes directly relevant to a small-to-mid outdoor vehicle: 1/10-scale RC-car research platforms costing roughly $600-$3,000 (MuSHR, F1TENTH) [D3-S21, p. 3; D3-S20], purpose-built mid-size rovers like the AgileX Hunter 2.0 (650 mm wheelbase, 33° max steering angle, 150 kg payload, 1.5 m/s) [D3-S24, pp. 5, 17], and converted golf-cart/mobility-scooter platforms with drive-by-wire kits costing on the order of $2,000-tens of thousands (OpenPodcar $2,000-7,000; AutonomouStuff PACMod on commercial donor vehicles) [D3-S23, p. 2; D3-S25a].

## Findings

### 1. Ackermann steering geometry and mechanism

- In 1759 Erasmus Darwin proposed a carriage-steering mechanism that steers each wheel individually rather than the whole front axle (the common practice of the time), reducing tyre scuffing by giving the inner wheel a larger steering angle than the outer one; a few years later G. Lankensperger proposed the same idea independently, and R. Ackermann patented it in 1818, after which, in 1878, C. Jeantaud proposed a four-bar linkage mechanism that approximates this geometry [D3-S34, p. 1 (§1)] — a layout still used today, in modified form with tie rods in place of the connecting rod, in current industrial practice [D3-S34, p. 4].
- The exact Ackermann condition relating the outer- and inner-wheel steering angles δ_o and δ_i to the vehicle's track width T and wheelbase w is 1/tan(δ_o) − 1/tan(δ_i) = T/w — "a standard and well-known derivation" that defines steering-system kinematics where the wheel-spin axes of the steerable wheels intersect a common centre of rotation on the rear-axle line, for non-zero steering angles at negligible lateral acceleration [D3-S34, p. 3, eq. (1)].
- A steering layout exactly satisfying this condition is called **100% Ackermann**; equal steer angles on both front wheels (no inner/outer difference) is **0% Ackermann** (parallel steering); a layout between the two is **less than 100% Ackermann**; a layout with more inner/outer angle difference than ideal Ackermann is **more than 100% Ackermann**; and a layout where the outer wheel is steered *more* than the inner wheel (δ_i − δ_o negative) is called **reverse-** or **anti-Ackermann** [D3-S34, p. 4]. Three distinct quantitative ratio definitions are in use for comparing a real linkage to ideal Ackermann: a linkage-geometry ratio ν_τ = τ/(T/2) (τ = distance from the four-bar linkage's rocker-axis intersection to the rear axle); a wheelbase-projection ratio ν_w = w/L (L = distance from the front axle to where the front wheels' velocity-centre projection falls on the wheelbase line); and a "net-steer ratio" ν_n = (δ_i − δ_o)/(δ_i,a − δ_o,a), the actual inner/outer steer-angle difference divided by the difference the exact Ackermann geometry would give for the same inner-wheel angle [D3-S34, p. 4, eqs. (2)-(4)].
- In one measured real four-bar steering-trapezoid linkage on a Formula SAE car, the actual inner/outer wheel-angle relationship deviated from the vehicle's own target "Ackermann correction coefficient" (a slip-angle-adjusted target percentage, distinct from and lower than the 100% zero-slip-speed ideal, because at racing speed the outer tyre needs the larger slip angle) by an absolute Ackermann-percentage error of 8.92%; multi-iteration adjustment of the steering trapezoid's hardpoint coordinates in ADAMS Car reduced this error to 4.54% [D3-S35, p. 1 (Abstract), p. 2].
- The only identified peer-reviewed, same-vehicle quantitative test of how much the Ackermann-ratio choice itself affects race-car performance (as opposed to geometry alone) found the effect to be small: comparing a baseline, a 100%-Ackermann, and a 0%-Ackermann (parallel) front-steering layout on a validated Formula SAE vehicle model around a full racetrack lap, the maximum lap-time difference among the three layouts was 26 ms (0.03%) with toe and camber neglected, rising to 329 ms (0.4%) once toe and camber were included — in both cases much smaller than the effect of the toe-angle setting itself, which the authors conclude is "the most influential parameter when it comes to performance of the vehicle under investigation" [D3-S34, p. 16 (§6-7)]. The authors state this is, to their knowledge, the first study to examine the effect of the Ackermann/parallel steering choice itself on vehicle performance, as opposed to only its effect on tyre wear/scrub, because "the exact Ackermann steering may not be the ideal steering in many scenarios — indeed it was developed under the assumption of zero tyre slippage, which is not the case realised unless very low speeds are considered" [D3-S34, p. 1 (Abstract/§1)].
- Ackermann steering vehicles are formally defined (in a JPL rover-mobility taxonomy) as one of three wheeled-vehicle classes by center-of-rotation constraint: **skid steering** (all wheels fixed-direction — the source does not itself state where this leaves the center of rotation), **Ackermann steering** (at least one pair of wheels non-steerable, so the center of rotation must lie along that fixed-wheel axis), and **all-wheel steering** (center of rotation unconstrained for any motion) [D3-S17, p. 2 (§II)].
- In a tandem/multi-wheel vehicle, a tandem pair of wheels on one side can be treated as one larger "virtual" wheel whose axis is equidistant from the two real axes, which minimizes slip when computing the Ackermann center of rotation for that configuration [D3-S17, p. 2].
- A vehicle with two independently steerable axles can run in at least four distinct geometric modes: single-axle **Ackermann** (front wheels only, steering toward a center on the rear-axle line), **Dual Ackermann** (front and rear wheels both steered toward a common center on a line midway between the axles, halving the number of independent steering angles needed by symmetry), **Crab** (both axles steered to the same angle so all wheels follow parallel straight tracks, i.e. the vehicle translates sideways without rotating), and **Point Turn** (wheels oriented so the vehicle rotates about its own center, zero turning radius) [D3-S19, pp. 21-27 (§4.1)].
- On one measured four-wheel robot, these four modes have very different minimum turning radii / maximum curvatures: Ackermann 1.94 m (curvature 0.515 m⁻¹), Dual Ackermann 1.29 m (curvature 0.775 m⁻¹, i.e. 34% tighter than single-axle Ackermann), Crab infinite radius (zero curvature — pure translation), Point Turn zero radius (infinite curvature) [D3-S19, p. 24, Figure 4.5 and accompanying table].
- Driving sideways (a crab maneuver) does not load a rocker-bogie-type suspension evenly, which reduces vehicle stability; the JPL taxonomy states crab maneuvers are therefore undesirable for long traverses and can only be performed by all-wheel-steering vehicles, not fixed-axle Ackermann vehicles [D3-S17, p. 2].
- The motion of any steerable wheeled vehicle can be described in terms of circular arcs; an arc of zero radius is a rotate-in-place (point-turn) motion, and the only geometric difference between an Ackermann vehicle and an all-wheel-steering vehicle is that the Ackermann vehicle's arc center is constrained to lie on its fixed-wheel axis, whereas the all-wheel-steering vehicle's arc center is unconstrained [D3-S17, p. 2].

### 2. Kinematic bicycle/Ackermann model and its validity

- The kinematic (no-slip) model of a wheeled vehicle is commonly called the "car-like robot," "bicycle model," "kinematic model," or "single-track model" interchangeably in the motion-planning/control literature, and is derived as a differential constraint on the motion of the vehicle's reference point(s) [D3-S31, p. 5 (§III)].
- This kinematic model is suitable for planning and control at low speeds (e.g. parking maneuvers and urban driving) where inertial effects are small compared with the mobility limits imposed by the no-slip assumption on the wheels; a drawback is that it permits instantaneous steering-angle changes, which can be problematic if a motion planner generates solutions containing such discontinuities (continuity can be imposed by augmenting the model so the steering angle integrates a commanded rate instead of being commanded directly) [D3-S31, p. 5 (§III)].
- A later ETH Zürich miniature-car-like-robot platform paper (Chronos/CRS) likewise frames the kinematic bicycle model, alongside a dynamic bicycle model with a tire force term, as the two standard plant models used for simulation and control-design benchmarking of car-like robots — i.e. the kinematic model remains the default low-complexity choice, with the dynamic model available when tire slip must be represented [D3-S32, p. 3 (§V-A)].
- Pure Pursuit's own geometric derivation places the vehicle coordinate frame at the rear axle with the x-axis colinear with it, because (citing a 1990 reference internal to that CMU report) propulsion and steering are geometrically decoupled in that frame — the same convention used by the kinematic bicycle model's rear-axle point [D3-S03, p. 5 (§2.0)].
- The numeric lateral-acceleration threshold at which the kinematic bicycle model's validity is usually bounded, **a_y < 0.5µg**, traces to a car-like-robot motion-planning paper, not to a vehicle-dynamics-modeling comparison paper: a paper from the Center of Robotics at Mines ParisTech on consistency between a kinematic-bicycle-model motion planner and a PID controller states that, "to ensure the validity of the kinematic bicycle model, we used the criterion derived in [the authors' own prior IEEE IV 2017 paper]," namely that the lateral acceleration a_y (defined as V²/R, where R is the path's curvature radius) "should be lower than 0.5µg"; the same paper separately summarizes that prior paper's own conclusion as "the lateral acceleration should always remain lower than 0.5µg, where µ is the road friction coefficient," and from this derives a maximum-steering-angle-vs-speed bound, δ_max(V) = atan[(l_f/l_r + 1)·tan(asin(0.5µg·l_r/V²))], used to keep a motion planner inside the model's own validity domain (l_f, l_r = distance from the vehicle's centre of gravity to the front/rear axle) [D3-S36, p. 4, eq. (5)]. A **separate** 0.5g lateral-acceleration limit, used elsewhere in the literature to bound a 2-degree-of-freedom *dynamic* (not kinematic) bicycle model's validity, is traced by the same paper to Park et al. 2009 (*Proc. IMechE Part D*) via an intermediate 2013 ASME paper — i.e. two numerically-similar but distinct 0.5g/0.5µg criteria, for two different model types, appear in this literature and should not be conflated [D3-S36, p. 1].
- Kong et al. 2015 (not independently downloaded — see Foundational references) compared a kinematic and a linear-tire dynamic single-track model against experimental vehicle measurements and reported **similar open-loop errors over short horizons** between the two models — a different, complementary finding to the lateral-acceleration validity threshold above, about model choice specifically for short-horizon receding-horizon control rather than about a hard validity boundary [D3-S37, p. 1 (§1, ref. 6)].
- A separate simulation study (also not independently downloaded, same 2017 IEEE IV paper as above) compared a kinematic bicycle model against a 9-degree-of-freedom vehicle model and found the kinematic model becomes "increasingly inaccurate at higher speeds and steering angles" specifically because it does not capture slip-induced understeer, consistent with the 0.5µg bound being a speed/steering-angle-dependent limit rather than a fixed one [D3-S37, p. 1 (§1, ref. 10)].

### 3. Turning-radius and maneuvering geometry

- For the "simple car" variant of the kinematic bicycle model — speed and steering angle as the two control inputs, steering angle bounded by |φ| ≤ φ_max < π/2 — the minimum turning radius is given in closed form as **ρ_min = L / tan(φ_max)**, where L is the wheelbase; this single relation is what makes a bounded maximum steering angle equivalent to a bounded minimum turning radius for any Ackermann/car-like vehicle [D3-S33, ch. 13, §13.1.2.1 ("A Simple Car"), pp. 725-726]. (See Findings §2 for a second, speed-dependent maximum-steering-angle relation, δ_max(V), derived instead from the kinematic model's own lateral-acceleration validity limit rather than from a fixed mechanical steering-angle limit.)
- Reeds & Shepp (1990) state that Dubins (1957) proved that when a car cannot reverse and cusps are not allowed, the shortest path between two configurations is always composed of arcs of the minimum turning radius and straight segments in one of a small sufficient set of patterns (at most 6 candidate paths of the forms "CCC" and "CSC," where C is a circular arc of the minimum turning radius and S is a straight segment) [D3-S01, p. 367 (Introduction)].
- Reeds & Shepp's own result extends this to a car that can also reverse: the shortest path is then of the form "CCSCC" (arcs and segments may vanish; reversals/cusps between arcs or segments are allowed), with a sufficient set of at most 68 (usually far fewer) candidate paths per pair of endpoints and directions, given in explicit closed form [D3-S01, p. 367].
- Nav2's Smac Hybrid-A* planner implements both of these as selectable `motion_model_for_search` options, `"DUBIN"` (default) or `"REEDS_SHEPP"`, with a `minimum_turning_radius` parameter (default 0.4 m, must be > 0) used both for search-graph expansion and for the smoother's maximum-curvature computation; a reverse-direction heuristic penalty is applied only in the REEDS_SHEPP model [D3-S14, "minimum_turning_radius" and "reverse_penalty" entries], and the recommended analytic-expansion distance is never smaller than 4-5× the minimum turning radius or planning times begin to spike [D3-S14, "analytic_expansion_max_length" entry].
- Nav2's MPPI controller enforces a minimum turning radius constraint directly on sampled control sequences rather than on a planned path: its `AckermannMotionModel::applyConstraints()` iterates every timestep of the sampled angular-velocity sequence `wz` and, wherever the ratio `|vx|/|wz|` would be below the configured `min_turning_r` (default 0.2 m), clips `wz` down to `copysign(|vx|/min_turning_r, wz)` — i.e. it hard-limits how sharply any rollout may turn relative to its forward speed [D3-S16, lines 107-121; D3-S15, "AckermannMotionModel" §].
- Regulated Pure Pursuit treats the turning-radius constraint differently again: because it is a pure path-tracking controller (no path feasibility check of its own), Macenski et al. state that for Ackermann-steering robots the input path must *already* be drivable given the vehicle's kinematic limits on minimum turning radius before RPP tracks it, whereas for a differential-drive robot (which can rotate in place) this constraint does not apply and any holonomically-planned path may be used [D3-S02, p. 9 (§6 "Limitations")].

### 4. Odometry, dead reckoning and calibration for car-like robots

- A 2020 IEEE survey restricted to the time period since UMBmark (1996) found substantially fewer published odometry-calibration methods specific to Ackermann steering than to differential drive: fifteen methods identified for differential drive versus only three for Ackermann (and two for tricycle, three for omnidirectional) — a disparity the authors note does not match the real-world prevalence of each geometry [D3-S06, Abstract].
- The three Ackermann-specific calibration methods found are: (1) a self-calibration algorithm (shared with tricycle AGVs) needing only one run and no initial parameter vector; (2) a least-squares method needing several well-conditioned runs (higher effort, not self-calibrating); and (3)/(4) two automobile-industry methods driven by automatic-parking-system accuracy needs — one adjusting kinematic parameters from the position-error center of gravity over 5 runs each direction, the other using final-orientation error and (unlike the first) making no trigonometric approximations, which gave it better odometry accuracy in the survey's comparison [D3-S06, p. 297 (§III-B), p. 298 (§III-B)].
- The self-calibrating method (shared between Ackermann and tricycle geometries) takes only about 15 minutes to run, substantially less effort than the least-squares alternative that requires several runs [D3-S06, p. 298 (§III-B)].
- `ros2_controllers`' `steering_controllers_library` computes odometry generically from whichever concrete kinematics (bicycle/tricycle/Ackermann) is configured, and publishes it both as a `nav_msgs/Odometry` message and as a TF transform, alongside a `control_msgs/SteeringControllerStatus` state message — i.e. the library treats odometry computation as a shared service independent of which steering geometry is in use [D3-S09, lines 34, 104-109].

### 5. Precision and energy/power comparison with skid-steering

- In a controlled, same-vehicle experiment (a wheeled robot capable of both skid and explicit/Ackermann-type steering, intended as a proxy for planetary-rover locomotion), power and torque for skid and explicit turning converge to equal values only at infinite radius (straight-line driving); as turn radius decreases toward a point turn, skid steering requires progressively more power and torque than explicit steering at every finite radius because of its larger sideslip angle, and at the point-turn limit skid steering needs **approximately double** the power of an explicit point turn [D3-S05, Abstract].
- On a passive-steering ("Zoë2") planetary-rover testbed built with an identical chassis switchable between locked (skid-steer) and unlocked (passive/Ackermann-like steer) axles, straight-line power draw was statistically indistinguishable between the two configurations, but as commanded steering angle increased, skid steering showed a statistically significant, strongly linear increase in power (r² = 0.92, p = 2.5×10⁻¹⁰) while passive steering showed no significant increase (r² = 0.02, p = 0.54); at the highest tested steering angle (0.40 rad / 23°) skid steering consumed about 30% more energy than passive steering [D3-S18, p. 61 (§4.4)].
- The same rover testbed found passive steering "substantially better at blind navigation" on commanded drive-arc trajectories, averaging about 12% drive-radius error, versus skid steering experiencing "over 100% error on average" — and passive steering required about 30% less energy than skid steering on the tightest turn radius tested (1.5 m) [D3-S18, p. 67 (§5.2)]. The authors attribute the efficiency gain to passive steering letting the wheels align with the path tangent, "virtually eliminating sideslip on the wheels and allowing fully in-line torque transfer along the drive arc" [D3-S18, p. 61].
- The general direction of this finding (explicit/Ackermann-type steering beats skid steering on both power and path-following precision, with the gap growing as the turn gets tighter) is consistent across the two independent testbeds (CMU wheeled rig [D3-S05] and CMU rover [D3-S18]) and the JPL rover taxonomy's qualitative framing [D3-S17], even though the exact numbers differ because the studies measure different quantities (point-turn power ratio vs. an arc-turning power/precision sweep) — see *Disagreements between sources* for how this is reconciled against Ackermann's maneuverability cost.

### 6. ROS 2 message- and control-level software support

- `ackermann_msgs` defines two messages, `AckermannDrive` (steering angle + steering-angle velocity, speed + acceleration + jerk, all measured at the vehicle's center of rotation) and `AckermannDriveStamped` (a `std_msgs/Header` plus an embedded `AckermannDrive`); it explicitly models the commanded steering angle as the yaw of a single virtual front wheel at the center of the front axle — i.e. the bicycle-model convention — "not the angle of the steering wheel inside the passenger compartment" [D3-S07a (AckermannDrive.msg), full text; D3-S07b (AckermannDriveStamped.msg)].
- The package states it was defined by the ROS "Ackermann steering group" and documents front-wheel Ackermann steering vehicles specifically [D3-S07c (README.rst)].
- `ros2_controllers`' `steering_controllers_library` is a shared library "for mobile robot controllers with steering drives (2 degrees of freedom), with so-called non-holonomic constraints," implementing only inverse kinematics and odometry (no path-tracking feedback loop of its own); it takes a body-twist command (linear x, angular z) and currently backs three concrete controllers distinguished by joint count: **bicycle** (one steering joint, one drive joint — "if your Ackermann steering vehicle uses differentials on axes… you can command only one traction velocity and steering angle for virtual wheels in the middle of the axes"), **tricycle** (one steering joint, two drive joints) and **ackermann** (two steering joints, two drive joints, i.e. the full four-wheel geometry) [D3-S09, lines 13-41; D3-S10; D3-S12].
- All three concrete controllers support both front-steering and rear-steering configurations via a `front_steering` parameter, and all publish odometry as both `nav_msgs/Odometry` and a TF transform [D3-S09, lines 30, 66-92, 107-108].

### 7. Nav2 and path-tracking/planning algorithms for car-like vehicles

- Macenski et al.'s Regulated Pure Pursuit paper states that "PP and its variants are applicable to Ackermann and differential-drive robots due to PP's formulation supporting dynamics with longitudinal motion and a turning rate in body-fixed frame," but that for Ackermann robots the input path must already satisfy the vehicle's kinematic turning-radius limits, unlike for differential-drive robots [D3-S02, p. 3, p. 9].
- Nav2's own live documentation for `use_rotate_to_heading` (a Regulated Pure Pursuit parameter, default `true`) states it is "recommended on for all robot types that can rotate in place," and that it cannot be set `true` at the same time as `allow_reversing` because the two would create ambiguous situations — i.e. the parameter is framed around in-place rotation capability (which a single fixed-axle Ackermann vehicle lacks), not as Ackermann-specific or differential-specific per se [D3-S13, lines 180-187].
- The Stanley method's originating paper proves global asymptotic stability for its kinematic steering control law (which actuates the front-wheel orientation directly, "not the vehicle's body," with respect to the desired trajectory) and reports field results from the Stanford Racing Team's "Stanley" vehicle over a 132-mile off-road DARPA Grand Challenge 2005 race: a typical RMS cross-track error "under 0.1 m" (standard deviation 0.09 m) at an average speed of 19.1 mph over gravel, loose sand, mud puddles and mountainous terrain, with Stanley the only vehicle of 40 competitors not to hit an obstacle or miss a gate in the qualification event [D3-S08, p. 1, p. 6].
- The Smac Hybrid-A* planner is the car-like/Ackermann-oriented member of Nav2's Smac planner family, selecting between `"DUBIN"` and `"REEDS_SHEPP"` motion models and exposing a configurable `minimum_turning_radius` (default 0.4 m) (see Findings §3) [D3-S14].
- Nav2's MPPI controller documentation states it "works currently with Differential, Omnidirectional, and Ackermann robots," selected via a `motion_model` plugin parameter set to `"mppi::DiffDriveMotionModel"`, `"mppi::OmniMotionModel"`, or `"mppi::AckermannMotionModel"`; only the Ackermann model exposes the `min_turning_r` constraint parameter (see Findings §3) [D3-S15, lines 12, 36, 250-256; D3-S16].
- Snider's CMU survey, comparing pure pursuit, the Stanley ("front wheel position based") controller, and a kinematic (Lyapunov-based) controller on the same simulated car-like vehicle over a lane-change and a figure-eight course, concludes that "the Stanley method is the simplest method presented and performs surprisingly well... In general, this method outperforms Pure Pursuit in most scenarios," but is "not as robust to large errors and non-smooth paths," and — unlike Pure Pursuit, which cuts corners — "a well tuned Stanley tracker will not 'cut corners' but rather overshoot turns," an effect attributed to Stanley having no lookahead [D3-S04, p. 66]. Separately, Paden et al.'s survey derives the same front-wheel-feedback (Stanley) control law and notes "a drawback to this controller is that it is not stable in reverse making it unsuitable for parking," whereas the rear-wheel-based geometric control law it also derives has stability "unaffected by the sign of" the reference velocity, making it usable in reverse [D3-S31, p. 19 (§V-A2-A3)].
- The same survey's overall conclusion is that none of the compared path-tracking methods "work well in all applications" and that they have "complementary characteristics," motivating combining methods for more general use [D3-S04, Abstract].

### 8. Off-the-shelf small/mid research platforms (RC-car-based and purpose-built rovers)

- F1TENTH is a 1/10-scale, open-source autonomous-racing evaluation platform (hardware + simulator + benchmarks + competitions) built around a base chassis consisting of a high-torque brushless DC motor, an open-source VESC electronic speed controller, **a servo motor controlling the Ackermann steering**, and a LiPo battery pack, plus a separate sensor/compute half [D3-S20, p. 81 (§4)]. It originates from the University of Pennsylvania and was published at the NeurIPS 2019 Competition and Demonstration Track [D3-S20, title page].
- MuSHR (Multi-agent System for non-Holonomic Racing), from the University of Washington's Personal Robotics Lab, is a comparable 1/10-scale platform explicitly inspired by and cost-reduced from the MIT RACECAR project: the basic MuSHR platform (no sensors) can be built for about **$600** versus roughly $1,000 for an equivalent MIT RACECAR build, and a sensor-equipped version (2D laser scanner, RGBD camera, IMU) costs about **$900** versus about $2,800 for MIT RACECAR [D3-S21, p. 3 (§V)].
- MuSHR's drivetrain uses a named brushless DC drive motor (Jrelecs F540 3930KV) and a named hobby servo for steering (ZOSKAY 1X DS3218), with a VESC-based speed controller (Turnigy SK8-ESC) converting Ackermann steering commands into servo positions and motor speeds [D3-S21, p. 2 (§II)].
- The AgileX Hunter 2.0 is a purpose-built, mid-size, front-wheel-Ackermann-steered ground vehicle with rocker suspension: 980×745×380 mm, 65-72 kg platform weight, 650 mm wheelbase, 605 mm front/rear track, **maximum steering angle 33°**, maximum payload 150 kg, maximum speed 1.5 m/s, maximum travel distance 40 km, obstacle clearance 50 mm, climbing ability ≤10° (with load), and a standard CAN-bus / RS-232 interface with an open-source ROS/ROS 2 SDK [D3-S24, pp. 5-6, p. 17]. The manufacturer states it delivers higher payload capacity, higher top speed and reduced tire/structure wear compared with "traditional four-wheel differential drive chassis" of similar scale [D3-S24, p. 16].
- The Chronos/CRS platform (ETH Zürich, published at ICRA 2023) is a smaller 1/28-scale car-like robot with open-source electronics, explicitly positioned as bridging the gap between small/cheap unicycle robots (not agile, cannot reach high speed) and larger/more expensive car-like robots [D3-S32, p. 1 (Abstract)].
- The Quanser QCar is an open-architecture, all-wheel-drive scaled research car (the source describes it only as a "scaled model car," without stating a specific scale fraction) used as an experimental Ackermann-steering platform in published vehicle-identification/path-tracking research: a single DC motor with encoder drives all wheels through a single-ratio gearbox, while a dedicated front-steering **servo motor guarantees a maximum steering angle of about ±30°**; its onboard compute is an NVIDIA Jetson TX2 [D3-S38, p. 3 (§II)]. Quanser's current product catalog instead offers the **QCar 2** as the newer product in this line, which the manufacturer describes as "an open-architecture, 1/10th scale vehicle designed for academic self-driving initiatives," built around an NVIDIA Jetson Orin AGX onboard computer, 2.7 kg mass, and two user-assignable PWM output channels explicitly documented as "Motor throttle control" and "Steering control," plus ROS 2, Python and MATLAB/Simulink support [D3-S39].
- The AgileX LIMO is a small, four-mode research platform (4.2 kg, 322×215×247 mm) that implements **four different steering modes — four-wheel differential, Ackermann, tracked, and Mecanum/omnidirectional — on one chassis by mechanically re-latching the same four independently-mounted wheel-hub-motor modules**, rather than by adding a dedicated Ackermann steering servo as F1TENTH/MuSHR/Hunter do: switching to Ackermann mode means pulling up a mechanical latch on each side and rotating it 30° so a marked line on the latch points toward the front of the vehicle, which re-orients the front wheel modules into a car-like steering geometry; switching to differential mode instead inserts the latch in a different rotational position, while tracked and Mecanum modes require physically swapping the wheel/track modules [D3-S40; D3-S41]. LIMO's advertised per-mode numeric specifications (e.g. an Ackermann-mode minimum turning radius and payload figure) appear in third-party product listings but could not be confirmed in the distributor documentation actually read for this topic, so they are not reported here (see *Open questions*).

### 9. Off-the-shelf golf-cart/full-size platforms and drive-by-wire kits

- OpenPodcar is an open-source hardware+software autonomous-vehicle research platform built on an off-the-shelf, hard-canopy, **Ackermann-steered** electric mobility scooter donor vehicle (Pihsiang TE-889XLSN) whose standard configuration steers via a human-operated loop handlebar [D3-S22, p. 3 (§"Donor vehicle")]; steering is automated with a 12 V DC linear actuator (Gimson GLA750-P: 750 N full-load force, 8 mm/s full-load speed, 250 mm stroke) mounted to the front wheel axle via a chassis anchor and bearings, not to the handlebar mechanism itself [D3-S22, p. 4 (§"Mechanical Modification for Steering")]. Total build cost from new components was about **USD 7,000 in 2022**, and the vehicle can carry a human passenger or equivalent load at up to 15 km/h [D3-S22, Abstract].
- The original (ROS 1) OpenPodcar's autonomous software uses `move_base` with a Timed-Elastic-Band (TEB) planner, which implements Dubins-path and Ackermann-steering geometry; minimum-turning-radius parameters are computed from the donor vehicle's technical specifications [D3-S22, p. 10 (§"Path planning")].
- OpenPodcar2 (University of Lincoln, UK) rebuilds the same donor-vehicle concept with a robust open-source electronics board (R4) and a **ROS 2 / Nav2** software stack; build cost was about **USD 7,000 from new components, or USD 2,000 with a used donor vehicle** [D3-S23, Abstract, p. 2]. Its software stack performs localization/mapping with RTAB-Map and uses "the SMAC planner from the nav2 ROS2 package" for path planning [D3-S23, p. 6]. The authors note that, at the time of their design, Nav2's MPPI controller server was in early release and supported Ackermann models on a similar foundation to its ROS 1 predecessor (TEB), and that swapping it in later could give more accurate control [D3-S23, p. 11].
- AutonomouStuff's PACMod v3 is a proprietary by-wire control module with an official open-source ROS driver (MIT-licensed, maintained by the AutonomouStuff Software Team); the driver subscribes to command topics for **accelerator, brake, steering, gear/shift and turn-signal** control on every supported vehicle, and — on vehicles that support them — also **horn, headlight, parking-brake and wiper** control [D3-S25a; D3-S25b, "Subscribed Topics"/"Conditionally Subscribed Topics"]. It publishes feedback topics for throttle/brake percentage, steering angle, gear state, individual wheel speeds, overall vehicle speed, and turn-signal/horn/headlight status, plus an `as_tx/enabled` topic that reports whether the PACMod's control of the vehicle is currently disabled or has been manually overridden [D3-S25b, "Published Topics"]. Supported donor vehicles listed in the package documentation include the **Polaris GEM series (MY2016+)**, Polaris Ranger, and a **Lexus RX-450h**, among others [D3-S25a; D3-S25b, "Supported Hardware"].
- OpenPodcar's own Mounting Board is separately CERN-OHL-W licensed specifically so it can be reused "in control of other similar Ackermann vehicles," not only the one donor vehicle it was designed for [D3-S23, p. 11 (§"Discussion")].

### 10. IGVC / outdoor-autonomy competition rules, design reports and retrospectives

- The 2026 IGVC AutoNav Challenge rules specify: an approximately 500 ft long course in a 120 ft × 100 ft area; **track width varying from 10 to 20 ft**, with a **minimum turning radius not less than 5 ft**; a minimum 5 ft clearance between the course boundary line and any obstacle; obstacles consisting of colored construction drums/barrels plus natural (trees/shrubs) and manmade (light posts/signs) obstacles randomly placed; simulated potholes as 2 ft diameter solid white circles that must be avoided; ramps up to 15% grade; a **minimum average speed of 1 mph** (checked over the first 44 ft / 30 s and over the whole run) and a **maximum speed of 5 mph**, hardware-governed [D3-S26, §II.1-II.2].
- This specific track-width/turning-radius requirement has not changed in at least the six years leading up to 2026: the 2020 rules document contains the identical sentence, "Track width will vary from ten to twenty feet wide with a turning radius not less than five feet" [D3-S42].
- A team that explicitly chose single-axle Ackermann steering for its IGVC entry (over an ATV-based chassis) recorded: "The main disadvantage presented by Ackerman steering is large turning radius; however, this steering system was chosen in order to more accurately simulate the pre-existing platforms with which embedded systems will be mated" — i.e. the team accepted the turning-radius penalty for a reason unrelated to course performance (matching an existing non-robotic platform) [D3-S27, p. 4 (§"Chassis")].
- The same team's original (2009) direct-drive steering mechanism (34:1 ratio, motor mounted where the ATV's handlebars had been) lacked enough torque to turn the wheels on some terrain, stalling and burning out two steering motors; the fix was a chain-and-sprocket drive (14-tooth motor sprocket to 60-tooth shaft sprocket) giving more torque at the wheel for the same motor current, at the cost of slower steering [D3-S27, p. 4 (§"Steering Motor")].
- A different IGVC team built a four-wheel-drive, **double-Ackermann-steered** ("dual Ackermann"/4-wheel-steer) robot and reported this "allows for strafing maneuvers impossible to achieve with conventional skid steer systems, while also being able to make tighter turns than a single Ackermann setup would be capable of" — their front and rear wheels are each independently steered by a linear actuator connected through a tie-rod link with opposing threads for toe adjustment [D3-S28, p. 2 (§1), p. 5 (§3.2)].
- A third IGVC team used an approximate (not geometrically exact) Ackermann steering linkage driven by a single high-torque motor and potentiometer feedback, noting explicitly that "most commercial cars operate perfectly fine with approximate Ackermann steering" and that their design, while efficient, suffered structural/material-strength problems from bending stress at the motor-to-linkage attachment point [D3-S29, p. 6 (§4.3)].

### 11. Field, agricultural and planetary-rover literature

- A 2009 IEEE conference paper on agricultural-vehicle platooning (leader-follower tractors) used RTK GPS to localize the leading tractor to centimeter-level accuracy, transmitted its position wirelessly to the following vehicle, fitted a desired path to the received points by curve fitting, and computed desired speed and **desired steering angle** for the following tractor from that path via a course-tracking controller plus a separate speed controller [D3-S30, p. 161 (Abstract)], with a yaw-angle controller structurally analogous to (replacing) the position controller used elsewhere in the same control architecture [D3-S30, p. 164].
- The AgileX Hunter 2.0 manual frames its front-wheel-Ackermann-plus-rocker-suspension configuration as specifically suited to low-speed autonomous driving with reduced tire wear versus a differential-drive chassis of similar scale, extending to agricultural-adjacent field/transport use cases through its stated modular sensor integration (stereo cameras, LiDAR, GPS, IMU, robotic arms) [D3-S24, p. 5, p. 16].
- The Deshpande (CMU) agricultural-robot thesis frames Ackermann steering as "the canonical approach to driving wheeled mobile robots with steerable front wheels" [D3-S19, p. 21], and reports single-axle Ackermann's minimum turning radius (1.94 m on their robot) against Dual Ackermann's tighter 1.29 m [D3-S19, p. 24] (see Findings §1). It motivates adding Crab and Point Turn modes, on top of Dual Ackermann, specifically for cases where "we do care about the orientation of the robot at a particular location" and curvature control alone is not sufficient [D3-S19, p. 35].
- The JPL rover-mobility taxonomy explicitly classifies planetary rovers by the same three-way steering-geometry scheme used for terrestrial vehicles (skid / Ackermann / all-wheel steering), applying it to the Rocky 7 Mars-rover prototype's two-steerable-front-wheel configuration, and states Ackermann steering is being explored specifically as a way to achieve both vehicle positioning *and* orienting for science-instrument placement without needing full omnidirectional driving capability, which is "undesirable, limited, or unavailable on some rovers" [D3-S17, p. 2].
- A CMU Master's thesis built a dedicated passive-steering planetary-rover testbed (Zoë2) specifically to quantify this tradeoff versus skid steering, reporting (see Findings §5 for full numbers) up to 30% lower turning power draw and far more precise blind (dead-reckoning) navigation for passive/Ackermann-like steering, at turn radii down to 1.5 m [D3-S18, pp. 61-67].

### 12. Terminology standards, test procedures and mechanical failure modes

- Neither of the two formal standards identified for this subtopic — ISO 8855 (vehicle-dynamics vocabulary) and SAE J695 (turning-ability/off-tracking test procedure) — could be read; both are paywalled with no open copy found (see Foundational references and Open questions), so their formal definitions of terms such as curb-to-curb vs. wall-to-wall turning radius are not reported here.
- A distinct, geometry-level cause of imprecise Ackermann steering (separate from wear) is **bump steer**: an unequal-length double-A-arm front suspension's upper/lower control arms and the steering tie rod generally do not share a common instantaneous centre of motion, so the toe angle changes as the suspension travels through jounce/rebound even with no driver steering input, degrading handling stability [D3-S35, p. 6]. One documented mitigation method, the "three-center theorem," geometrically places the steering linkage's disconnection point (where the tie rod meets the steering rack) so that it coincides with the suspension's own instantaneous-centre geometry; applied to a Formula SAE car's suspension/steering hardpoint design and verified in an ADAMS Car dynamic simulation with ±30 mm wheel jump, this kept toe-angle variation to 0.0088° under parallel wheel jump and 0.00872° under opposite wheel jump — small enough to meet the design's own precision requirement without further optimization [D3-S35, pp. 6-7].
- The mechanical failure modes actually documented for this topic come from IGVC team design reports rather than a standards body or durability study: a bent/yielded aluminum drive bar at an Ackermann linkage's motor attachment point under repeated bending stress [D3-S29, p. 6 (§4.3)], and burned-out steering motors from an under-geared (34:1) direct-drive mechanism lacking torque on some terrain [D3-S27, p. 4 (§"Steering Motor")] — see Findings §10 and *Common mistakes* for both. No source quantified how these failure modes degrade steering precision over time (e.g. a measured toe-angle drift per unit of tie-rod wear), which is noted under *Open questions*.

## Recommended practice

1. Treat a car-like vehicle's motion as the kinematic bicycle model for low-speed, low-lateral-acceleration planning and control, and switch to a dynamic (slip-angle) model only when the application's speed/acceleration regime requires it — the kinematic model is the default in both the Nav2-adjacent survey literature and in dedicated car-like-robot platform papers [D3-S31, p. 5; D3-S32, p. 3].
2. When computing or configuring a minimum turning radius for planning (Nav2 Smac Hybrid-A*) or control (Nav2 MPPI `AckermannMotionModel`), keep the configured radius consistent with the same physical constant in both places, since they enforce it in different ways (the planner by motion-primitive expansion, the controller by clipping sampled angular velocity) [D3-S14; D3-S16].
3. Verify that any path handed to a pure path-tracking controller (e.g. Regulated Pure Pursuit) for an Ackermann vehicle already respects that vehicle's minimum turning radius before tracking begins, since RPP itself performs no path-feasibility check for non-holonomic vehicles [D3-S02, p. 9].
4. Select `use_rotate_to_heading` based on in-place-rotation capability, not drive-type labels: enable it only if the vehicle can actually rotate in place, and do not combine it with `allow_reversing` [D3-S13, lines 180-187].
5. For Ackermann odometry calibration, prefer a self-calibrating, single-run method where available (lower effort, no initial-parameter-vector requirement) over a least-squares method requiring multiple well-conditioned runs, unless the extra accuracy of a trigonometric-approximation-free method is specifically needed [D3-S06, pp. 297-298].
6. Choose a path-tracking controller by scenario rather than by reputation alone: the Stanley method tends to outperform pure pursuit in most scenarios tested but is less robust to large errors/non-smooth paths [D3-S04, p. 66] and unusable in reverse [D3-S31, p. 19], while pure pursuit is simpler to tune and more robust to such conditions — surveys recommend considering a combination of methods rather than one universal choice [D3-S04, Abstract].
7. When a single fixed minimum turning radius is operationally binding (e.g. row-crop headlands, tight competition lanes), consider a dual-steered-axle ("Dual Ackermann") or mode-switching configuration rather than accepting single-axle Ackermann's larger radius, if the added actuator and control complexity is affordable for the application [D3-S19, pp. 21-24, p. 35].

## Common mistakes

- Treating Regulated Pure Pursuit as a full motion planner for Ackermann vehicles: RPP performs no path-feasibility check of its own, so an input path that violates the vehicle's minimum turning radius will simply be tracked poorly or infeasibly rather than rejected — the path must already be drivable before RPP sees it [D3-S02, p. 9].
- Mounting an Ackermann steering linkage's drive bar directly on a high-torque motor shaft without reinforcement: one IGVC team reported recurring bending/yielding failures at exactly this attachment point and had to redesign with thicker material and redundancy [D3-S29, p. 6 (§4.3)]. Separately, another IGVC team under-sized a direct-drive steering mechanism's gear ratio (34:1) and burned out two steering motors on tough terrain before fixing it with a higher mechanical-advantage chain drive [D3-S27, p. 4].
- Using a crab (sideways-translation) maneuver on a rocker-bogie-suspended rover: this loads the suspension unevenly, reducing stability, and the JPL taxonomy states it should be reserved for all-wheel-steering vehicles and avoided on long traverses even when the hardware nominally supports it [D3-S17, p. 2].
- Enabling `use_rotate_to_heading` based on an assumption that it is a "differential-drive-only" setting: Nav2's own documentation frames it instead around whether the specific robot can rotate in place at all, and warns it cannot be combined with `allow_reversing` [D3-S13, lines 180-187].
- Choosing single-axle Ackermann steering for a hardware-matching or cost reason while overlooking its turning-radius penalty in a space-constrained course or field: one IGVC team explicitly accepted "large turning radius" as a known disadvantage for an unrelated reason (matching an existing non-robotic platform), which is a reasonable tradeoff only when the course/field geometry has already been checked against it [D3-S27, p. 4].

## Disagreements between sources

- No source directly contradicts another on the core power/precision finding (Ackermann/explicit/passive steering beats skid steering on both power and path-tracking precision at any finite turn radius) — it is corroborated independently by a CMU wheeled test rig [D3-S05], a CMU planetary-rover testbed [D3-S18], and a JPL rover-mobility taxonomy's qualitative framing [D3-S17]. The sources do differ in the *magnitude* reported (skid steering needs "approximately double" the power of explicit steering at a full point turn [D3-S05], versus "up to 30% more" power at a 23° steering angle or on a 1.5 m-radius arc [D3-S18]), but this is not a contradiction: the two studies measure different operating points (an idealized point turn vs. a bounded steering-angle/arc-radius sweep that never reaches zero radius), and power disadvantage for skid steering is expected to grow as the turn tightens toward a point turn in both studies' own data.
- There is a genuine tension, not a factual disagreement, between the precision/efficiency advantage of Ackermann-type steering (Findings §5) and its maneuverability cost (Findings §1, §3, §10): single-axle Ackermann cannot turn in place and has the largest minimum turning radius of the geometric modes measured on one agricultural robot (1.94 m, vs. 1.29 m for Dual Ackermann, 0 m for Point Turn) [D3-S19], and IGVC teams and an agricultural-robotics thesis independently treat this as the steering choice's main practical drawback in space-constrained courses or fields [D3-S27; D3-S19] — motivating dual-axle, mode-switching, or all-wheel-steering designs that trade mechanical complexity for recovering tight-turn capability.

## Open questions

- *Resolved in the 2026-10-06 gap check (previously open):* the precise historical origin of Ackermann steering is now sourced to a dated, named, peer-reviewed secondary account (Veneri & Massaro 2020, itself citing King-Hele 2002 and three vehicle-dynamics textbooks) rather than to an excluded encyclopedic/trade-press page — see Findings §1. The primary 1816/1818 patent/invention documents themselves, however, remain unlocated, so the account's own secondary sources could not be independently checked.
- *Resolved in the 2026-10-06 gap check (previously open):* the numeric lateral-acceleration threshold for kinematic-bicycle-model validity is now sourced to Polack et al. (IEEE IV 2017, not independently downloaded but described via two sources that did read it) as **a_y < 0.5µg**, which is distinct from a separately-derived, numerically similar 0.5g limit for a different (2-DoF dynamic) model type — see Findings §2 and *Key numbers*. Kong et al. 2015's own finding (kinematic vs. dynamic single-track models have similar open-loop error over short horizons) is now also cited via the same secondary route, correcting the previous pass's unconfirmed guess that Kong et al. was the origin of the 0.5µg figure.
- No source meeting the evidence-acceptance checklist (author/publisher, venue, traceability) was found giving quantitative tie-rod-end wear, backlash, or bump-steer tolerances for Ackermann linkages generally; only automotive trade-press pages exist, which the standards for this research exclude. One quantitative bump-steer *mitigation* result was found and added (Findings §12: a "three-center theorem" hardpoint design kept toe-angle variation to under 0.01° under ±30 mm wheel jump on one FSAE car), but no source quantifies steering precision loss specifically from in-service wear over time.
- Neither ISO 8855 (vehicle-dynamics vocabulary) nor SAE J695 (turning-ability/off-tracking test procedure) could be read — both are paywalled with no open copy found, and a 2026-10-06 search for a secondary source explaining their formal curb-to-curb vs. wall-to-wall turning-radius definitions found only non-qualifying pages (encyclopedic, calculator and forum sites) — so this topic still cannot report those definitions from a source meeting the evidence-acceptance checklist (Subtopic 12a).
- No single IGVC design report stating an explicit side-by-side cost/turning-radius/maintenance rationale for choosing Ackermann *over* skid-steering specifically (as opposed to reports from teams that simply built an Ackermann vehicle) was found in the time available; the reports found (D3-S27, D3-S28, D3-S29) describe teams that already chose Ackermann and report its consequences, not a documented comparative decision process.
- No source was found describing a direct, controlled comparison of Ackermann vs. differential/skid-steer specifically on **outdoor grass, mud, or loose dirt/gravel traction and durability** (as opposed to power/precision on a hard-surface or planetary-regolith testbed); the IGVC and agricultural sources describe outdoor use but do not isolate traction/durability as a measured variable against an alternative steering geometry on the same chassis. A 2026-10-06 gap-check search (direct terms and the neighbouring tracked-vs-wheeled / agricultural-traction literature) confirmed this remains the case: the closest results either compare tracked vs. wheeled mobility in general (not Ackermann vs. skid-steer specifically) or study a skid-steer-only agricultural robot without an Ackermann comparison arm.
- AgileX LIMO's advertised Ackermann-mode minimum turning radius and payload figures, reported identically across several third-party product listings, could not be confirmed in the distributor documentation actually read for this topic (which omits per-mode numeric specifications) and are therefore not reported in Findings §8.
