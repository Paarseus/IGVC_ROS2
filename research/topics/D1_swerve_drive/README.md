## Summary

- In the standard wheeled-mobile-robot classification, a 4-module independently-steered-and-driven swerve chassis is **not** the fully omnidirectional type: it is Campion's type (δm=1, δs=2) — the same mobility degree (δm=1) as a car/bicycle, but with two independently-steerable wheel coordinates (δs=2) that redundant extra modules must be kinematically coordinated to match, rather than the fully omnidirectional type (δm=3, δs=0) reserved for robots with no standard rolling-without-slip wheels at all. [D1-S01, p. 742]
- The inverse-kinematics algorithm almost every FRC/FTC team and community library (WPILib, YAGSL) runs today traces to a non-academic 2011 whitepaper by a Chief Delphi community member known as "Ether," later formalized as WPILib's `SwerveDriveKinematics`. [D1-S05; D1-S10, section "The swerve module state class"]
- Swerve's defining failure mode is a kinematic singularity: wheel steering angles must all point at a shared instantaneous center of rotation (ICR), and when the ICR coincides with or nears a module, the required steering rate goes to infinity; a validated potential-field avoidance method using tunable leg lengths exists for a research platform (DLR's Rollin' Justin) but is not implemented in any FRC/FTC-market module or its software. [D1-S07, Abstract and §I]
- No source found compares *swerve's* (independent per-wheel steering) dead-reckoning precision quantitatively against skid-steer; the nearest same-vehicle, instrumented comparison found is a 1999 CMU thesis on NASA's "Nomad" rover testbed, but it compares skid-steering against *double-axle* steering (both sides' wheel pairs steered together via linkage, not independent per-wheel), finding the two "not significantly better" than each other in GPS-measured turning-radius precision while skid steering drew markedly more power/torque at all but the largest radius tested; outdoor swerve-specific deployment evidence remains limited to two IGVC AutoNav teams' self-reported design documents, not independent studies. [D1-S42, §4.3.2, §5.2; search log, this topic]
- Off-the-shelf swerve modules (REV, SDS, WCP, ThriftyBot) are built and marketed for the flat carpet of the FRC/FTC playing field; **no vendor publishes an ingress-protection (IP) rating for any of them**, and only REV's EasySwerve documents any debris shielding at all (a glass-filled-nylon enclosure described as protecting against "carpet debris," not water or dust). [D1-S20, section "Description"]
- The one confirmed outdoor swerve-drive IGVC entry with public build detail, University of Oklahoma's "Twistopher" (2025), used custom (not off-the-shelf FRC) modules with 8-inch pneumatic wheels and an acrylic/weatherstripped chassis specifically to handle grass and weather, and its report claims — without presenting a quantitative comparison — that swerve has "more traction" than mecanum outdoors and is "less susceptible to wheel slip and error in odometry than mecanum." [D1-S30, §3.1, §4.1]
- ROS 2's official `ros2_controllers` package has no swerve/4+-module controller: `steering_controllers_library` implements only Bicycle, Tricycle, and Ackermann kinematics (at most two steered wheels, explicitly non-holonomic), while Nav2's MPPI controller has a generic `OmniMotionModel` (`isHolonomic() == true`) that is agnostic to the physical wheel realization (equally usable by swerve, mecanum, or omni-wheel bases) and contains no per-module or singularity-aware modeling. [D1-S17, lines 14, 40–42; D1-S18, section "Motion Models"]

## Findings

### 1. Classification and kinematic foundations

- In Campion's classification, a wheeled mobile robot's motion is characterized by two integers: degree of mobility δm (dimension of the space of instantaneously achievable body velocities) and degree of steerability δs (number of independently-oriented steering coordinates needed). Only five (δm, δs) combinations are kinematically possible: (3,0), (2,0), (2,1), (1,1), and (1,2). [D1-S01, p. 742]
- Type (3,0) — δm=3, δs=0, no fixed and no "centered orientable" (standard, no-sideslip) wheels at all — is the only type the paper calls "omnimobile" (fully omnidirectional): at every instant it can move in any direction without first reorienting any wheel. The paper's examples of this type (URANUS, UCL) use wheels that do not impose the standard rolling-without-slip constraint, i.e. Swedish/omni-style wheels, not standard swerve wheels. [D1-S01, p. 742]
- Type (1,2) — δm=1, δs=2 — has no fixed wheels and at least two "centered orientable" (standard, steerable, no-sideslip) wheels; if more than two such wheels are fitted, their orientations "must be coordinated" so that the rank of the steering constraint matrix stays at δs=2. A 4-module swerve chassis (four independently steerable, no-sideslip standard wheels, no fixed wheels) fits this type: it has the **same degree of mobility (δm=1) as a car-like/Ackermann robot**, not the δm=3 of a true omnidirectional platform — its velocity must belong to a one-dimensional distribution parameterized by the orientation angles of (any) two of its wheels. The paper's named example of this type is the robot "KLUDGE." [D1-S01, p. 742]
- This formal result matches the practical description in the IGVC "Swervi" design report: "true nonholonomic swerve drive is an over constrained system, there are multiple solutions to a given position and orientation... there is no unique body position and orientation that exists given a set of wheel angles and velocities" — i.e. a swerve chassis has more independently-actuated steering angles than the degrees of freedom they need to encode, so a consistent command requires every wheel to be coordinated to a single shared ICR. [D1-S29, §2.2]
- Standard (swerve) wheels enforce a rolling-without-slip constraint along the wheel's rolling direction and zero velocity perpendicular to it (the "zero motion line"); the point where all wheels' zero-motion lines intersect is the instantaneous center of rotation (ICR), and when R (the wheel's turning radius about the ICR) is infinite the wheel moves in a straight line. This is the same ICR construction used for an Ackermann vehicle, which — because its wheels are not independently steered — always has a single, geometrically-fixed-by-linkage ICR, unlike swerve's electronically-coordinated one. [D1-S02, pp. 57–59]
- The Swervi report credits swerve's independent-steering mobility gain over "other drivetrains such as Ackermann steering, differential drive, and omni wheel drive." [D1-S29, §2.1] Mecanum/omni wheels differ from swerve wheels mechanically: they use passively-rotating angled rollers around the wheel's rim to permit deliberate sideways sliding at the contact patch, trading the no-sideslip constraint for an extra, uncontrolled-direction degree of freedom at each wheel — unlike swerve, where the wheel itself never slides sideways and omnidirectionality is achieved only by physically re-steering the wheel. This roller-mechanics description is general wheeled-mobile-robot domain knowledge (the same Swedish/omni-wheel construction named in Campion's classification, D1-S01, p. 742, Findings §1 above), not a statement drawn from the Swervi report itself, which does not describe mecanum/omni roller mechanics.
- "Crab" terminology for coordinated, same-angle wheel steering predates and is used independently of the FRC "swerve" term: an industrial AGV product (the Lift-A-Loft SPW16SPL scissor lift) documents a "crab drive mode" as one of three selectable drive modes (standard forward/reverse, 360° rotation-in-place, and side-to-side crab), where "the system re-orients the drive wheels to the appropriate position depending on the drive mode selected." [D1-S28, lines 36, 38]
- A second, coarser textbook classification splits wheeled mobile robots into only two categories — "omnidirectional" (no equality constraint on chassis velocity at all) and "nonholonomic" (subject to "a single Pfaffian velocity constraint") — and explicitly states the assumption that "robots roll on hard, flat, horizontal ground without skidding (i.e., tanks and skid-steered vehicles are excluded)" from the chapter's scope entirely. This two-category split is coarser than Campion's five-type (δm, δs) classification and does not by itself give a distinct category for a redundantly-actuated independently-steered (swerve) platform, which is why Campion's classification (above) is the more directly applicable foundational reference for swerve specifically. [D1-S03, §13.1, pp. 513–514]
- A kinematic + dynamic model of the swerve structure under the name "4WIS4WID" (four-wheel-independent-steering, four-wheel-independent-drive), developed outside the FRC/FTC context, reports a trajectory-tracking control scheme combining non-linear kinematic control with dynamic sliding-mode control, with stability of both the kinematic and dynamic control laws "proved by Lyapunov stability analysis," and validated only "through computer simulations" (no physical robot results are claimed in the abstract). [D1-S06, abstract]

### 2. Inverse and forward kinematics of a multi-module chassis

- The practitioner-standard inverse-kinematics derivation (attributed to the Chief Delphi community member "Ether," originally dated 2 Jan 2011 with revisions dated 26–28 Mar 2011) computes each module's required velocity vector by treating the module's position relative to a chosen reference point as a lever arm: for a module at position **r** from the center of rotation, with desired chassis linear velocity **v** and angular velocity ω, the module's velocity is **v_m = v + ω × r**; the module's commanded wheel speed and steering angle are then this vector's magnitude and direction. [D1-S05, "Derivation of the inverse kinematics..."; D1-S12, §2 "Derivation"]
- WPILib's `SwerveDriveKinematics` implements this in software: the class constructor takes each module's fixed (x,y) location relative to the robot center as a `Translation2d`, and its `toSwerveModuleStates(ChassisSpeeds)` method then converts a desired (vx, vy, ω) into the array of per-module wheel-speed/steering-angle states; the reverse direction, `toChassisSpeeds(SwerveModuleState...)`, recovers a single `ChassisSpeeds` from the module states. WPILib's own documentation describes this only at the class/method-usage level and does not state how the conversion is computed internally. [D1-S10, sections "Constructing the Kinematics Object," "Converting Chassis Speeds to Module States," "Converting Module States to Chassis Speeds"] The underlying computation — building a matrix from the module locations and using its (Moore–Penrose) pseudo-inverse, because the module-velocity system is overdetermined ("n * 2 values available and three variables") — is documented explicitly by the community `zinger_swerve_controller` project (`V_i = |A| * V` for the inverse direction, `V = |A|⁻¹ * V_i` via the pseudo-inverse for the forward/odometry direction) and, for the forward direction specifically, by the Twistopher team's report (see next bullet). [D1-S35, "Kinematics" section]
- A non-geometric-center instantaneous center of rotation is supported directly: WPILib's module-state conversion method takes an optional second argument specifying the center of rotation as a `Translation2d`; the provided vx/vy still apply to the whole rigid frame, but ω is measured about that chosen point — e.g. setting the center of rotation at a specific module and commanding zero vx/vy with nonzero ω makes the robot visibly pivot about that module. [D1-S10, section "Using custom centers of rotation"]
- Forward kinematics (module speeds + angles → chassis twist) for an over-determined system (more independently-sensed modules than the 3 degrees of freedom needed) is solved by a least-squares / pseudo-inverse approach on the module-position matrix: the Twistopher (IGVC 2025) team describes resolving each module's measured linear x/y velocity components into a state vector, then "multiplying this vector with the pseudo-inverse of the module positions matrix" to compute the robot's estimated translation and rotation. [D1-S30, §6.5]
- A dedicated academic treatment of this inverse problem — estimating the ICR itself from noisy per-wheel steering-angle measurements on an independently-steered platform — frames it as a least-squares estimate ("the point that minimizes its distance to each propulsion axis"), but notes this naive least-squares estimator "does not cope well with parallelism or near-parallelism": when most or all wheels are parallel (e.g. straight-line driving), the system of equations relating wheel axes to ICR position degenerates because there are more unknowns than equations, so the paper proposes instead projecting the current wheel-angle vector onto a precomputed "constraining surface" in actuator space. [D1-S15, section "II. ICR Estimation"]
- "Module-angle optimization" lets a module spin its wheel in reverse rather than rotate 180°, to minimize the angular distance the module must travel: WPILib's `SwerveModuleState.optimize()` compares the desired heading to the module's current angle and, if flipping the wheel's drive direction and negating the target heading by 180° reduces the required rotation, does so automatically. [D1-S10, section "Module angle optimization"]
- "Cosine compensation" (scaling a module's commanded speed by the cosine of its remaining heading error after optimization) is documented as reducing the translational "skew" a swerve drive experiences when changing direction, by not driving the wheel at full commanded speed while it is still partway through re-aiming. [D1-S10, section "Cosine compensation"; lines 137, 142–143]
- The translational-skew problem itself is a first-order-kinematics artifact: when a swerve chassis translates and rotates simultaneously at constant commanded chassis velocity, each individual module's required linear speed and heading are continuously changing even though the chassis state is constant, so naively holding "first order" module commands for a finite timestep causes the realized path to skew in the direction of rotation. A footnote in the same source adds that part of the observed skew is also caused by the response delay between when a module is commanded a new state and when it physically achieves it — a separate, control-loop-latency contribution the paper explicitly sets aside as out of scope. [D1-S12, §1 "Introduction" and footnote 1]
- One documented fix, used by WPILib and by the community library YAGSL, is timestep discretization: because `SwerveDriveKinematics` computes module states assuming the commanded velocity is held constant for the whole next control-loop period (typically 20 ms), but the true path curves slightly within that window, `ChassisSpeeds.discretize(speeds, dt)` "works out what constant-velocity command over `dt` would actually produce the desired end pose," correcting the skew/drift this causes, especially at higher rotation rates. If no discretization time is configured, YAGSL applies no correction at all. [D1-S13, section "Chassis Speed Discretization"]
- A separate, second-order kinematic approach (not described as related to or a replacement for the discretization fix above) derives each module's full acceleration vector rather than only its velocity vector: differentiating the per-module velocity equation gives module acceleration **a_m = a + α×r − ω²r**, where α is the chassis's angular acceleration and the −ω²r term is the centripetal acceleration each module must additionally track as the chassis rotates — intended to let a motion profile or feedforward command module acceleration directly rather than only re-deriving it from successive velocity commands. [D1-S12, §2 "Derivation," eq. 3 and the simplified acceleration equation following it]
- An alternative open-access academic derivation of inverse kinematics for a three-wheel swerve platform (outside the FRC/WPILib lineage) presents the chassis-to-module mapping as an explicit kinematics matrix for a three-wheel layout, stating that "Swerve Drive enables omnidirectional motion that requires optimal control and complex development"; the derived kinematics were validated only in simulation, which the paper reports let the modeled robot "move well... with translational, diagonal movement and rotational capabilities as required." [D1-S16, Abstract]

### 3. Singularities and kinematic degeneracy

- A kinematic singularity occurs when the instantaneous center of rotation crosses or comes close to one of the wheel (module) axes, requiring an infinite steering rate to maintain a consistent ICR-aligned configuration — this is described generally for independently-steered wheeled platforms, not specific to any one vendor's module. [D1-S07, §I "Introduction"]
- Documented avoidance strategies in the academic literature include: (a) constraining the accessible commanded-velocity space to exclude regions that would require passing through a singular configuration; (b) treating singular configurations as obstacles and building repulsive potential fields in velocity/configuration space, which has specifically been applied to ICR-based singularities; and (c) using "special wheels." [D1-S07, §I "Introduction"] The parenthetical gloss on (c) — that this means changing the wheel type, e.g. to omni/mecanum, which removes the no-sideslip constraint that creates the singularity in the first place — is this research's own inference from the general wheel-type distinction (Findings §1), not Dietrich et al.'s own wording.
- Dietrich et al.'s own contribution adds a fourth option specific to platforms with a variable "leg length" (distance from each wheel module to the body center): a potential-field-based controller that uses the tunable leg length as an additional control input to minimize deviations from the nominal trajectory while avoiding the singularity, validated in simulation and experimentally on DLR's four-independently-steered-wheel mobile platform for the humanoid Rollin' Justin. This approach is not applicable to a fixed-footprint FRC/FTC-style swerve module, whose modules cannot change their distance from the chassis center at runtime. [D1-S07, Abstract and §I "Introduction"]
- A fixed-footprint alternative exists for platforms without Dietrich et al.'s variable leg length: Sorour et al. propose "both a representational singularity free kinematic model, and a simple numeric treatment for the kinematic singularity," incorporating steer-rate damping so the treatment respects the steering joints' actuator acceleration limits, and move the singularity treatment into the kinematic-model layer itself (rather than a separate outer-loop planner) for better real-time performance; the method was tested on the Neobotix MPO-700 (a 4-independently-steered-wheel industrial AGV platform) and reported to outperform the robot's own embedded controller. The full text could not be retrieved (see D1-S39, not downloaded); this finding is drawn from the paper's abstract, read via its institutional-repository record page. [D1-S39, abstract]
- A second fixed-footprint-applicable approach, developed on AZIMUT-3 (a nonholonomic omnidirectional research platform with sidewards off-centred, compliant steerable wheels, not a rigid fixed-footprint FRC-style module), introduces the "H representation" — "a new parametrization of the motion state space free of any non-structural singularities" — and an ICR-estimation algorithm built on it; comparing this approach against three other operational-space ICR-estimation methods on criteria of "reliability, efficiency, accuracy and robustness," the authors report it "provides the best compromise for these performance indicators." A companion paper adapts a time-scaling principle originally developed for manipulator trajectories to the H representation, producing an ICR-based motion controller reported to handle "actuators coordination and their physical limits, as well as structural singularities" on the physical AZIMUT-3 platform. Neither paper's full text could be retrieved (see D1-S40, D1-S41, not downloaded; both findings are drawn from their abstracts, read via a secondary bibliographic API). [D1-S40, abstract; D1-S41, abstract]
- No source found gives a numeric figure (e.g. degrees of ICR-to-module proximity, or milliseconds/degrees of added path-tracking error) for how far short of true zero speed a real swerve module's steering-rate saturation begins to visibly degrade short-horizon path tracking; the closest the literature comes is Sorour et al.'s steer-rate-damping treatment, which is tuned against the steering actuator's acceleration limits rather than expressed as a quantified tracking-error budget. This remains listed under Open questions below.

### 4. Closed-loop module control

- The standard per-module control architecture, as documented by the community library YAGSL (built on WPILib) for "smart" motor controllers, runs two independent closed loops per module directly on the module's own motor controller hardware rather than on the main robot controller: the controller has "integrated PID loops which can control the output based on a connected sensor's input (typically an encoder)," one such loop closing the drive-wheel velocity and another closing the steering-angle position; REV's SPARK MAX is named elsewhere as the required azimuth-motor controller for the REV MAXSwerve module specifically, and CTRE's TalonFX is referenced in the same YAGSL module-configuration documentation. [D1-S14, "Swerve Modules" page, "Review" checklist and TalonFX current-limit reference; D1-S19, "Description" ("SPARK MAX Motor Controller")]
- Steering/drive feedforward in this architecture is specified per-module as static/velocity/acceleration gain terms (the "s"/"v"/"a" feedforward fields in YAGSL's `pidfproperties.json`), configured once up front — older runtime-tunable feedforward-replacement methods (e.g. `replaceSwerveModuleFeedforward`) existed in older YAGSL versions but "no longer exist as runtime methods on `SwerveDrive`" in the current version documented. [D1-S14, "Swerve Modules" page and "Swerve Drive Drift" page hint box on removed runtime methods]
- Vendor modules use two different steering-encoder arrangements: an **on-axis** absolute encoder mounted directly on the module's vertical steering axis (REV MAXSwerve's "Through Bore Encoder," 10-bit resolution; SDS MK4i/MK4n/MK4c's "on-axis steering encoder" explicitly advertised as "zero backlash"), versus encoders coupled through the azimuth gear reduction. Vendors emphasize the on-axis, zero-backlash property specifically because it means **the module can be disassembled and reassembled without resetting the encoder's angle offset** — any backlash or off-axis gearing would introduce angle error whenever the gear train is disturbed. [D1-S19, "Description"; D1-S21, D1-S22, D1-S23 — SDS MK4n/MK4c/MK4i product pages, "On-axis steering encoder" bullet]
- REV MAXSwerve's **steering/azimuth** encoder is the same 10-bit "Through Bore Encoder" named in the bullet above (angular resolution directly on the turning axis, not a separate drive-wheel encoder); ThriftyBot's module instead uses a proprietary "Thrifty Absolute Magnetic Encoder" for steering. No source gave a comparable resolution figure for the magnetic steering encoders used by SDS or WCP modules. [D1-S19, "Description"; D1-S25, "Description"]
- The Twistopher report's control/odometry section describes continuous feedback from "absolute encoders on the angle motors" and "encoders in the drive motors" used to compute setpoints and estimate motion, but gives no explicit CAN-bus loop-rate or latency figure. [D1-S30, §6.5]
- WPILib's own SysId (System Identification) characterization tool, used to measure the feedforward/PID gain terms referenced above, is documented as supporting only three mechanism types — "Simple Motor Setups," "Elevators," and "Arms" — with no swerve- or drivetrain-specific mode listed. [D1-S43, "System Identification (SysId) Tool" overview] Asked directly how to characterize a swerve drivetrain with SysId, a Chief Delphi community member posting as "calcmogul" answered: "SysId is primarily intended for identification of differential drive and appendage models. It's not designed for swerve drive. You can abuse the tool to get some of the information you want though," and described the practical workaround teams use — characterizing each module's two degrees of freedom separately under the tool's "general mechanism" project type, "locking the module rotation with a piece of wood or something for the linear test, and spinning the module in place for the rotation test" — explicitly noting "swerve module system ID is different from whole-drivetrain system ID." [D1-S36, posts dated 2023-01-20]
- One vendor module ships two different steering-sensor arrangements on the same product line rather than one: West Coast Products' Swerve X "Standard" configuration uses an off-axis "Sensor Shaft with Magnet" coupled to the steering shaft through a 64-tooth timing belt (the kit's bill of materials lists a "64T Encoder Belt"), while the same module's "Flipped" (gears-below) configuration instead uses a dedicated "Encoder Mount (On Axis)" part — i.e. the belt-driven arrangement is specific to one mechanical configuration of the module, not a fixed design choice of the vendor. [D1-S33, "Individual Components" bill of materials, parts WCP-0364-004/WCP-1421-005]
- Quantified single-module control-loop performance data (not available for any off-the-shelf FRC/FTC vendor module checked in this research) comes from a university VEXU team's differential-swerve module prototype: isolated bench testing of one bevel-gear-coupled differential-swerve pod reported "motor feedback loops respond accurately to angle corrections (3-5° error)" and a "pod alignment time averaged under 0.4 seconds for 90° turns," using a PID position loop on the module's steering angle. [D1-S37, §V "Testing and Observations," subsection "B. Single Pod Testing"] This is the only steering-angle-lag/tracking-error figure found in absolute units in this research; it is specific to one non-off-the-shelf prototype module, not any vendor's product, so the broader Open-questions gap for vendor modules (REV, SDS, WCP, ThriftyBot) remains.

### 5. Odometry, sensor fusion and heading-hold precision

- The two documented swerve odometry approaches are: (a) pure wheel-based dead reckoning using only encoders and a gyroscope (WPILib's `SwerveDriveOdometry`), which WPILib's own documentation explicitly warns "will drift over time, especially as your robot comes into contact with other robots... however, odometry is usually very accurate during [a short] period"; and (b) a Kalman-filter-based pose estimator that additionally fuses external measurements such as vision, documented in WPILib as `SwerveDrivePoseEstimator` and, in the research literature, as EKF/UKF fusion of wheel odometry with visual odometry and IMU data. [D1-S11, lines 4, 99; D1-S32, Abstract]
- A 2025 peer-reviewed (ECMR) study of a 4WIS4WID (four-wheel independent steer, four-wheel independent drive) mobile robot found that relying on wheel odometry alone caused the robot to be "unable to reach the intended endpoint" during a straight-line motion test, and to exhibit "unintended displacement" along the cross-axis during a lateral motion test, in both cases attributed to wheel slippage (and, for the lateral case, gravitational effects specific to that robot's wall-climbing application); fusing wheel odometry with an IMU and visual odometry via EKF or UKF "significantly improved pose estimation accuracy... especially in reducing orientation errors and compensating for slippage." No numeric RMSE or percentage-error figures were present in the paper's text (only in un-extracted plot data), so none are quoted here. [D1-S32, §V "Experimental Validation," §VI "Conclusion"]
- The dominant wheel-odometry error source most frequently identified by the FRC community documentation (not an independent error-budget study) is wheel-radius/gear-ratio calibration error: YAGSL's drift-cause checklist lists "the gear ratio/wheel diameter is not correct" as a specific, named cause, noting that "if your swerve drive consistently travels further than expected... this is a likely cause," alongside the physical risk of the absolute-encoder's magnet slipping relative to the module ("absolute encoder offsets would need constant readjustment until the magnet is secured"). [D1-S14, "Configuration causes" and "Physical components" sections]
- No peer-reviewed or vendor source found directly compares swerve/holonomic odometry precision and repeatability, on the same or comparable platforms, against skid-steer or Ackermann dead reckoning; several targeted searches for this comparison (see Search log maintained in `SCOPE.md`) returned no result. This is listed under Open questions.
- Heading is held in the swerve sources found either by gyro-only yaw tracking, fed directly into the odometry update step (WPILib's `SwerveDriveOdometry::Update()` takes the gyro's `Rotation2d` as an explicit input alongside module states, not computed from the wheels at all), or by fusing gyro yaw with wheel-derived heading-rate in a Kalman filter. No source found makes or supports a claim that swerve's wheel-geometry redundancy gives it an intrinsic heading-observability advantage over a 2-wheel differential/skid base; the sources that discuss heading precision attribute it to the IMU/gyro fusion, not to the wheel count or steering redundancy. [D1-S11, lines 99, 108–111]

### 6. Known failure modes and wheel-coupling effects

- "Wheel skew"/scrub in the automotive sense (a steering-axis offset from the wheel's ground-contact point coupling drive-torque reaction into steering torque) is not explicitly named or quantified in any swerve-specific source found in this research; the automotive reference that would formalize it (Gillespie's *Fundamentals of Vehicle Dynamics*) could not be obtained (see Foundational references, D1-S09, not downloaded). This gap is listed under Open questions.
- The term "skew" in the swerve-specific sources found instead refers exclusively to the *translational path* skew from first-order kinematics during simultaneous translate+rotate motion (see Findings §2), not to a wheel-contact-patch torque-coupling effect — this is a terminology mismatch worth flagging explicitly.
- Documented mechanical failure/drift causes, compiled from a community swerve-library's maintenance checklist, include: a magnetic absolute encoder's magnet slipping on the module (requiring re-adhesion with threadlocker/glue) or the encoder itself becoming loose; a drive or steering motor losing a firm mechanical connection to the module's shaft, which the checklist says causes the motor to "twitch" while running under a current limit; wheel misalignment; and modules behaving inconsistently under identical PID gains, attributed to needing fresh grease on the gears. [D1-S14, "Physical components" section]
- Documented electrical/wiring failure modes from the same checklist: worn, loose, or incorrect wiring to the encoder, motor controller, or gyroscope, each producing distinct symptoms — erratic/nonsensical encoder telemetry and possible uncontrolled motor spin for an encoder fault; slow telemetry updates, motors suddenly stopping or spinning out of control, or motors not responding at all for a motor-controller fault; and slow telemetry, unusable field-relative control, or uncontrollable autonomous drift for a gyroscope fault. [D1-S14, "Wiring" section]
- Documented software/configuration failure modes include: a scheduler-loop overrun elsewhere in the robot's code, an incorrectly-set maximum physical module velocity, open-loop control in which modules run at inconsistent speeds relative to each other, a chassis-speed discretization that does not account for system delay, and un-tuned vision-measurement standard deviations feeding a pose estimator. [D1-S14, "Software causes" section]
- One motor-controller vendor (CTRE) publishes per-device default CAN-bus utilization figures that can be used to estimate loading on a multi-module swerve robot, though no source found publishes a total-bus figure for a complete swerve robot directly: on CAN 2.0 with default status-frame rates, a Talon FX motor controller uses "4.1%" of total bus bandwidth and a CANcoder absolute encoder "1.7%," with the vendor recommending "total CAN bus utilization below 90% to prevent any unexpected behavior," and noting that the vendor's own API "automatically start[s] up a diagnostic server which adds a constant 0-5% total CAN bus utilization." [D1-S38, "CAN Bus Utilization" table and body text] Summing these published per-device defaults for a representative 8-motor swerve robot (8 Talon FX motor controllers, one per drive and steering motor, plus 8 CANcoder steering encoders and one Pigeon 2 IMU) gives approximately 8×4.1% + 8×1.7% + 3.1% ≈ 50%, plus up to 5% for the diagnostic server — roughly half of the vendor's recommended ceiling at default settings, before accounting for any other CAN devices on the same bus; this arithmetic is this research's own calculation from the cited per-device figures, not a number stated directly by the vendor for a swerve configuration. Brownout risk specifically from simultaneous multi-module current draw (an electrical-supply question, distinct from CAN-bus data loading) is not addressed by this source and remains listed under Open questions.
- A 2-motor coupled-differential ("differential swerve") module, as sold historically by ARMABOT, uses "two motors [that] combine to control module steering and wheel movement" rather than one dedicated motor per function. [D1-S26, product description] The vendor's own installation instructions (not the product page) show the mechanism is **bevel-gear**-based, not planetary: a "top bevel gear assembly" and a "bottom bevel gear group" mesh at a central shaft, the same general bevel-gear differential-swerve architecture independently described by the COSMOS paper's own pod (see the corroborating bullet below; D1-S37, §III). [D1-S26, installation-instructions PDF, steps 1–2] The vendor's separately-linked outline drawing gives a 5:1 drive ratio and a 5:1 steering ratio, and a 6.29 in × 8.79 in top-plate footprint accepting up to a 4 in wheel with a 1/2 in hex bore — figures not present on the product page's own text and not previously found in this research. [D1-S26, outline-drawing PDF] The vendor's speed chart additionally gives wheel free-speed figures (10.5–24.8 ft/s, depending on motor, wheel diameter, and pulley/belt combination) for CIM- and miniCIM-class motors, but, like the product page and the other two linked PDFs, states no module weight and no sealing/ingress-protection specification. [D1-S26, speed-chart PDF]
- The practical trade documented for ARMABOT's differential-swerve product versus a standard 2-independent-motor module is wiring, not kinematics: because both motors are fixed (non-rotating relative to the chassis) rather than one motor needing to rotate with the module's azimuth, no continuous-rotation cable management (slip ring or cable-wrap limit) is needed for that motor — though no source read for this product states this explicitly; the product page and the three linked PDFs only confirm the two-motor/combined-function description, the bevel-gear mechanism, the 5:1/5:1 ratios, and that encoders, controllers, and wheels are sold separately. [D1-S26, product description and linked PDFs]
- An independent academic/project source corroborates the general coupled-differential description given informally above (from ARMABOT's product page) with its own build: a differential-swerve pod built around two independently-driven bevel gears meeting at a central shaft, where "the differential swerve approach separates motor outputs into rotational and translational components" so that, unlike a standard coaxial module (the source's own example of a coaxial module is SDS's MK4i), "all motors remain actively engaged" whether the module is purely driving, purely steering, or both — the source frames this as the source of the architecture's claimed efficiency advantage over a coaxial module. [D1-S37, §II "Related Work," §III "Design of the Drive System"] The same source's own comparison table rates its differential-swerve design "High" for both pushing power and energy efficiency (versus a comparable mecanum/omni drive's "Low" on both), but at a "High" mechanical- and control-complexity cost (versus "Medium" for mecanum and "Low" for a non-holonomic tank drive) — i.e. the source itself reports the standard efficiency-versus-complexity trade-off for this mechanism, not an unqualified advantage. [D1-S37, Table I] The same source also names a real, non-competition precedent for the alternative (coaxial, one-motor-per-axis) swerve architecture outside FRC/FTC: NASA's "Modular Robotic Vehicle (MRV)," cited only as offering "excellent maneuverability and mechanical simplicity," with no further detail given in this source. [D1-S37, §II "Related Work"]

### 7. Testing, validation and comparative precision literature

- Documented validation tests for swerve-specific odometry/pose estimators in the sources found are: (a) a straight-line (single-axis) translation test and a lateral translation test, each compared against ground truth/a safety rig, used to validate an EKF/UKF pose estimator against raw wheel odometry on a 4WIS4WID robot [D1-S32, §V]; and (b) field-relative autonomous-path tests (e.g. PathPlanner-driven autonomous routines) used informally by FRC teams to diagnose drift causes such as incorrect gear ratio or module location, per a community tuning checklist — not a controlled, published experiment. [D1-S14, "PathPlanner" section]
- No peer-reviewed study, competition design report, or vendor document found reports a quantitative, controlled precision/repeatability number (e.g. position error in cm over a measured distance, or heading error in degrees over a measured duration) for a swerve-drive platform specifically, on any surface. The closest datum found is qualitative: the 2025 4WIS4WID wall-climbing-robot study's statement that wheel odometry alone could not reach the intended endpoint of a test move, without a quantified error magnitude in the extracted text. [D1-S32, §V — see also Findings §5]
- The closest quantitative comparison found anywhere in this research between a no-sideslip steered-wheel locomotion mode and skid-steering — on the same physical vehicle, so confounding mechanical differences are controlled out — is a 1999 Carnegie Mellon Robotics Institute master's thesis on NASA's "Nomad" planetary-rover testbed. **Important scope caveat:** Nomad's "explicit steering" is not independent per-wheel (swerve-style) steering; it is "double axle steering," where the two wheels on each side of the vehicle are steered together through a four-bar-linkage mechanism so they trace concentric arcs (closer to a 4-wheel-steer/Ackermann-family geometry than to swerve's one-steering-motor-per-wheel redundancy), with skid steering produced on the same chassis by holding the steering linkages straight and differentially varying the left/right wheel speeds instead. [D1-S42, §3.2 "Transforming Chassis," §1.6.2 "Double axle steering"] Using differential GPS to track the vehicle's center position during steady-state circular turns at commanded radii of 4, 8, and 12 m, the thesis reports actual turning radii of 4.16 m, 8.27 m, and 12.40 m for explicit steering versus 4.24 m, 7.29 m, and 12.46 m for skid steering, expressed in the thesis as a "geometric slip" ratio (Rdesired/Ractual) of 0.96, 0.97, 0.97 for explicit steering versus 0.94, 1.1, 0.96 for skid steering; the author's own stated conclusion from this data is: "The explicit steering results are not significantly better than skid steering except for the 8m radius turn." [D1-S42, §4.3.2 "Position Data," Table 4 "Geometric Slip"] The same thesis separately found, from motor-current-derived torque measurements across the same radii, that "for all turns skid steering requires greater power and torque than for explicit turning because sideslip angles are greater in all cases," converging to equal power/torque only in the straight-driving (infinite-radius) limit, with skid steering's point-turn power "approximately double that of an explicit point turn." [D1-S42, Abstract; §5.2 "Perspectives"] This is a genuine, if partial, answer to the "swerve vs. skid-steer precision" question posed by this topic's research scope: on the one directly-measured same-vehicle comparison found, position-tracking precision was roughly comparable between the two steering modes (not dramatically better for the steered-wheel mode), while energy/torque cost was markedly and consistently higher for skid steering at all but the largest radius tested — but because the steered mode tested is double-axle, not independent-per-wheel swerve, this does not settle the swerve-specific question, which remains open below.
- No general mobile-robot kinematics survey or textbook found in this research explicitly ranks swerve/4WIS4WID against differential, Ackermann, and omni/mecanum drivetrains for precision-critical tasks as a stated conclusion; the closest is Campion's formal result (Findings §1) that swerve, despite requiring per-wheel steering actuators, carries the *same* degree-of-mobility classification (δm=1) as a simple car-like robot, not the higher mobility of a true omnidirectional (δm=3) platform — a structural reason the sources checked do not claim swerve to be kinematically "better" than Ackermann for precision, only more maneuverable in practice due to zero-radius turning and lateral translation. [D1-S01, p. 742]
- Isolated single-module bench testing of a differential-swerve pod (Findings §4, §6) reported "pod alignment time averaged under 0.4 seconds for 90° turns" and a steering angle-correction error of "3-5°," which, while not a whole-chassis position/heading precision figure, is the only concrete module-level control-loop performance number found for any swerve-type module (vendor or prototype) in this research. [D1-S37, §V.B]
- This remains a largely confirmed gap for swerve specifically, not a search miss within this topic's scope: multiple distinct search strategies targeting "swerve vs skid-steer odometry precision," "holonomic vs differential drive odometry precision comparison," general surveys, and (in this gap-check pass) neighboring automotive/AGV/planetary-rover dead-reckoning-accuracy framings were tried; the Shamah thesis above is the only same-vehicle, instrumented precision comparison found between a no-sideslip steered-wheel mode and skid steering, and it is for double-axle steering, not independent-per-wheel swerve.

### 8. ROS 2 / Nav2 / ros2_control software maturity

- `ros2_controllers`' `steering_controllers_library` is explicitly scoped to "mobile robot controllers with steering drives (2 degrees of freedom), with so-called non-holonomic constraints," and implements exactly three kinematic models: Bicycle (one steering, one drive joint), Tricycle (one steering, two drive joints), and Ackermann (two steering, two drive joints). None of the three models a 4+-module independently-steered-and-driven (swerve) base, and the library's own framing (non-holonomic, 2 DOF) is structurally incompatible with swerve's redundant per-wheel steering without substantial extension. [D1-S17, lines 14, 40–42]
- No official, maintained `ros2_control` swerve hardware-interface or controller package was found as part of the `ros-controls` organization's released packages. One community package, `zinger_swerve_controller` (built for ROS 2 Humble, Apache-2.0 licensed), was read directly in this gap-check pass: it implements a `ros2_control`-independent, plain-Python swerve controller that derives the same pseudo-inverse chassis-to-module kinematics already described from WPILib above (its README states the inverse relation as `V_i = |A| * V` and the forward/odometry relation as the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse of the same matrix, explicitly because the module-velocity system is overdetermined — "n * 2 values available and three variables"), and plans a future upgrade to a full 3D dynamic model from academic work by Neal Seegmiller, but as of this check currently ships only "a controller that... assumes linear behaviour for the drive modules," not the motion-profile-based (e.g. S-curve) controller its own `profile.py` module is designed to eventually support. [D1-S35, "Kinematics" and "Controllers" sections] As a maintenance-status data point: the repository has 42 GitHub stars and 14 forks, is not archived, carries 6 open issues, and its most recent commit (at the time of this check) dates to 2024-09-21 — over a year before this gap check — indicating a small, currently-dormant community effort rather than an actively-maintained package. [GitHub repository metadata, `pvandervelde/zinger_swerve_controller`, checked 2026-10-06]
- Nav2's Regulated Pure Pursuit (RPP) controller — unlike MPPI — is explicitly scoped around nonholonomic robot types in its own documentation: "This controller is suitable for use on all types of robots, including differential, legged, and ackermann steering vehicles. It may also be used on omni-directional platforms, but won't be able to fully leverage the lateral movements of the base (you may consider DWB instead)." [D1-S34, package README] This directly answers the subtopic question of which Nav2 controllers are documented as built around a nonholonomic assumption: RPP is designed for and tracks a path the same way regardless of robot type, and the maintainers' own stated guidance for a platform that needs to use lateral (vy) motion is to pick a different controller (DWB, or — per Findings above — MPPI with its `Omni` motion model) rather than RPP, since RPP's steering-angle/curvature-based path-tracking law has no mechanism to command vy directly.
- Nav2's MPPI (Model Predictive Path Integral) controller supports three selectable `motion_model` plugins — `mppi::AckermannMotionModel`, `mppi::DiffDriveMotionModel`, and `mppi::OmniMotionModel` — configured by a single string parameter. [D1-S18, section "Motion Models," lines 79–81]
- The `OmniMotionModel` class's only override of the base `MotionModel` interface is `isHolonomic()`, which unconditionally returns `true`; the class otherwise defines no per-wheel, per-module, or singularity-aware behavior. This means Nav2 MPPI's "holonomic" support operates purely at the chassis-twist level (it will sample and roll out vy≠0 trajectories) and is agnostic to *how* that holonomy is physically realized — the same `Omni` setting is equally applicable to a swerve base, a mecanum base, or any other platform capable of independent vx/vy/ω, and MPPI itself contains no model of swerve-specific effects such as module re-steering lag or ICR singularities. [D1-S18, `motion_models.hpp`, class `OmniMotionModel`]
- Nav2 MPPI's documented holonomic-specific parameters are limited to velocity-space sampling bounds: `vy_max` ("Max VY in either direction, if holonomic") and the `wz`/`vx` sampling standard deviations; there is no swerve-specific or per-module parameter anywhere in the controller's documented parameter set. [D1-S18, lines 49–55]
- No source found documents anything swerve- or holonomic-specific in `robot_localization` or `navsat_transform`; both packages consume a published `nav_msgs/Odometry` or twist/pose message regardless of the wheel architecture that produced it, so a swerve base's odometry (however it is produced — WPILib-style wheel odometry or a ROS 2 `SwerveDriveOdometry`-equivalent) would be fused identically to a differential or Ackermann base's odometry from `robot_localization`'s point of view. (This is an inference from the packages' general input-agnostic design, not a specific documented statement about swerve, so it is not assigned a strong citation beyond general ROS 2 package knowledge and is flagged here rather than stated as a finding with a dedicated source.)

### 9. Outdoor and non-carpet deployments

- Vendor-side evidence that swerve modules are designed for the FRC/FTC flat-carpet playing field specifically: REV's EasySwerve module's base plate and cover are "molded from 30 percent glass-filled nylon" explicitly to "shield[] the entire geartrain from **carpet debris**" — carpet, not grass, mud, dirt, or water, is the stated threat model, and no IP (ingress protection) rating is given for this or any other vendor's enclosure found in this research. [D1-S20, "Description"]
- No vendor (REV, SDS, WCP, ThriftyBot, ARMABOT) publishes a dust/water ingress-protection rating for any swerve module checked in this research; this was confirmed as a genuine absence by direct inspection of each vendor's own specification pages (not a search-coverage gap — the "Specifications" sections of each product page were read in full and contain no IP-rating field, no mention of IP6x/IP5x, and no explicit outdoor-use warranty language). [D1-S19 through D1-S26, all "Specifications" / product-description sections]
- Two confirmed, directly-documented outdoor deployments of swerve-drive robots at IGVC AutoNav (a grass/paved-lane outdoor competition) were found:
  - Georgia Tech RoboJackets' "Swervi" (IGVC 2022): built from scratch with custom four independent swerve modules (two motors each: one drive, one steering) specifically to replace a prior differential-drive robot's "limited mobility and maneuverability"; the report documents a new ICR-based wheel-odometry model made necessary by the switch (see Findings §1, §2) but does not describe any module weatherproofing, sealing, or wheel-choice rationale for outdoor terrain specifically — its weatherproofing discussion (latched doors, rain-excluding vent covers, waterproof-rated indicator light/LiDAR/e-stop) is scoped to the robot's electronics enclosure, not the swerve modules themselves. [D1-S29, §2.1, §2.2, §3.3 "Weatherproofing"]
  - University of Oklahoma Sooner Competitive Robotics' "Twistopher" (IGVC 2025, explicitly "SCR's first robot featuring swerve drive"): used four **custom-machined** (not off-the-shelf FRC-market) swerve modules, explicitly because of outdoor requirements — each module uses an 8-inch **pneumatic** wheel "ideal for all-terrain and all-weather performance," a 15:1 drive gear ratio tuned for a ~8 MPH top speed so the drive motors' power curve peaks near typical IGVC operating speed, and the whole chassis is weatherproofed with "acrylic panelling and weatherstripping" providing "a watertight seal." [D1-S30, §4.1, §4.2, §4.4 "Weatherproofing"]
- The Twistopher report makes an explicit, but unsupported-by-data, comparative traction/odometry claim: "Compared to other holonomic drive systems like mecanum, swerve drive has more traction and is more suited for outdoor environments with uneven terrain or obstacles. It is also less susceptible to wheel slip and error in odometry than mecanum." No measurement, citation, or test result is given in the report to support this statement — it is presented as the team's design rationale, not as a validated result, and is the only swerve-vs-mecanum-outdoors comparison statement found anywhere in this research. [D1-S30, §3.1]
- No source found documents a swerve-drive deployment, failure report, or maintenance record for mud or standing water specifically (as opposed to dry grass/dirt/pavement); this remains an open question (see below) despite the two IGVC design reports above, since neither describes a water- or mud-ingress incident or test.
- A separate, non-FRC/IGVC academic line of work applies the 4WIS4WID (functionally equivalent to swerve) architecture to an agricultural field robot concept: a 2024 preprint from IIT Kanpur researchers develops deep-reinforcement-learning crop-row-following and multi-row waypoint navigation for a 4WIS4WID robot to "perform functions like pesticide spraying and fruit harvesting" in an agricultural field, citing "non-geometric internal obstacles, space constraints, and outdoor conditions" as the motivating challenges; the work is simulation-based navigation/control research and does not report a physical outdoor field trial or any terrain-interaction/sealing data. [D1-S31, Abstract]
- The industrial/AGV "crab steering" product found (Lift-A-Loft's SPW16SPL aerial-lift platform) is an indoor aircraft-manufacturing-facility unit (used to position between work stands inside a hangar/factory), not an outdoor-rated product; its trade-press coverage gives no duty-cycle, load-capacity, or sealing specification, so it cannot be used as evidence either way about outdoor independent-steer durability. [D1-S28]

## Recommended practice

(Procedural steps as described by the sources themselves — not a recommendation about adopting swerve.)

1. Build the per-module inverse kinematics from each module's fixed (x, y) offset from the chosen robot-center reference point (WPILib's `SwerveDriveKinematics` constructor pattern), supporting an arbitrary module layout and, via an optional second argument to the module-states method, an arbitrary (non-center) instantaneous center of rotation. [D1-S10, sections "Constructing the Kinematics Object," "Using custom centers of rotation"] For the forward/odometry direction, or if implementing the conversion directly rather than using WPILib's class, the underlying computation is a matrix built from the module locations, inverted via its Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse because the per-module-velocity system is overdetermined. [D1-S35, "Kinematics" section]
2. Apply module-angle optimization (allow reversed wheel rotation instead of a 180° re-steer) before sending each module's setpoint [D1-S10, section "Module angle optimization"], and apply cosine compensation (scale commanded module speed by the cosine of remaining heading error) to reduce translational skew while a module is still re-aiming. [D1-S10, section "Cosine compensation"]
3. Discretize the commanded `ChassisSpeeds` before converting to module states whenever translation and rotation are commanded simultaneously, to avoid the first-order-kinematics translational skew described above; if no discretization time is configured, no correction is applied. [D1-S12, §1; D1-S13, "Chassis Speed Discretization"]
4. Secure the steering absolute encoder's magnet (e.g. with threadlocker/glue) and verify firm motor-to-shaft coupling before attributing erratic behavior to software; a community tuning checklist recommends working through hardware wiring/encoder/motor coupling causes before configuration causes (module locations, gear ratio) and only then PID gains, in that order. [D1-S14, overall checklist ordering, "Hardware causes" before "Configuration causes" before PID tuning]
5. When fusing wheel odometry with additional sensors for precision, use a Kalman filter (EKF or UKF) that incorporates at minimum an IMU and, where available, visual odometry, rather than relying on wheel odometry alone — demonstrated specifically to correct for wheel-slip-induced endpoint error and cross-axis drift in a 4WIS4WID platform. [D1-S32, §V, §VI]
6. If operating near a kinematic singularity cannot be avoided by path shaping alone and the platform has a variable footprint (adjustable leg/module distance from center), a potential-field controller can use that extra degree of freedom as an additional control input to avoid the singularity while minimizing trajectory deviation — demonstrated on a research humanoid mobile base, not on any FRC/FTC-style fixed-footprint module. [D1-S07, Abstract]

## Common mistakes

- Treating a swerve chassis as instantaneously fully omnidirectional (Campion type (3,0)): formally it is type (1,2), the same degree of mobility as a car-like robot, because its wheels still obey the standard no-sideslip rolling constraint and must be kinematically coordinated to a shared ICR before translating in an arbitrary new direction. [D1-S01, p. 742]
- Using naive first-order kinematics (constant per-timestep module commands) for a simultaneous translate+rotate maneuver, which skews the realized path in the direction of rotation; the fix is chassis-speed discretization, not a faster control loop alone. [D1-S12, §1]
- Using a naive least-squares ICR estimator without accounting for the near-parallel-wheels degeneracy (straight-line driving), where the estimate becomes numerically ill-conditioned because there are more unknowns than independent equations. [D1-S15, section "II. ICR Estimation"]
- Assuming any off-the-shelf FRC/FTC-market swerve module (REV, SDS, WCP, ThriftyBot) is rated for outdoor dust/water ingress because it is a sealed-looking metal or molded-plastic assembly: none of the specification pages checked publish an IP rating, and only one (REV EasySwerve) documents any debris shielding at all, and that is explicitly scoped to carpet debris. [D1-S19–D1-S25]
- Citing the Twistopher report's "swerve has more traction than mecanum outdoors" statement as an established, measured result: the report itself presents it as unreferenced design rationale, with no test data in the document. [D1-S30, §3.1]
- Assuming WPILib's SysId tool can characterize a whole swerve drivetrain the same way it characterizes a differential drivetrain: a WPILib core community member states directly that "SysId is primarily intended for identification of differential drive and appendage models. It's not designed for swerve drive," and that swerve module system ID (characterizing each module's two degrees of freedom separately, with the module's rotation physically locked for the linear test) "is different from whole-drivetrain system ID." [D1-S36; D1-S43]
- Treating Nomad's 1999 "explicit steering" result (Findings §7) as evidence about independent-per-wheel swerve specifically: Nomad's explicit-steering mode is double-axle (linkage-coupled per side) steering, not independent per-wheel steering, so its near-parity with skid steering in turning-radius precision should not be generalized to swerve without the same caveat the thesis itself requires.

## Disagreements between sources

- No direct factual disagreement between sources was found on a shared, specific claim within this topic's scope. The one place sources could be read as being in tension is terminological, not factual: FRC/WPILib/YAGSL documentation uses "skew" exclusively for the first-order-kinematics translational-path effect (Findings §2), while this research's own search for an automotive-style "wheel skew" (steering-axis-offset torque coupling, Findings §6) found no swerve-specific source using the term that way at all — so there is no disagreement to record, only an absence of the automotive usage in the swerve literature checked.

## Open questions

- No quantitative comparison of *independent-per-wheel* swerve odometry/heading precision and repeatability against skid-steer or Ackermann dead reckoning (same or comparable platform) was found in peer-reviewed literature, competition design reports, or vendor documentation, despite multiple targeted searches in this and the earlier research pass. A same-vehicle precision comparison does exist, but for double-axle (not independent-per-wheel) steering versus skid-steer, on a 1999 planetary-rover testbed — see Findings §7 (Shamah thesis) — so the swerve-specific version of this question remains open. [Findings §5, §7]
- No source gives a numeric figure (degrees of ICR-to-module proximity, or ms/degrees of added path-tracking error) for how close to a kinematic singularity a real swerve module's steering-rate saturation begins to visibly degrade short-horizon path tracking. Two additional academic singularity-treatment methods were found in this gap check (Sorour et al.'s actuator-acceleration-limited steer-rate damping; Clavien/Lauria/Michaud's "H representation"), but neither expresses the result as a quantified tracking-error-versus-singularity-proximity figure, so the specific quantification remains unanswered. [Findings §3]
- No source gives a measured steering-angle lag during fast direction changes translated into heading/path error for any **off-the-shelf vendor** module (REV, SDS, WCP, ThriftyBot). One figure now exists for a non-vendor differential-swerve prototype module (3-5° angle-correction error, <0.4 s for a 90° re-aim) — see Findings §4, §7 — narrowing but not closing this gap. [Findings §4]
- CAN-bus *data loading* is now partially quantified (Findings §6: ≈50% of a CAN 2.0 bus's recommended ceiling, by this research's own arithmetic on CTRE's published per-device defaults, for a representative 8-Talon-FX-plus-8-CANcoder swerve robot), but brownout risk from simultaneous multi-module *current draw* (an electrical-supply question, not a data-bus question) remains unquantified in any source found. [Findings §6]
- No automotive-style "wheel skew"/scrub-radius analysis was found applied to a swerve module specifically (the reference textbook, Gillespie, could not be obtained — see D1-S09, not downloaded). An automated open-access check was re-run in this gap check for Muir & Neuman (D1-S04) via the Unpaywall API against its journal DOI, confirming no open-access location is registered; Gillespie's 1992 SAE textbook has no DOI, so the same DOI-keyed check could not be run for it — its unavailability rests on the manual paywall search only (see Foundational references, D1-S09). [Findings §6]
- No documented outdoor deployment, failure report, or maintenance record covering mud or standing-water ingress specifically (as opposed to dry grass/dirt/pavement) was found for any swerve-drive robot. [Findings §9]
- The maintenance status of one community `ros2_control` swerve controller package was checked in this gap check (`zinger_swerve_controller`: 42 stars, Apache-2.0, last commit 2024-09-21, appears dormant — Findings §8); its test coverage and design completeness beyond what its own README documents, and the status of other community efforts (e.g. the ROS Discourse "Independent (Swerve) steering controller" thread), were not independently checked and remain open.
- No independent, non-manufacturer source (e.g. a competition team's post-season teardown report, or an academic durability study) was found quantifying azimuth-gear backlash growth, belt wear (for belt-reduced modules such as MK4i or WCP's Standard Swerve X), or bearing wear under repeated cornering load for any specific off-the-shelf module over its service life.
