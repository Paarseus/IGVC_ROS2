## Summary

- The correction a tracked vehicle needs when turning is not an artifact of imperfect tuning: a 2022 tracked-locomotion review states plainly that for tracked ground mobile robots "the contact surface with the ground is remarkable, and macroscopic skidding is unavoidable during steering" [D5-S01, section 6.1], and a 2021 account of fielding a 1450 kg tracked UGV with ROS 2 makes the same point independently — modelling a tracked vehicle as a wheeled unicycle "has some potential problems as variation of the relative velocity of the two tracks results in slippage as well as soil shearing and compacting in order to achieve steering" [D5-S17, p. 117580S-2]. Both are general statements about the mechanism, not reports of a solved problem.
- Track **tension** is a second, separate lever on top of surface/speed/radius: soil-bin experiments on a rice combine found tractive efficiency depends on tension in a soil-type-dependent, non-monotonic way (peaking at about 1.75 kN in sandy loam, falling monotonically with tension in loam) [D5-S05, p. 237], and a multibody-dynamics study of a military tracked vehicle explored how "pre-tensions, traction forces, turning resistances, sprocket torques, ground profiles, and vehicle speeds" each drive dynamic track tension during a turn [D5-S07, abstract]. No source found ties tension into a live steering/kinematic-correction control loop — every tension-sensing method found (patents, the two tension studies above) is positioned for maintenance alerting or offline/simulation analysis, not real-time correction [D5-S08; D5-S06].
- Beyond a fitted constant multiplier, the literature offers three families of alternative: terramechanics/multibody models that predict slip and traction from track geometry, grousers and soil parameters rather than fitting one number [D5-S09]; online estimators (EKF, sliding-mode observers, soft-switching SMO + neural adaptation) that identify slip or ICR location in real time [D5-S10] [D5-S11] [D5-S12]; and a 2026 review of the whole field for wheeled/tracked harvesters [D5-S04]. One such design is reported in two stages: without its slip observer active, an adaptive controller still leaves "a large trajectory tracking control error during the turning of the tracked robot...not eliminated" relative to a plain (non-adaptive) backstepping baseline, but once the same paper's online soft-switching sliding-mode observer (SSMO) is added and compensated into that adaptive controller, the full method "effectively improves the trajectory tracking accuracy of the tracked robot, especially when it turns" [D5-S12, "6 Conclusions"] — i.e. the uncompensated controller shows the residual, and the paper's own proposed, fully-compensated method is reported to correct it specifically on turns.
- Tracked-chassis products exist well beyond one named baseline, from hobby/competition-scale molded-rubber-track kits [D5-S14] and bolt-on drive modules [D5-S13] up to a government-owned modified iRobot PackBot used as an open research platform by multiple labs and at least one IGVC team [D5-S16] [D5-S19] [D5-S15], and a 1450 kg tracked UGV used in NATO-affiliated autonomy research [D5-S17]. Every one of these, at every scale, is still commanded as a two-value (forward velocity, rotation velocity) differential-drive interface converted to per-track speeds downstream — none exposes or needs a tracked-specific motion model to its ROS stack [D5-S17, p. 117580S-12] [D5-S23] [D5-S24].
- The clearest documented before/after case found is a team that fielded a tracked ("tank") platform one year and a wheeled platform the next: the tracks "frequently came off" under competition-grass stress, could not climb the autonomous-challenge incline without slipping back, and limited achievable speed to 2.5–3 mph against a 5 mph design target, so the team "immediately" decided to move to wheels [D5-S18, pp. 2–3]. Independently, agricultural field-robotics sources state that tracked robots are chosen specifically for soft-terrain traction and flotation but, in the same breath, that they "have complex track-ground interactions and slippage due to differential velocities between treads" [D5-S20, p. 2], which "can result in off-track navigation due to unknown traction coefficients" [D5-S20, abstract] — the same trade-off from two different directions.
- Beyond tension, two further documented, independent axes of rubber-track failure/maintenance exist: soft-debris (mud) packing into undercarriage components, which manufacturers only partly mitigate by track/idler/sprocket shaping [D5-S26, col. 1], and cold-temperature stiffening/brittleness, which one manufacturer's own comparison testing shows depends heavily on the specific rubber compound used (brittle point −56°C vs. −94°C for two tread compounds) [D5-S25, Table 2]. Real-time IMU-based slip correction is also no longer only an academic idea: production patents exist for both a tracked-capable vehicle controller that closes the loop from IMU-predicted speed into propulsion power [D5-S27] and, in the academic literature already covered by sibling topic `C2_drive_kinematics`, a GPS+IMU sensor-fusion estimator field-validated on a tracked vehicle [cross-referenced C2-S30].

## Findings

### 1. Is the tracked turning-correction burden fundamental, and does track tension add a separate dependency?

- A dedicated 2022 review of tracked ground mobile robot locomotion states, discussing why skid-steering analysis is harder for tracks than wheels: "while for wheeled robots the contact areas with the ground are relatively small with respect to the robot dimensions, for [tracked ground mobile robots] the contact surface with the ground is remarkable, and macroscopic skidding is unavoidable during steering (skid steering)." [D5-S01, section 6.1, p. 10 of 17]
- Independently, researchers at the Norwegian Defence Research Establishment, building a ROS 2 autonomy stack on a 1450 kg Milrem THeMIS tracked UGV, make the same point from a controls perspective: modelling a tracked vehicle as a wheeled "unicycle" (the standard differential-drive abstraction) "has some potential problems as variation of the relative velocity of the two tracks results in slippage as well as soil shearing and compacting in order to achieve steering." [D5-S17, p. 117580S-2]
- The 2022 review traces the skid-steering turning-resistance analysis to a line of classical mechanics work (Steeds 1950, Weiss, Crosheck, Kitano and Jyozaki, Ehlert et al.) and notes that "a general theory for skid steering on firm ground is discussed [in Wong & Chiang, 2001], which shows a close agreement with experimental results" — i.e. the physical mechanism is considered solved analytically on firm ground, not merely fitted. [D5-S01, section 6.1, p. 10 of 17]
- Track **tension** is a documented, separate dependency on top of surface, speed and turning radius. A soil-bin experiment on a rice-combine undercarriage, run at three tension levels (0.71, 1.75, 3.84 kN) and three forward speeds (0.17, 0.32, 0.45 m/s) in loam and sandy loam, found tractive efficiency's dependence on tension was "significantly dependent on soil type but not on forward velocities": efficiency fell monotonically with increasing tension in loam, but rose to a peak and then fell in sandy loam, with the peak (the tested combine's maximum tractive efficiency) at about 1.75 kN tension in sandy loam and at the lowest tested tension in loam, both near 5% slippage. [D5-S05, p. 237 (abstract)]
- A multibody-dynamics simulation study of a high-mobility tracked military vehicle explored "the effects of pre-tensions, traction forces, turning resistances, sprocket torques, ground profiles, and vehicle speeds, for dynamic responses of track tensions," validating the simulated dynamic tension against experimental measurements. [D5-S07, abstract]
- A multibody model of a small farming tracked vehicle that adds custom grouser-contact forces derived from deformable-soil mechanics reports that tests in the multibody environment "show how the proposed model is able to obtain tractive performance similar to equivalent analytical solutions and how the grousers improve the availability of tractive force for certain type of soil characteristics" — i.e. grouser geometry, not just tension, measurably changes the tractive-force/slip outcome the correction has to account for. [D5-S09, abstract]
- Without its real-time slip-parameter observer compensating it, an adaptive trajectory controller for a tracked robot still leaves a turning-specific residual relative to a plain (non-adaptive) backstepping baseline: "there is still a large trajectory tracking control error during the turning of the tracked robot, and it illustrates that the influence of slide effect on trajectory tracking control is not eliminated." Once the same paper's soft-switching sliding-mode observer (SSMO) is identified and its slip-parameter estimates are compensated into that adaptive controller, the same authors report the opposite outcome specifically on turns: the full method "effectively improves the trajectory tracking accuracy of the tracked robot, especially when it turns." [D5-S12, "6 Conclusions"]
- Agricultural-robotics sources frame the same trade-off from the traction side: tracked robots are preferred over wheeled robots for field work specifically because of "improved traction and large contact area with the ground, which minimizes adverse impacts on the soil," citing Bekker 1956 — but the same robots suffer "complex track-ground interactions and slippage due to differential velocities between treads." [D5-S20, p. 2]

### 2. Track-tension monitoring: sensing methods and whether tension feeds a control loop

- A U.S. patent (assignee DRB Industrial Co., granted 2024) for a "track tension monitoring method and system" senses tension **indirectly**, via hydraulic pressure in the track tensioner, through "a sensor unit configured to be installed in the tensioner and measure tension generated in the tracks by sensing pressure transferred to the tensioner." [D5-S08, col. 3]
- That patent's stated purpose is maintenance alerting, not control: when track tension is found abnormal, "the output section 80...may sound an alarm to a user or output a warning message on a screen through an alarm signal" [D5-S08, col. 5], so that "the user may take an action so that excessive tension or low tension may not be applied to the tracks" [D5-S08, col. 8] — a human-in-the-loop maintenance response, not an automatic correction.
- A 2000 paper on "Track Tension Estimation in Tracked Vehicles under Steering" develops kinetic models for the road wheels to estimate track tension around the sprocket **during turning and pivoting**, explicitly so that the method "does not require the tuning of the turning resistance, which makes it difficult to estimate the track tension in turning," and verifies the estimator through multibody-dynamics simulation of steering and pivoting. [D5-S06, p. 115 (abstract)] This is the one source found in this search that connects tension to steering dynamics through an estimator rather than a static maintenance check — but the tension estimate is the paper's *output* (a monitoring/estimation result), not an input the paper feeds back into a steering controller.
- No source found in this search uses a measured or estimated track-tension value as a real-time input to a steering or kinematic-correction control loop; every tension-related method found here is positioned as offline analysis (soil-bin and multibody-simulation studies, D5-S05/D5-S07), an estimator whose output is the tension itself (D5-S06), or a maintenance alert (D5-S08). This gap is carried forward to Open questions.

### 3. Model-based and sensor-fusion alternatives to a hand-tuned constant multiplier

- Terramechanics/multibody models predict traction, slip and sinkage from physical parameters instead of a single fitted number. A small farming tracked vehicle's multibody model, with custom per-link grouser-contact forces derived from deformable-soil mechanics equations, can "investigate vehicle performance and limit operating conditions related to the vehicle and soil characteristics," reporting results "in terms of traction force, slip and sinkage on different types of terrain." [D5-S09, abstract]
- Online estimators reported specifically for tracked vehicles include:
  - An extended Kalman filter that identifies an online slip model based on the tracks' instantaneous centers of rotation (ICRs), parameterized over both tracks' rolling speeds, to predict short-term forward trajectory from position measurements (rather than the more common velocity-feedback ICR estimate). [D5-S10, abstract]
  - A sliding-mode observer and an extended Kalman filter, compared directly against each other for estimating tracked-vehicle slip parameters from trajectory measurement: "the simulations and the experimental results demonstrate that sliding mode observer gives high accuracy and better convergence speed over Extended Kalman Filter," and the sliding-mode observer "is robust against measurement noise." [D5-S11, "Conclusions"]
  - A soft-switching sliding-mode observer (SSMO) that identifies slip parameters in real time and compensates them into a backstepping trajectory controller whose gains are themselves adaptively tuned online by a back-propagation neural network. The paper reports two separate simulation comparisons: first, with the SSMO not yet in the loop, the adaptive (BPNN-tuned) backstepping controller "achieves better control effects, especially on the linear trajectories" than a plain, non-adaptive backstepping baseline, but a large turning-specific tracking error remains uncorrected at this stage (per Finding 1 above); second, once the SSMO's slip-parameter estimates are compensated into that same adaptive controller, the full method is reported to improve tracking accuracy "especially when it turns" — i.e. the turning-specific gain comes from adding the SSMO, not from the adaptive-vs-plain-backstepping comparison alone. [D5-S12, abstract; "6 Conclusions"]
- A 2026 review of slip-ratio control for wheeled/tracked harvesters groups the whole field into three families — kinematic-model-based control, dynamic/terramechanics-model-based control, and combined kinematic-dynamic models — and states that purely kinematic methods "do not require explicit consideration of the full complexity of vehicle-environment dynamics... [and] usually perform well under medium-low-speed and low-curvature conditions," but "their applicability becomes limited under conditions of large lateral acceleration," while combined kinematic-dynamic models "usually perform better in slip control, adaptation to complex terrain, and heavy-load operation" at the cost of needing to model "traction, tire-soil friction, and sinkage effects" explicitly. [D5-S04, section 4.2.2–4.2.3]
- Sensor fusion of GNSS (GPS) with IMU data, specifically for a tracked vehicle's online ICR/slip estimate, is already covered in depth by sibling topic `C2_drive_kinematics`: Çiloğlu and Kutluay's EKF estimates the left and right track ICR positions online "from GPS and IMU," feeding a variable artificial track width to a pure-pursuit path-tracking controller for an off-road tracked vehicle, and field-validated on a dirt road with about 7% grade, where it cut pure-pursuit mean absolute path error from 17.1 cm to 9.6 cm (about 44%) [cross-referenced from C2-S30, not re-downloaded into D5 — see this topic's Foundational-references overlap note]. This directly answers this topic's "sensor-fusion slip correction" question for the tracked case and was not previously cross-referenced from D5's own Finding 3.
- The same real-time IMU-based correction idea is also implemented and patented by a production machine manufacturer, not only studied academically: a Deere & Company patent, "IMU Based Traction Control for Wheeled or Tracked Machine," detects a wheel/track slippage condition by comparing a drivetrain component's own ground speed against a predicted ground speed derived from an inertial measurement unit's measured fore/aft acceleration, and "generat[es] a driveline modification command to adjust propulsion power of the drivetrain component until the wheel slippage condition reaches a specified target" — closing the loop from the IMU-predicted speed directly into a propulsion-power command, on a machine the patent's own title and claims cover for "wheeled or tracked" drivetrains alike. [D5-S27, Abstract]
- That same review's conclusion calls for future work to make these methods more field-ready: "future research should focus on enhancing the robustness of slip prediction models and developing more adaptive, real-time control strategies... combining data-driven approaches with physical models holds great potential." [D5-S04, "Conclusions"] This frames current terrain-adaptive/online methods as still maturing rather than closed problems.
- One quantitative field result for computational cost comes from the same tracked field robot as Finding 1/5's traction-coefficient discussion, using a receding-horizon estimation-and-control (RHEC) framework: mean tracking (Euclidean) error 0.0423 m and required computation time 0.88 ms per update (worst case 2.85 ms) on a bumpy, wet soil field, demonstrating millisecond-scale online slip/traction-parameter estimation is achievable on the robot's own onboard compute. [D5-S20, abstract; "Conclusions"] The same framework, compared against an EKF-based controller on the identical hardware, violated a 0.12 m row-spacing tracking-error bound 17 times while the RHEC framework violated it zero times — a direct online-estimator-vs-EKF comparison on a tracked vehicle, not merely a simulation study. [D5-S20, Figs. 8–9 discussion]

### 4. Other tracked-chassis products and kits

- **Belt/track drive modules (competition-robot scale).** A representative bolt-on tracked drivetrain module uses timing-belt treads (nitrile roughtop or urethane flat) driven by five polycarbonate pulleys and 22-tooth sprockets per side, with tension set by a dual-turnbuckle mechanism contained within the side plates so tensioning does not change the chassis's outer frame dimensions; the module accepts several interchangeable gearbox options bolted to its inside plate. [D5-S13] A listed product-page customer review reports that the structural extrusions connecting two such modules "are not robust enough" for high-impact competition use, citing denting and eventual drivetrain failure after field impacts. [D5-S13]
- **Molded continuous rubber tracks (hobby/research-robot scale).** A representative product line sells bare molded rubber tracks (no splice, mesh-reinforced, sold at least in 2.75 and 4 inch widths in this product family) that require the buyer to design their own wheels and guide cleats; the 4-inch-wide track is specified at about 2160 mm circumference, 0.61 inch tread thickness over the lugs, 4.1 lb per tread, with lugs 8 mm thick and 10 mm tall spaced every 48 mm. [D5-S14] This is a track-only product — unlike D5-S13, it supplies no drive sprockets, tensioner or gearbox mount, so the integration burden of designing those falls entirely on the builder.
- **Government-owned modified research platform (mid-size, tracked).** The GVR-Bot is a U.S. Army-owned, modified iRobot PackBot 510: the Army replaced the original PackBot's single, one-piece electronics system with a distributed architecture of government-designed and -owned hardware and software components, stated to reduce maintenance cost because "if anything breaks, you can trouble-shoot it down to that one part." [D5-S16] Several GVR-Bot chassis exist and are shared across Army research organizations under an IOP V2-compliant open architecture. [D5-S16] A 2024 peer-reviewed robotics paper that deploys a safety-control framework on a GVR-Bot describes its drivetrain integration concretely: a custom compute payload (an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin) sends commanded linear/angular body velocities over ROS1 to the GVR-Bot's internal drive computer (an Intel Atom), which converts them "into individual track speeds, which are regulated via high-rate controllers on the GVR-Bot." [D5-S15, section IV-A, "Hardware"] A separate USMA West Point IGVC team's 2018 design report, built on this same GVR-Bot base, independently confirms the two-tracks-with-rubber-tread layout, a suspension with "suspending brackets in the inner side of the track which are adjustable to different grounds," and that the team tested and adjusted parameters "on a replicated testing ground" because the competition runs on grass. [D5-S19, section 3.3]
- **Large-scale tracked research UGV with a documented ROS 2 interface.** The Milrem THeMIS, used as the Norwegian Defence Research Establishment's "Tor" autonomy research platform, is "interfaced using ROS2, and is controlled by sending velocity commands for each belt" through a proprietary Milrem low-level controller; the research team's own higher-level path-following controller computes a standard unicycle-style forward/rotational velocity pair and then converts that pair to left/right belt speed before sending it on. [D5-S17, pp. 117580S-2, 117580S-11] Even in simulation, the vehicle is modelled as "a two wheeled robot where the density of the two 'wheels' have been tuned" to approximate its real on-grass behaviour — i.e. the dedicated multi-year ROS 2 integration of a 1450 kg tracked UGV still represents it to its own motion planner as a differential-drive pair of wheels, not as a tracked-specific model. [D5-S17, p. 117580S-12]
- **Track material/construction and precision/maintenance.** No single source compares molded continuous belts versus segmented/linked tracks for precision; what the sources do connect to maintenance is track material and tension (see subtopic 6) rather than molded-vs-segmented choice specifically.
- **Cost.** Only the bolt-on drive-module and bare-track products (D5-S13, D5-S14) publish prices directly: a complete two-module drive-module chassis plus connecting extrusions is sold as a single consumer product, and a representative 4-inch bare molded track pair is listed at $349 (with custom lengths at extra cost and 4–8 week lead time). [D5-S13; D5-S14] No cost figure was found for the GVR-Bot or the Milrem THeMIS — both are government-/program-owned platforms rather than commercially listed products.

### 5. IGVC and field-robotics design-report experience with tracked platforms

- A Penn State IGVC team's 2009 design report describes abandoning its prior year's tracked ("tank") platform for a wheeled Segway RMP400-based platform, under a section titled "Failure Analysis": "over the course of the competition, the tank treads frequently came off in the middle of a practice run. It was obvious that they could not handle the additional stress posed by the tall grass on the competition field. Further, the tank platform last year was unable to make it up the incline in the Autonomous Challenge without slipping back down. Given these drawbacks and the success of other teams' wheeled platforms, it was immediately obvious that we needed to transition away from treads to a wheeled robot platform." [D5-S18, p. 2]
- The same report documents a speed shortfall specific to the tracks under load: "the tank robot had a theoretical maximum speed of 5mph, but because of the tall grass and inclines, we observed an actual top speed around 2.5 to 3 mph," and when the team tried to fix this by raising the gear ratio or fitting larger motors, the gear-ratio change "overheated the motors during testing," while the larger motors "fixed the top speed issue, but the tank tracks came off much more frequently under the higher motor power" — a direct, reported coupling between pushing more power through the tracks and track-throw frequency. [D5-S18, p. 3]
- The same report separately notes that the tank platform's quadrature encoders "were not used in competition," so the team had no closed-loop velocity/yaw feedback on the tracked platform and identified "a platform with accurate wheel odometry" as "essential" for its replacement design — a maintenance/integration gap as much as a mechanical one. [D5-S18, p. 3]
- Conversely, a USMA West Point 2018 IGVC team deliberately built on a tracked GVR-Bot platform and reports advantages framed around robustness and battery logistics rather than precision: "Izzy's platform and battery system require less batteries for powering and a more reliable driving system than last year's robot which required twelve of the same type of battery," and the team valued the GVR-Bot's "military-grade" design "to withstand inclement weather." [D5-S19, section 1.4; section 3.2] The report does not discuss navigation precision or kinematic correction at all — its focus is sensor integration and ROS software architecture on top of the inherited chassis.
- Agricultural/field-robotics sources state the traction-vs-precision trade-off directly from the field side: tracked robots are preferred for field-based phenotyping "due to their improved traction and large contact area with the ground," but the same robots have "complex track-ground interactions and slippage due to differential velocities between treads," which can cause "off-track navigation due to unknown traction coefficients" serious enough to "cause crop damage" in a row-crop setting. [D5-S20, p. 2, abstract]

### 6. Durability, failure modes and maintenance burden specific to rubber tracks

- An official compact-track-loader rubber-track maintenance guide states the two opposite tension failure modes directly: "proper track tensioning is particularly important, as excessive tightness can accelerate component wear, and looseness can cause de-tracking." [D5-S21, p. 9] It further attributes de-tracking specifically to worn sprockets: "if rubber track is too loose due to worn-out sprockets, meshing and skipping could occur, potentially causing the track to derail." [D5-S21, p. 10]
- The same guide recommends checking track sag "on a weekly basis or as needed," since "rubber tracks lose tension over time, even with proper care and maintenance," and gives a concrete replacement criterion: replace rubber tracks "when 40 percent of the tread depth remains when compared to a new set of tracks." [D5-S21, p. 10]
- It lists explicit operating restrictions for rubber tracks: they are "not designed or recommended for work... over sharp or angular rocks," "in soils containing materials such as concrete and rebar," "for landfill maintenance," or "for extended periods of high-speed roading" — "continuous roading and aggressive turning on hard surfaces will shorten track wear life," and daily hard-surface roading "should never exceed 10 percent of total daily operation." [D5-S21, p. 12]
- It also prescribes a break-in and storage regime: new tracks must be broken in "immediately" per the operator's manual with alignment rechecked at every replacement, and idle tracked machines should be "moved once a month," kept "out of direct sunlight," and stored away from "fuel vapor or ozone-producing electric devices" during off-season storage. [D5-S21, p. 9]
- At the smaller field-robot scale, a long-running University-of-Michigan account of UGV reliability research cites earlier studies (Carlson and Murphy, cited second-hand, not independently downloaded — see Sources) reporting that search-and-rescue UGV mean time between failures is "typically between 6 and 20 hours," and separately that "current UGV design goals are a mean-time-between-failure (MTBF) of 100 hours, and most actually achieve MTBF of about 20 hours or less." [D5-S22, pp. 2–3] The same source illustrates the PackBot (an iRobot tracked platform, the GVR-Bot's unmodified ancestor) as "a typical UGV" in its reliability discussion but does not itself break out a track-specific failure rate. [D5-S22, p. 1, Figure 1]
- **Mud/debris packing is a separate rubber- and steel-track failure/maintenance mechanism from tension and tread wear.** A Caterpillar undercarriage patent's background section states that during operation "relatively soft material such as mud can become packed into components of the track system and have various deleterious consequences," while separately "rocks making their way between rotating components of a track system in the track chain itself are one example where mechanical stress and wear on track system components occurs." [D5-S26, col. 1, lines ~36–44] It further reports that manufacturers have long used idler-recoil mechanisms (to let rocks "pass through the track or be crushed without unduly stressing the components") and shaped track/idler/sprocket geometry plus striker bars to push packed debris back out during normal track operation — but that even so, "manual track and/or machine cleaning is still often necessary, and tends to be quite labor intensive," i.e. the design mitigations reduce but do not eliminate the problem. [D5-S26, col. 1, lines ~34–48]
- **Rubber-track material behaviour is also temperature-dependent, a third documented axis of rubber-track behaviour change alongside tension and wear.** A Goodyear/LOC Performance Products patent on cold-environment endless rubber tracks reports ASTM D2137 brittle-point testing of 150°C-cured track-tread rubber samples (Table 2): a control compound (natural rubber plus styrene-butadiene rubber) had a brittle point of −56°C, while two reformulated low-glass-transition-temperature compounds — Sample B (low-Tg natural polyisoprene plus cis-1,4-polybutadiene) and Sample C (the same two elastomers in different proportions) — reached an estimated −80 to −90°C and a measured −94°C respectively (Table 1 and Table 2) — the patent's own discussion calls this brittle-point improvement "significant for use at very low temperatures." (The patent's broader claimed composition elsewhere in the specification additionally allows synthetic polyisoprene and a low-freeze-point plasticizer as options, but the actual Samples B and C whose brittle points were measured use polybutadiene, not synthetic polyisoprene, and list no plasticizer ingredient.) [D5-S25, cols. 5–6, 9–10, Table 2 and discussion] Fielded tracks built with the reformulated compounds, mounted on several tractor-type vehicles, showed "no indications of adverse physical effects or damage...due to the extremely low temperature" after "an average of from about 700 to about 1000 vehicular operational hours" at conditions reaching "a low temperature of about −50°C." [D5-S25, col. 13, "Example III"]

### 7. General wheeled-vs-tracked mobility and terrain-suitability literature

- A controlled 1970 U.S. Army field study tested six wheeled and four tracked vehicles across multiple terrain and seasonal conditions using the Waterways Experiment Station's analytical mobility model, and concluded that "neither wheels nor tracks appeared to result consistently in better performance; wheeled vehicles performed better in some circumstances, and tracked performed better in others," and that "a vehicle can perform well in one set of terrain conditions, but it will suffer penalties in another." [D5-S03, "Conclusions," para. 95] Wet-season conditions reduced performance (lower average traverse speed, lower average center-line speed, higher average fuel consumption, lower cargo-delivery rate) for most vehicles in the study, wheeled and tracked alike; the M561 is listed as the exception vehicle for traverse speed, center-line speed and cargo-delivery rate, and the fuel-consumption measure separately lists three exception vehicles (M561, M706 and M54A2). [D5-S03, "Conclusions," para. 95d]
- The dedicated 2022 tracked-locomotion review summarizes tracks' core suitability case plainly in its abstract: "tracked locomotion, due to the large contact surface with the ground, is particularly suitable for tackling soft, yielding, and irregular terrains, but is characterized by lower speed and energy efficiency than wheeled locomotion, and lower obstacle-climbing capability than legged locomotion." [D5-S01, abstract]
- The 2022 review proposes three parallel taxonomies for classifying any tracked ground mobile robot: by **body architecture** (non-articulated vs. articulated, and the type of articulation), by **track profile** (constant, or passively/actively deformable), and by **track type** (continuous, modular/mechanical, or omni-tracks). [D5-S01, section 2, "Classifications of Tracked Locomotion Systems"] Within this scheme, a simple two-track skid-steer chassis with rigid, non-deformable tracks sits in the non-articulated/purely-tracked body-architecture category (TGMRs-NA-T), the constant-track-profile category (TGMRs-CP) — stated to be "the most widespread for their simplicity, especially for small-scale realizations in which the structural resistance is less critical" — and the continuous-track-type category (TGMRs-CT), stated to be "undoubtedly the most widespread, especially for medium to large scale realizations, for the better structural features of the continuous tracks." [D5-S01, section 7, "Conclusions"]
- The general wheeled/tracked/legged/hybrid locomotion survey (shared with sibling topic D2) states that tracked robots "are well suited to move on uneven and soft terrains and overcome obstacles, thanks to their large ground contact surface, but they move more slowly and consume more energy than wheeled robots," and are "subject to vibrations as the lateral track profile is a polygon with moving vertices," which "limits maximum speed and reduces mechanical efficiency." [D5-S02, section 3.2] (Quoted identically by D2-S11 in the sibling wheeled-skid-steer topic; reproduced here because it compares both architectures directly.)

### 8. ROS 2 / Nav2 integration maturity for tracked platforms

- Every documented tracked-platform ROS integration found in this search exposes the same two-value differential-drive-style command interface at its ROS boundary, converted to per-track speeds either in the platform's own low-level controller or immediately before it: the Milrem THeMIS accepts "velocity commands for each belt" over a proprietary controller behind its ROS 2 node [D5-S17, p. 117580S-2], and the GVR-Bot's internal Intel Atom drive computer converts a commanded (linear, angular) body-velocity pair "into individual track speeds, which are regulated via high-rate controllers on the GVR-Bot" after the command arrives over ROS1 [D5-S15, section IV-A, "Hardware"]. Neither platform's ROS-facing interface is track-specific.
- The Milrem integration goes a step further at the simulation/planning layer: even when deliberately modelling the vehicle for a software-in-the-loop test, the researchers represent "Tor" (their THeMIS-based platform) in their 2D physics simulator "as a two wheeled robot where the density of the two 'wheels' have been tuned such that the simulated behaviour in Veranda is similar to Tor driving on an even grass field." [D5-S17, p. 117580S-12] The authors explicitly discuss the standard motion-model dichotomy before making this choice: "vehicles are broadly divided into two categories; Ackermann vehicles or car-like vehicles that are modeled as bicycles, and differential drive or skid-steer vehicles that are modeled as unicycles," noting only afterward that modelling a tracked vehicle this way "has some potential problems." [D5-S17, p. 117580S-2] No tracked-specific alternative is adopted even by this dedicated, multi-sensor, multi-year tracked-UGV autonomy project.
- This matches what sibling topic `D2_wheeled_skid_steer` already found for wheeled skid-steer bases: Nav2's MPPI controller exposes a `motion_model` parameter with only `"DiffDrive"`, `"Omni"`, and `"Ackermann"` as options [D2-S36; copied into this topic's `sources/` as D5-S23], and the underlying `nav2_mppi_controller` source implements exactly those three as concrete classes — `DiffDriveMotionModel`, `OmniMotionModel`, and `AckermannMotionModel` [D2-S37; copied as D5-S24] — no tracked-specific motion model exists in the Nav2 codebase for either wheeled or tracked skid-steer vehicles. A tracked vehicle is represented to Nav2, as to every ROS integration found in this search, as an (optionally kinematically corrected) differential-drive base.

### 9. Net assessment: when sources favour tracks, and when they favour switching away

- Sources that favour tracks do so on **terrain/traction grounds**, stated as the primary reason: the tracked-locomotion review's own summary of the case for tracks is soft/yielding/irregular terrain capability from large ground contact [D5-S01, abstract]; the field-phenotyping researchers' stated reason for choosing a tracked platform is "improved traction and large contact area with the ground, which minimizes adverse impacts on the soil" [D5-S20, p. 2]; and the West Point team's stated reasons for its GVR-Bot choice are ruggedness, weather resistance and reduced battery/power-system complexity, not precision [D5-S19, sections 1.4, 3.2, 3.4].
- Sources that favour switching away from tracks do so primarily on **mechanical reliability and achievable-speed grounds under real competition/field stress**, not on the kinematic-correction burden itself: the Penn State team's "immediate" decision to leave tracks was driven by de-tracking under grass load, inability to climb a required incline, and a roughly 40–50% shortfall between theoretical and achieved top speed — all mechanical/traction failures rather than a stated complaint about turning-correction accuracy or tuning effort [D5-S18, pp. 2–3]. No source found in this search states explicitly that the kinematic/control correction burden itself (as opposed to mechanical reliability, traction, or speed) was the deciding factor in a tracks-to-wheels switch.
- The clearest quantified both-directions trade-off is the pair of observations in Finding 5: field-robotics sources state tracks are chosen *for* traction/flotation and simultaneously report, in the same body of literature, that the resulting "complex track-ground interactions and slippage due to differential velocities between treads" can themselves cause "off-track navigation due to unknown traction coefficients" [D5-S20] — i.e. the same physical property (large, compliant, multi-point ground contact) that gives tracks their terrain advantage is reported as the source of their navigation-precision cost. This is consistent with, but does not by itself resolve, the fundamental-vs-reducible question in Finding 1.
- The one documented large-scale tracked-UGV autonomy program found (Milrem THeMIS/Tor) did not switch away from tracks; instead it retained the tracked chassis for its mechanical/mobility properties and absorbed the kinematic mismatch at the software layer, by controlling and even simulating the vehicle through an ordinary differential-drive abstraction [D5-S17] — the same strategy already found in wheeled-skid-steer practice.

## Recommended practice

What the sources recommend, in order. (The ICR/effective-width model-fitting procedure itself — spin-in-place calibration, external ground truth, per-terrain re-identification — is covered in depth in `C2_drive_kinematics`'s own Recommended practice and is not repeated here.)

1. Treat turning-induced skid on a tracked chassis as a physical property of the large, rigid track–ground contact patch, not a tuning defect to be eliminated — the mechanism is described as unavoidable even in a dedicated 2022 review, so design in a correction rather than expecting to remove the need for one. [D5-S01, section 6.1]
2. When the correction needs to extend beyond a single fitted multiplier, choose the family that matches available effort and data: a terramechanics/multibody model (grouser geometry, soil shear parameters) when physical parameters are known and offline computation is acceptable [D5-S09, abstract]; an online estimator (EKF or sliding-mode observer) when conditions vary during operation and only trajectory/position measurements are available, noting that a sliding-mode observer is reported to give higher accuracy and better noise robustness than an EKF on the same estimation task [D5-S11, "Conclusions"]; or a combined kinematic-dynamic model when large lateral acceleration or heavy load pushes a pure kinematic model outside its reported comfort zone of "medium-low-speed and low-curvature conditions." [D5-S04, section 4.2.2]
3. Validate turning performance specifically, not only straight-line tracking: one adaptive controller, before its slip observer's estimates were compensated into it, still reported "a large trajectory tracking control error during the turning of the tracked robot" relative to a plain backstepping baseline — but the same paper's full method, with the observer's estimates compensated in, reported the opposite result specifically on turns. The useful lesson is to test the complete, fully-compensated design on turning paths rather than assuming straight-line results generalize, not that an estimator is inherently unable to fix turning error. [D5-S12, "6 Conclusions"]
4. Check track tension on a fixed schedule (an OEM compact-track-loader guide recommends checking sag "on a weekly basis or as needed") rather than only when a problem appears, because tension drifts over time "even with proper care and maintenance" and both too loose (de-tracking) and too tight (accelerated component wear) are documented failure directions. [D5-S21, p. 9, p. 10]
5. Do not assume a single "more tension is better" or "less tension is better" rule: a soil-bin study found the tension that maximizes tractive efficiency depends on soil type — tractive efficiency fell monotonically with tension in loam but peaked at about 1.75 kN before falling in sandy loam — so a tension target should be set per operating surface, not as one fixed number. [D5-S05, p. 237]
6. If adding tension sensing, treat it as a maintenance/alerting signal (the one production patent found senses tensioner hydraulic pressure and drives an operator alarm) rather than a steering-control input — no source in this search reports tension feeding a real-time kinematic-correction loop, so that remains to be developed and validated, not assumed safe to wire in directly. [D5-S08, col. 3]
7. When integrating a tracked platform into a ROS/ROS 2 stack, plan the command interface as an ordinary two-value (linear, angular) differential-drive "unicycle" converted to left/right track speeds, matching every documented tracked ROS integration found (a 1450 kg tracked UGV and a GVR-Bot-class research platform alike); do not expect or build for a tracked-specific `motion_model` in Nav2, since none exists for either wheeled or tracked skid-steer bases. [D5-S17, p. 117580S-2] [D5-S15, section IV-A] [D5-S23] [D5-S24]
8. Before increasing motor power to fix a tracked platform's top-speed shortfall, separately verify track retention margin under the higher power — one documented case raised top speed by fitting larger motors but also made the tracks "came off much more frequently under the higher motor power," so the two problems should be budgeted and tested together, not fixed one at a time. [D5-S18, p. 3]
9. Limit sustained hard-surface, high-speed transport ("roading") of a rubber-tracked vehicle to a small fraction of total operating time — OEM guidance caps recommended daily hard-surface use at 10% and states that "continuous roading and aggressive turning on hard surfaces will shorten track wear life" — if a platform's test or transport routine is mostly pavement, budget for shorter track life than the manufacturer's soft-terrain duty-cycle figures assume. [D5-S21, p. 12]
10. When inspecting a rubber track, check for packed mud/debris in the undercarriage clearance as its own item, separate from a tension/sag check — a manufacturer patent's background notes that even track/idler/sprocket geometry designed to self-clear debris during operation still leaves "manual track and/or machine cleaning...often necessary" in practice. [D5-S26, col. 1]
11. If a platform must operate in cold conditions, check the track manufacturer's rubber-compound brittle-point rating rather than assuming "rubber track" is a single cold-weather category — one manufacturer's own comparison testing showed a roughly 38°C brittle-point difference between two track-tread compounds. [D5-S25, Table 2]

## Common mistakes

- Treating looseness as the only track-tension failure mode: an OEM guide states excessive tightness "can accelerate component wear" just as looseness "can cause de-tracking" — both directions are failure modes, not only the under-tension one. [D5-S21, p. 9]
- Using a hard-surface/high-speed "roading" duty cycle as the default test or transport mode for a rubber-tracked vehicle: OEM guidance states rubber tracks are "not designed or recommended for... extended periods of high-speed roading" and caps recommended daily hard-surface use at 10%, because "continuous roading and aggressive turning on hard surfaces will shorten track wear life." [D5-S21, p. 12]
- Pushing more motor power through a tracked drivetrain to fix a top-speed shortfall without separately addressing track retention: one team's attempt to fix an under-speed tracked platform by fitting larger motors did raise top speed, but also made the "tank tracks came off much more frequently under the higher motor power" — the two problems (power delivery and track retention) are coupled, not independent. [D5-S18, p. 3]
- Judging an online slip/ICR estimator design from a comparison that does not yet include the estimator: one adaptive-backstepping controller, tested without its own slip observer's estimates compensated in, still showed "a large trajectory tracking control error during the turning of the tracked robot" — but the same paper's full, observer-compensated method is reported to correct turning accuracy specifically. The mistake would be to read the first (uncompensated) result as "estimators can't fix turning error" rather than checking the fully-compensated result. [D5-S12, "6 Conclusions"]
- Treating a dedicated tracked-vehicle ROS 2 integration as if it will expose or require a tracked-specific kinematic/motion model: the one large-scale example found still represents the vehicle to its planner and even its physics simulator as an ordinary two-wheel differential-drive unicycle. [D5-S17, p. 117580S-12]
- Treating tension and tread wear as the only rubber-track failure modes: a manufacturer patent's background section separately documents soft-debris (mud) packing and hard-debris (rock) intrusion into undercarriage components as distinct, long-recognised failure/wear mechanisms that track/idler/sprocket shaping only partly mitigates — manual cleaning "is still often necessary" even with those mitigations in place. [D5-S26, col. 1]
- Assuming a rubber track's low-temperature performance is a fixed property of "rubber tracks" as a category rather than a formulation choice: brittle point varied by up to about 38°C between two track-tread rubber compounds compared in the same patent, so cold-climate suitability depends on which specific rubber compound is used. [D5-S25, Table 2]

## Disagreements between sources

- **Does tension's effect on tractive efficiency rise or fall with more tension?** The soil-bin rice-combine study found the relationship **depends on soil type**: efficiency fell monotonically with tension in loam, but rose to a peak (around 1.75 kN) and then fell in sandy loam [D5-S05, p. 237]. No single, soil-independent direction is reported — stated here as a within-source, condition-dependent result rather than a disagreement between two different sources, since no other downloaded source gives a competing direction for the same quantity.
- **Is the correction burden something to route around at the software layer, or something to reduce at the model layer?** The field-ready Milrem THeMIS integration deliberately absorbs the tracked-vehicle mismatch inside an ordinary differential-drive software abstraction, treating the physical mismatch as a tolerable approximation error for a large, high-traction vehicle [D5-S17]. The terramechanics/online-estimator literature (D5-S09, D5-S10, D5-S11, D5-S12) instead works to model or estimate the mismatch explicitly. Both appear in the literature with no source stating the other approach is wrong; they read as different design points (approximate-and-tolerate vs. model-and-correct) rather than a resolved disagreement.

## Open questions

- No source found in this search uses a real-time track-tension measurement or estimate as a live input to a steering/kinematic-correction control loop; every tension method found is positioned for maintenance alerting (D5-S08) or offline/simulation analysis (D5-S05, D5-S06, D5-S07). Whether tension-informed real-time correction has been attempted anywhere is not answered by the sources gathered here.
- No source found gives a controlled, like-for-like precision/repeatability comparison between molded-continuous-belt tracks and segmented/linked tracks at a small/mid-size robot scale; the material/construction-choice question in subtopic 4(c) is not answered.
- No source found states numerically how much of the tracked turning-correction burden specifically (isolated from general mechanical reliability, traction or speed) was a deciding factor in any team's or project's documented switch away from tracks — Finding 9 shows mechanical/traction/speed reasons stated explicitly, but no source isolates the kinematic-correction-tuning burden as its own named reason.
- Published, named-author cost figures for the GVR-Bot or the Milrem THeMIS were not found in this search (both are government-/program-owned platforms rather than commercially listed products); subtopic 4(d)'s cost question is answered only for the bolt-on drive-module and bare-track products.
- The 2026 slip-ratio-control review's own stated future-work gap — robustness of slip-prediction models and more adaptive, real-time control strategies, especially combining data-driven and physical models — is reported here as the field's own assessment of what remains open, not resolved by any source in this search. [D5-S04, "Conclusions"]
- A 2025/2026 Politecnico di Milano master's thesis (A. T. Z. A. Farag, supervisors G. Ferretti and L. Bascetta, "Slip estimation in skid-steered vehicles") is confirmed via its institutional-repository metadata record to propose exactly the kind of two-stage GNSS/IMU sensor-fusion-plus-sliding-mode-observer framework this topic asks about, validated on an AGILEX BUNKER PRO tracked skid-steer robot with a lower reported RMSE than benchmarked state-of-the-art methods — but its full text is embargoed until 2026-11-10 and requires institutional login, so its detailed methodology and numeric results could not be verified or cited beyond this metadata-level description (see Sources, "Not downloaded" note).
- No checklist-passing source was found giving a track service-life figure (hours or distance to replacement) specific to a small or mid-size robot scale, as opposed to the compact-track-loader figures already cited from D5-S21; only non-citable trade-forum/blog figures and a DTIC report blocked behind a 403 in this environment were found.
