# C4 — Controller–vehicle interface

## Summary
- MPPI assumes the command it sends is only the *mean* of what the vehicle really receives (the commanded input "has to pass through a lower level of control before reaching the actual system", so "the noise enters the system through the control input"), simulates thousands of perturbed command sequences through a motion model, and blends them by cost; the first command is sent and the rest is shifted forward to warm-start the next cycle. [C4-S01, pp. 2, 8–9]
- Every predictive tracker is only as good as its internal model: MPPI on a real car failed in exactly the direction where its learned model was wrong (predicted over-steer, got under-steer) [C4-S01, pp. 17–18], and Autoware's MPC documentation says late turn-in is "often due to incorrect delay time and time constant in the steering model". [C4-S38, "Other tips for tuning"]
- The response of many powertrains "can be modeled adequately by a time delay and a first-order transient response", although "most WMR motion models in related work omit powertrain dynamics"; one skid-steer vehicle was identified at time constant 0.81 s and delay 0.16 s, and including such powertrain models "were instrumental in removing large prediction errors associated with transients". [C4-S17, pp. 20–21]
- Delay compensation works by predicting the state forward over the delay, by adding the actuator state to the model, or by replaying already-sent commands during the delay window; on a vehicle with 600 ms steering delay (a four-wheel-steered vehicle with hydraulic steering), Nav2 MPPI oscillated without compensation and tracked "visibly better" with it. [C4-S16, pp. 1–2; C4-S15, p. 14; C4-S30, "Per-Axis Delay Compensation"; C4-S37, PR #6154 description]
- On skid-steer and tracked vehicles, slip makes body motion depend on terrain: a heavy skid-steer robot rotated more per command on snow than on concrete [C4-S20, p. 6], and field controllers handle this by estimating traction/slip parameters online [C4-S12, pp. 5–6] or by learning the model error from experience. [C4-S11, pp. 1, 13]
- A constant command-to-motion gain error (the vehicle delivering a fixed fraction of the commanded speed or turn rate) leaves a steady tracking offset in a predictive controller unless the controller estimates it: MPC theory removes such offset with an integrating disturbance estimate [C4-S05, pp. 52–53]; on a 900 kg ground robot whose turn-rate commands were artificially halved, a fixed-model MPC kept a large lateral error while an online-adapted model "adapts quickly" and cut the error [C4-S50, pp. 2186–2187]; an adaptive inner loop let simulated MPPI survive a 40% loss of actuator power that made plain MPPI fail. [C4-S51, p. 5]
- How the controller's starting velocity is set matters: Nav2 MPPI on Humble seeds every rollout from the latest measured odometry twist, while later releases add acceleration limits in the rollout, forward prediction of the measured state, an "open loop" option (use the last command instead of odometry) and per-axis delay compensation. [C4-S26, optimizer.cpp `updateInitialStateVelocities`; C4-S28, optimizer.cpp `prepare`; C4-S29, `open_loop`, `model_delay_vx`]

## Findings

### 1. MPPI formulation and its assumptions about the vehicle
- The original MPPI derivation (path-integral control) assumes dynamics that are "affine in control and subject to an affine brownian disturbance", dx = f dt + G u dt + B dw. [C4-S49, p. 2]
- It also requires a fixed link between noise and control cost, which "limits the noise in the system to only effect state variables that are directly actuated (i.e. the noise is control dependent)"; the authors state the description does not hold "if there are known strong disturbances on indirectly actuated state variables or if the dynamics are only partially known". [C4-S49, p. 2]
- In the special case used for all its experiments, the noise term is interpreted "as a random change in the control input", so the update is a cost-weighted average of random control perturbations. [C4-S49, p. 5]
- The earlier path-integral methods sampled with the system's natural noise; MPPI's importance-sampling step lets the designer change both the mean and the variance of the sampling distribution, because "the natural variance of the system is typically too low to achieve good performance". [C4-S49, pp. 3, 8]
- As an MPC scheme it needs "rapid convergence to a good control input" and "the ability to sample a large number of trajectories in real-time" (GPU); the next cycle is warm-started with the un-executed part of the previous sequence. [C4-S49, p. 6]
- All results in that paper are simulations (cart-pole, a miniature race car with a non-linear tyre model, a quadrotor) with the controller at 50 Hz; the paper reports no test in which the control model differed from the simulated system. [C4-S49, pp. 6–8]
- MPPI (IT-MPC) models the vehicle as a general, usually non-linear, discrete-time state-transition function x(t+1) = F(x(t), v(t)); the controller does not set the input v directly but only its mean u, with v ~ N(u, Σ). The authors call this "a reasonable noise assumption for many robotic systems where the commanded input has to pass through a lower level of control before reaching the actual system." [C4-S01, p. 2]
- Unlike the earlier path-integral derivation, the information-theoretic derivation does not require dynamics that are affine (linear) in the control input, so arbitrary non-linear or learned models can be used. [C4-S01, p. 9; C4-S03, p. 1]
- Algorithm: take the current state from an external state estimator, sample K perturbed control sequences, simulate each through the model, compute costs, turn costs into weights with an exponential of cost divided by the "temperature" λ (lower λ → weight concentrates on the best samples; higher λ → closer to a plain average), average, smooth with a Savitzky–Golay filter, send the first control, and shift the remaining T−1 controls forward to warm-start the next cycle. [C4-S01, pp. 6–8]
- Control limits are handled by pushing a clamping function into the dynamics, x(t+1) = F(x(t), g(v(t))), instead of rejecting samples or solving a constrained quadratic program; the authors report this "works well in practice". [C4-S01, p. 7]
- Sampled controls produce chattering, which is removed by smoothing the output sequence (Savitzky–Golay filter). [C4-S01, p. 7]
- The warm start (sampling around last cycle's un-executed sequence) is "key to achieving a high level of performance", but a strong disturbance can push all samples into regions from which recovery is impossible; the fix used is to keep a small fraction of samples that are pure noise around zero. [C4-S01, p. 8]
- Tuning quantities used on the 1:5-scale AutoRally car: 40 Hz control frequency, 2 s horizon, λ = 12.5, control-cost γ = 0.1, Σ = Diag(0.0306, 0.0506); a few thousand samples of 2–3 s trajectories gave 40–60 Hz loops on a GPU. [C4-S01, pp. 7, 13]
- The authors state that sampling methods suit vehicles because their dynamics are "highly non-linear, but not unstable"; "highly unstable systems are difficult to sample useful trajectories from". [C4-S01, p. 11]
- Accurate state feedback (pose and velocity) is stated as a requirement: "Model predictive control algorithms require accurate state feedback in order to operate"; AutoRally used a GPS+IMU factor-graph estimator at 10 Hz, interpolated to 200 Hz with IMU integration. [C4-S01, pp. 11–12]
- Because commands are built from random samples, MPPI "may encounter chattering when implemented on an actual platform"; rapid command changes "burden the actuators and cause the system to become unstable". [C4-S46, p. 1]
- Smoothing the output with an external filter (e.g. Savitzky–Golay) is decoupled from the optimisation: filtering the noise can push commands outside the actuator limits, and filtering the control sequence "can cause a delay in the system response" because the filter uses past values. [C4-S46, p. 3]
- Smooth MPPI (SMPPI) instead samples the *rate of change* of the command and integrates it, with the sampling spread set from "the physical limit of the input rate of change", and adds a cost on command variation; simply lowering MPPI's sampling spread also smooths commands but makes the controller "unable to respond to rapidly changing environments". [C4-S46, pp. 3–4]

### 2. Other path trackers and what they assume (baselines)
- Pure pursuit steers along the circular arc that joins the vehicle to a goal point one lookahead distance ahead on the path; the lookahead is its one parameter. [C4-S06, pp. 10, 14]
- Longer lookahead converges to the path "more gradually and with less oscillation" (acting like damping), but the longer it is, "the less 'curvy' of a path that can be followed". [C4-S06, p. 14]
- Pure pursuit is a proportional controller on cross-track error at the lookahead point with gain 2/ℓd²; the lookahead is commonly scaled with speed and saturated at a minimum and maximum. [C4-S14, PDF p. 17]
- Decreasing lookahead gives more precise tracking "and eventually oscillation"; increasing it gives less precise tracking "and eventually stability", with corner cutting in turns and steady-state error in curves growing with speed. [C4-S14, PDF pp. 18, 20, 73]
- Pure pursuit on a straight path at constant speed is stable, has "a small steady state tracking error" on constant non-zero curvature, and is undefined when the vehicle is farther from the path than the lookahead. [C4-S08, p. 18]
- Pure pursuit and its variants "account for [no] dynamic effects of the vehicle"; they are geometrically derived and apply to differential-drive and Ackermann robots through a forward velocity and turn rate. [C4-S07, pp. 3–4]
- Regulated Pure Pursuit (RPP) adds a speed-scaled lookahead, slows linear speed in sharp curvature and near obstacles, and computes angular velocity from the regulated (not the desired) linear velocity "which prevents consequential undershoot". [C4-S07, pp. 4–5]
- In simulation (TurtleBot 3, ground-truth pose), mean tracking error on a step path was 0.03 m for RPP versus 0.10 m for Adaptive PP and 0.19 m for PP. [C4-S07, p. 7]
- Dynamic Window Pure Pursuit (DWPP) argues that ordinary PP variants either clip commands in an extra layer or let the robot clip them, which "can result in overshoot from the reference path owing to the inability to fully realize the planned velocity"; DWPP instead picks the command inside the velocity/acceleration-feasible window closest to the line ω = κv. [C4-S41, pp. 4, 10]
- The survey's comparison table lists pure pursuit and other kinematic trackers as locally exponentially stable under their assumptions, linear MPC with stability that "depends on horizon length", and non-linear MPC as "Not guaranteed" but "Works well in practice". [C4-S08, p. 18]
- In a CarSim comparison of trackers (pure pursuit, Stanley, kinematic, LQR, preview), geometric trackers achieved most of the tracking performance of the others "until speeds are significantly increased"; LQR with a feedforward term reduced steady-state error in curves but overshot on rapid curvature changes. [C4-S14, PDF pp. 73–74]
- DARPA Urban Challenge winner Boss used MPC whose optimisation contained only "a very simple kinematic model of the vehicle, a time delay and rate limits on steering". [C4-S14, PDF p. 8]
- For the Stanley tracker (a geometric tracker that corrects the cross-track error measured at the front axle), delays in "signal processing and steering angle control" reduce precision and stability; a pragmatic fix reads the path curvature for the feedforward term at a point the vehicle will reach after a feedforward time t_ff, instead of at the closest point. [C4-S44, pp. 615, 634]
- On a real test car this cut root-mean-square cross-track error on a dynamic circuit from 0.106 m to 0.033 m and maximum error from 0.262 m to 0.084 m. [C4-S44, p. 630, Table 4]

### 3. Motion models used inside controllers
- Kinematic models "are suitable for planning paths at low speeds (e.g. parking maneuvers and urban driving) where inertial effects are small". [C4-S08, p. 5]
- A simple kinematic model "can suffice when a vehicle closely exhibits kinematic motion, such as driving slowly or performing parking maneuvers"; unmodelled dynamics otherwise force high gains and loss of tracking accuracy. [C4-S14, PDF pp. 72, 74]
- A common simplification in path-following design is to assume the inner (velocity/heading) loops respond fast enough to be ignored and design on the kinematic model only; one review treats the inner-loop effect as an internal disturbance and analyses robustness to it. [C4-S23, p. 3]
- AutoRally MPPI used a 7-state model (position, heading, roll, longitudinal and lateral velocity, yaw rate) split into a trivial kinematic part and a learned dynamic part (either a regression on 25 physics-derived basis functions or a 2×32 tanh neural network). [C4-S01, pp. 12–13, 18]
- The neural network beat the basis-function model on validation (R² 0.78 vs 0.68), and "both models suffer from significant inaccuracies which reflect the effect of hidden variables, like track condition and battery voltage". [C4-S01, p. 13]
- An "enhanced" kinematic model that adds a slip prediction matched a full dynamic model's prediction accuracy on a skid-steer vehicle, while the dynamic model took 4.5–5.5× the computation of a plain kinematic model and the enhanced kinematic model only 1.3×. [C4-S18, p. 7]
- Higher-fidelity vehicle models predict motion better but their parameters "can be significantly more difficult to estimate, and computationally expensive to compute". [C4-S24, p. 21]
- Sampling 1200 trajectories of 2.5 s at 40 Hz means about 4.8 million model evaluations per second, which the authors say was only possible on a GPU for their model. [C4-S01, p. 7]
- A learned-disturbance approach keeps a fixed simple model (unicycle) and learns the difference between it and reality as a Gaussian process (GP, a non-parametric regression that also gives uncertainty) of state, input and past states/inputs; the query includes the previous velocity and previous input as well as the current state and input, because historic states are needed to capture unmodelled (higher-order) dynamics. [C4-S11, pp. 1, 5–6]
- In automotive racing MPC, a simple nominal vehicle model is kept and a Gaussian process learns the residual (the part of the measured motion the nominal model does not explain) online from a small dictionary of 300 data points. [C4-S45, p. 1]
- As driving became more aggressive the nominal model's error grew by almost 40% while the error with the learned correction stayed "virtually constant"; lap time improved by almost 10% (≈20.2 s → ≈18.3 s). [C4-S45, p. 7]
- A learned correction from a previous session gave about 20% larger prediction error than one learned during the run, "demonstrating the need for a learning procedure during operation". [C4-S45, p. 7]
- The Nav2 MPPI maintainer described the original motion model as "just a pass through" (the sampled command is taken as the achieved velocity) and named `predict` as "exactly the place for the dynamics model to translate control requests into the vehicle's actual responses"; for low-speed (sub-10 m/s) AMRs he asserted that traditional dynamics models are good enough, with learned (DNN) models reserved for skidding or very high speeds. [C4-S47, maintainer comments 2023-12-06 and 2024-01-12]

### 4. Identifying the command-to-motion response
- AutoRally's system-identification data were ~30 minutes of human driving in five choreographed manoeuvres (slow driving, zig-zags, full-throttle/full-brake runs, slides, fast laps), each 3 min per direction. [C4-S01, p. 12; C4-S03, p. 6]
- AutoRally models were trained on one-step prediction error even though "minimizing multi-step prediction error is technically the correct objective", because multi-step is non-convex; without regularisation the fitted model gave unstable forward simulations. [C4-S01, p. 12]
- Training on one-step error "does not necessarily result in accurate multi-step prediction"; compounding multi-step errors can in the worst case grow exponentially. [C4-S03, p. 6]
- Model asymmetry in training data showed up in control: the human pilot drove slightly differently in each direction, and the learned model was accurate counter-clockwise but predicted over-steer clockwise when the car under-steered. [C4-S01, p. 17]
- Integrated prediction error minimisation (IPEM) calibrates the integrated model (the prediction over a horizon) instead of one-step derivatives; it needs only low-rate state observations and gives parameters that "optimize simulation accuracy for the chosen time horizon". [C4-S17, pp. 1, 4]
- Many powertrains can be modelled "by a time delay and a first-order transient response": ω̇ = (ω̃(t − τd) − ω)/τc, with τd and τc estimated online by an extended Kalman filter from encoder data. [C4-S17, p. 20]
- The DRIVE protocol (skid-steer robots, 75–470 kg, six terrains, 14.7 km) first finds the vehicle's true reachable command space, sets speed/acceleration limits, then sends uniformly random wheel-speed commands; each command is held 6 s (one transient 2 s window, two steady 2 s windows) so that both transient and steady-state slip can be identified. [C4-S21, pp. 1–2, 6–7]
- DRIVE's "unloaded calibration": lift the vehicle, command maximum speed, read maximum wheel speed from encoders, compute the reachable angular speed, and verify that commanded speed equals encoder speed across the command space before field runs. [C4-S21, pp. 15–16]
- Earlier protocols (steady forward speed with slowly increasing turn rate) sample only small accelerations and cover only a small part of the command space. [C4-S21, pp. 2–3]
- For a steering actuator, one study set the command to 1, recorded the response until it settled, and fitted a first-order model (Kδ = 30 Hz for a simulated Prius). [C4-S16, p. 5]
- Because MPPI evaluates rapidly changing sampled commands, one study included driving "with rapidly changing commands for random movements, representing the sampled noisy trajectories of MPPI" in its identification data, in both turning directions and on several friction levels. [C4-S46, p. 5]
- An online-learned correction needed an initial set of 250 data points (slightly less than two laps) collected with the nominal controller before it could be switched on reliably. [C4-S45, pp. 6–7]
- Validation practice: compare the model's multi-step prediction with measured trajectories (AutoRally: model output run with recorded inputs from the same initial state [C4-S01, p. 17]; Baril: relative translational and angular error per metre on held-out segments [C4-S20, p. 6]).

### 5. Matching controller limits to what the chassis delivers
- DWPP's velocity and acceleration constraints "were determined experimentally" for the test robot. [C4-S41, p. 16]
- When a pure-pursuit-type controller ignores acceleration limits, the gap between commanded and actual velocity causes overshoot; with limits built into the command computation, DWPP produced no constraint violations and the smallest mean and maximum cross-track error (errors decreasing in the order PP, APP, RPP, DWPP) over five runs on three corner paths on a real robot, at the cost of longer travel time. [C4-S41, pp. 3, 21]
- Nav2's velocity smoother clips controller output by its own limits, so its maximum velocity/acceleration must be "consistent with, or greater than" the controller's. [C4-S30, "Add Dynamic Window Pure Pursuit Option"]
- Clearpath's Nav2 demo configs set the controller's acceleration limits equal to the velocity smoother's (Husky: 0.5 m/s² and 0.5 rad/s² in both; Jackal: 10.0 in both), and use the filtered odometry topic. [C4-S40, a200 and j100 nav2.yaml]
- In MPPI the prediction horizon (time_steps × model_dt) times maximum speed must fit inside the local costmap, or the robot "will be artificially limited in its maximum speeds"; e.g. 3 s at 0.5 m/s needs at least a 1.5 m costmap radius. [C4-S25, "Prediction Horizon, Costmap Sizing, and Offsets"]
- `model_dt` should be set to the control period (0.05 s at 20 Hz) and may be lower "but not larger". [C4-S25, "Prediction Horizon…"; C4-S27, same section]
- Faster robots need larger velocity sampling standard deviations to explore the velocity space; if the robot does not reach full speed, increase the std; if angular velocity chatters, reduce it. [C4-S27, "Prediction Horizon…" and tuning notes]
- DRIVE lesson: random high-acceleration commands caused premature wear (skipping gears, 30° wheel bending on asphalt, tyre tread 12 → 2 mm) on a 470 kg skid-steer at 4 m/s²; acceleration limits should be set per terrain or for the worst terrain. [C4-S21, p. 15]

### 6. Command delay, actuator lag and velocity under-delivery
- Three delay types are distinguished: computation delay, actuator command-processing delay, and actuator dynamics (lag); "most commonly used control algorithms assume the planned command to be applied instantaneously". [C4-S16, p. 1]
- Compensation methods reported: shift the initial state forward by one control step (computation delay); model actuator dynamics as a first-order ODE and add the actuator state to the model (lag); transform sensor values to the current time (sensor delay). [C4-S16, pp. 1–2]
- In simulation, a controller without delay compensation overshot the first turn and then collided with an obstacle while returning to the path; with compensation the path was "closer to the reference line and smoother". [C4-S16, pp. 5–6]
- Racing MPC at 50 Hz sends each new input at the end of the sampling period and compensates this fixed delay by simulating the non-linear model forward over that interval before optimising. [C4-S15, p. 14]
- On a four-wheel-steered vehicle with hydraulic steering, MPPI without delay compensation oscillated after each serpentine turn; with it, tracking was much better but the vehicle began to steer slightly early and cut corners. [C4-S37, PR #6154 description]
- The Nav2 maintainer questioned whether the delay parameter was needed or "another way to mask the real issue" of poor low-acceleration handling, but planned to merge it as a useful feature. [C4-S36, maintainer comment 2026-04-07]
- An identified skid-steer hydraulic powertrain had time constant 0.81 s and delay 0.16 s. [C4-S17, p. 21, Fig. 5]
- In road vehicles, delays in the lateral loop are split into input delay (sensor reading, communication, computation) and steering lag (low-level controller, actuator communication and hardware limits); reported values are 0.1 s input delay and 0.2 s steering delay, with other studies finding 0.2–0.5 s steering delay. [C4-S44, p. 616]
- The best feedforward time found experimentally (0.18 s in simulation, 0.20 s on the real car) matched the vehicle's summed delays (≈0.01 s localisation rate, 0.1 s steering actuation, ≈0.05 s yaw response; the remaining ≈0.04 s the authors attribute to communication and other effects not analysed). [C4-S44, p. 628]
- Delay-induced deviation grows with speed: in simulation, after a curvature step the maximum deviation was 1.21 m at 8 m/s and 0.12 m at 3 m/s for plain Stanley, "since the vehicle with increasing velocity drives straight a longer distance during the delay time". [C4-S44, pp. 628, 630]
- Feedback gains that worked in simulation (k = 3.0 s⁻¹) had to be lowered on the real car (0.8 s⁻¹); the authors attribute this partly to filtering the yaw-rate signal, which delayed it, and to a steering breakaway torque that delays small corrections and amplifies oscillation. [C4-S44, p. 627]
- Persistent offset (a constant model error, such as a vehicle that reaches only part of the commanded speed) is removed in MPC theory by adding an integrating disturbance state to the model and estimating it with a Kalman filter ("offset-free MPC"). [C4-S05, Sec. 1.5, pp. 50–51 (PDF pp. 100–101)]
- The offset-free result (Lemma 1.10) needs as many integrating disturbances as measurements, a stable closed loop and inactive constraints at steady state; it "does not require that the plant output be generated by the model", i.e. it holds for any plant–model mismatch that leaves the loop stable. [C4-S05, Sec. 1.5.2, pp. 52–53 (PDF pp. 102–103)]
- In a constrained system the guarantee is weaker: "either there is zero steady offset, or the system trajectory is unbounded, or the system constraints are active at steady state". [C4-S05, p. 49 (PDF p. 99)] (Our reading, not stated in the source: a vehicle that cannot reach the commanded speed because an input limit is active is not covered by the zero-offset guarantee.)
- A tutorial review states that when "permanent, nonzero mean, disturbances are present and/or there is mismatch between the MPC model and the actual plant", a plain MPC does not track without offset; offset-free designs augment the model with disturbances and estimate them with an observer. [C4-S52, p. 1]
- In its linear two-input example with persistent unmeasured disturbances, the standard MPC kept a steady offset, the two offset-free variants removed it, and closed-loop cost was 1.356 (standard) versus 1.017 and 0.934 (offset-free), i.e. the standard MPC was 45.2% worse than the best variant. [C4-S52, pp. 5–6]
- The two offset-free variants tracked equally well but differed in input smoothness: the one with disturbance-observer poles at the origin gave rougher inputs than the one with poles at 0.5, "less sensitive to measurement noise"; i.e., faster disturbance estimation (poles at the origin) gave rougher inputs for the same offset removal. [C4-S52, pp. 5–6]
- Stability of offset-free MPC under mismatch had long been assumed rather than proven; a 2025 technical report gives nonlinear stability results that extend only to "sufficiently small plant-model mismatch". [C4-S53, pp. 1–2]

#### Constant gain mismatch in sampling-based and field MPC
- MPPI "often lacks robustness, especially when the simulated dynamics are different from the true dynamics": the sequence is optimised for the nominal model but applied to the real one, so the next initial state differs from the plan and the warm-started controls can be "far from optimal or in the worst case detrimental". [C4-S51, p. 1]
- In simulated multirotor racing (15 races per case), plain MPPI failed to complete all runs (crash or divergence) when motor thrust control power was reduced by 40%, when mass rose by 50%, or under a constant pitching moment; adding an L1 adaptive inner-loop controller, which makes the vehicle behave like the nominal model MPPI simulates, completed all runs in every case and reduced lap time. [C4-S51, p. 5, Table II and Fig. 6]
- A vehicle-tracking MPPI study (simulation, preprint) found that a persistent unmodelled disturbance "induces a persistent steady-state offset in the nominal MPPI" on every test path; adding a learned residual model to the MPPI rollouts cut lateral (Y) RMSE by up to 69%, while yaw RMSE rose slightly. The disturbance was additive and sinusoidal, not a command gain. [C4-S54, pp. 1, 4]
- On a 900 kg skid-steer ground robot, turn-rate commands were artificially multiplied by 0.5 at the start of the second lap of a circular course ("similar to the vehicle getting a flat tyre or losing power in one motor"), at 2 m/s desired speed over eight repeats; the controller with a fixed model "incurs a large lateral error because the model is no longer accurate". [C4-S50, p. 2186]
- With fast adaptation (a weighted Bayesian linear regression refitted each step on the last 3 s, 30 samples, of data) the controller had a large error at the moment of the change "but adapts quickly"; combining it with learning from previous laps gave the lowest error and fastest convergence. [C4-S50, pp. 2185–2187]
- Read from the paper's Fig. 6 (approximate): after the change, median lateral error per run stayed around 0.8–0.95 m without learning, versus roughly 0.15–0.25 m with fast adaptation and 0.1–0.25 m with fast plus long-term learning. [C4-S50, p. 2187, Fig. 6]
- The model structure matters: the learned part of the speed and turn-rate dynamics is linear in the commanded and current velocity ([v_cmd, v]w), so a gain between command and response is one of the fitted parameters; the method "requires the unknown part of the dynamics to be linear in a set of model parameters". [C4-S50, pp. 2180, 2185]
- The same study tested "Loaded & Understeer" (turn-rate commands ×0.7) and "Loaded & Oversteer" (×1.2) configurations; rotational dynamics differed most between configurations, and a GP-based model became over-confident in the ×1.2 case while the linear-regression model stayed calibrated. [C4-S50, p. 2186, Fig. 4]
- For comparison, the tracked-robot traction factor µ (effective speed = µ × commanded) in [C4-S12] is the same kind of gain, estimated online by MHE (see section 8). [C4-S12, pp. 5–7]
- In path following, constant disturbances are removed either by an integral term (simple, but only for straight lines in the cited analysis) or by estimating the disturbance (works for any path). [C4-S23, p. 23]
- Pure pursuit tuned at constant speed can absorb the vehicle's dynamic side slip by raising the lookahead gain until the computed arc is tighter than the path, cancelling the effect; this tunes the tracker to a specific course. [C4-S14, PDF pp. 19, 73]
- The two largest disturbances found on the field robots in the learning-MPC study were "the unmodeled dynamics of the robot (including actuators) and the wheel-terrain interactions". [C4-S11, p. 3]

### 7. Feeding measured velocity and odometry back into the controller
- Two feedback modes exist in Nav2 components: "open loop" (use the last command as the current velocity; good "when acceleration limits are set appropriately") and "closed loop" (use odometry; "it is important that the odometry is high rate and low latency"). [C4-S31, `feedback`]
- The Nav2 maintainer states that if "the acceleration limits are accurately set from empirical data and your robot has a professional quality response time", open loop could be better "only if the odometry from the robot is delayed and/or inaccurate (but the robot did actually execute the command accurately and timely)"; otherwise the modes are largely the same; DWB and MPPI use the current speed to limit the next trajectory, while RPP uses speed only for adaptive lookahead and rotate-to-heading. [C4-S35, comment 2025-09-15]
- The DWPP author notes that in closed loop, if the previous command is not yet reflected in odometry when the next command is computed, proper acceleration may not be achieved, and "the higher the publish frequency of the velocity command, the more difficult". [C4-S35, comment 2025-09-18]
- A contributor reports that with low acceleration limits (0.1 m/s²) and closed-loop feedback, MPPI kept adding small steps to the measured (not-yet-changed) speed so the motor never produced enough torque; open loop let the steps accumulate. [C4-S42, PR #5617 description]
- Nav2 (main branch) closed-loop mode now predicts the state one controller period forward toward the last command, clamps the measured speed to within the acceleration limits of the last command, and predicts the pose forward — "to compensate for the latency between measurement and when this command will take effect". [C4-S28, optimizer.cpp `prepare`]
- Filtering inside the controller adds delay: in Autoware MPC, a lower cutoff of the steering low-pass filter (a second-order Butterworth filter on the output command) gives stronger noise reduction "but it also induce operation delay"; stronger smoothing of the reference-path curvature "can cause delay in feedforward computation". [C4-S38, "Other tips for tuning"]
- In receding-horizon estimation for a tracked robot, a low process-noise weight gives better accuracy "however, it causes time-lag between true and estimated values". [C4-S12, p. 9]
- Autoware notes "frequent reports of performance degradation due to errors in input information" (e.g. speed offset from a tyre-radius difference) and advises cross-checking speed with GNSS and steering with IMU yaw rate. [C4-S38, "Confirmation of the input information"]
- A tracked field robot ran its controller at 5 Hz because its RTK GNSS updated at 5 Hz. [C4-S12, p. 4]
- ros2_controllers `diff_drive_controller` can compute odometry from commands (`open_loop: true`) or feedback, averages velocity over a rolling window (`velocity_rolling_window_size`, default 10), and times out stale commands (`cmd_vel_timeout`, default 0.5 s). [C4-S39, parameter file]
- A contributor (not a maintainer; level D covers maintainer answers only, so this is an unconfirmed user report) with a slow-steering, high-delay robot reported that in closed loop MPPI made "big snake motion around the straight path" and could veer off, which he attributed to odometry lagging behind; on the robot, adding delay compensation reduced but did not remove the oscillation compared with open loop; in a later simulation test, closed loop "together with the delay compensation" had "no oscillations anymore" but got "stuck before the u-turn". [C4-S48, comments 2026-04-07, 2026-04-09 and 2026-04-13]
- The Nav2 maintainer named the two candidate differences between open and closed loop as "(1) delay (which we've modeled) and (2) noise (which we haven't)", and stated his aim that open loop become unnecessary, kept only for robots with no odometry. [C4-S48, maintainer comments 2026-04-07 and 2026-04-13]
- The same contributor (unconfirmed user report) found that a remaining noisy command signal also occurred with ground-truth odometry and no delay in simulation, and disappeared when raw sampled controls were clamped in the motion model — i.e. not every command jitter comes from the feedback. [C4-S48, comment 2026-04-14]

### 8. Slip in skid-steer and tracked path tracking
- Skid-steer turning requires wheel slip because all wheels are aligned with the body; the ICR approach identifies the instantaneous centres of rotation of each tread from experimental data and uses the resulting kinematics both for dead-reckoning and to compute the drive velocities sent for motion control. [C4-S19, pp. 1, 4–5]
- Terrain changes the identified parameters: turning efficiency was lower on asphalt than on concrete "due to greater friction"; an experimental symmetric model for asphalt with 20 psi tyres was α = 0.91, xICR = 0.275 m against the non-slip value 0.2 m. [C4-S19, p. 5]
- On a 590 kg skid-steer robot over 2 km of snow and concrete, the ideal differential-drive model had much higher errors than any calibrated model; above ~30° commanded rotation the robot turned more on snow than on concrete, a non-linear effect "hard to describe with linear kinematic models". [C4-S20, pp. 6–7]
- Angular prediction error was correlated with the command and peaked when one side's wheel speed command was about twice the other's; translational error was not correlated with the command. [C4-S20, p. 7]
- A tracked agricultural robot added two traction parameters (µ for effective speed, κ for effective turn rate; slip = 1 − µ and 1 − κ) to the kinematic model and estimated them online with moving-horizon estimation (MHE, an optimisation over a window of past measurements). [C4-S12, pp. 5–7]
- With online traction estimation the mean Euclidean error to the reference was 0.0423 m versus 0.0514 m with an EKF that did not estimate traction; computation was 0.88 ms mean, 2.85 ms worst case on a Raspberry Pi 3. [C4-S12, pp. 4, 12, 17]
- The EKF did not estimate traction parameters because it cannot enforce their bounds; unconstrained estimates could go below zero and destabilise the controller. [C4-S12, p. 11]
- Learning-based NMPC on skid-steer and Ackermann-steered robots (50–600 kg; the 600 kg robot is Ackermann-steered; 0.35–1.2 m/s, gravel/sand/dirt) started from a plain unicycle model with no terrain knowledge and cut maximum lateral and heading errors by about 75% within a few trials in one experiment and by more than 50% over 20 trials in another. [C4-S11, pp. 1, 10–11, 15]
- Tube-based NMPC with online parameter estimation on a tractor-trailer on bumpy grass gave mean Euclidean errors of 7.95 cm (tractor) and 5.42 cm (trailer) on a straight line. [C4-S22, p. 8]
- Planetary rovers (JPL Rocky 8, six-wheel rocker-bogie): wheel odometry "is not better than 10% of the distance traveled" nominally and worse in high slip, while visual odometry achieved at least 2.5%. [C4-S43, p. 3]
- Slip was detected by comparing the wheel-kinematics motion with the IMU + visual-odometry filter estimate using a Mahalanobis-distance test (χ², 95%, threshold 11.07); when the test fails, the difference is used as the slip vector. [C4-S43, p. 13]
- The slip-compensated follower adds the measured yaw slip rate to a carrot-heading controller and commands a sideways (crab) velocity against measured lateral slip; forward slip is handled by driving longer. [C4-S43, pp. 14–15]
- Its sample period was set by the ~1 Hz visual-odometry rate, and gains were tuned in order (heading gain until no heading oscillation, then yaw-slip gain, then lateral-slip gain until no crabbing oscillation). [C4-S43, p. 16]
- On a 10° slope, mean-square path offset was 0.05 m² with continuous slip compensation versus 0.54 m² without. [C4-S43, p. 18]

### 9. Robustness to model error and disturbances
- Plain MPPI's weak point is its importance-sampling trajectory: "a severe disturbance can change the initial state of the system into a region where the importance sampler is no longer valid". [C4-S04, p. 1]
- Tube-MPPI adds a disturbance-free nominal system and an ancillary feedback controller (iLQG) that keeps the real state near the nominal one; its main failure is that the nominal and real states diverge, and its sampler ignores the feedback controller. [C4-S13, pp. 1–3, 5; C4-S04, pp. 2–3]
- RMPPI chooses the nominal state by optimisation near the real state and bounds free-energy growth; on AutoRally hardware MPPI was reliable only up to a 9 m/s target, and MPPI and Tube-MPPI "perform similarly with the same cost function". RMPPI took a longer racing line and was not faster in lap time. [C4-S04, pp. 7–8]
- AutoRally MPPI needed a time-decaying track-boundary penalty: a fixed impulse penalty "is effective in simulation with perfect dynamics, but fails on the actual system" because disturbances push most samples into the penalty. [C4-S01, p. 13]
- With the learned model, MPPI's only failures at the 11 m/s target were in the direction where "systematic modeling error … caused the vehicle to under-steer around the corners". [C4-S01, p. 18]
- MPC's short horizon and constant re-planning help against learned-model error: final performance with learned models was within 10% of the known-model controller in the simulated cart-pole and quadrotor tasks. [C4-S03, p. 6]
- Delay-aware tube MPC bounds the effect of estimated delays and model uncertainty with a disturbance-invariant set around the nominal trajectory. [C4-S16, pp. 1–3]

### 10. Testing and metrics for controller–vehicle fit
- Metrics used: mean and maximum cross-track (lateral) error [C4-S41, p. 16; C4-S11, p. 13], heading error [C4-S11, p. 13], constraint-violation ratio and travel time [C4-S41, p. 16], lap success rate over ~100 laps per setting [C4-S01, pp. 15–16], mean Euclidean error to the reference measured with RTK GNSS [C4-S12, p. 12], relative translational (%) and angular (°/m) prediction error [C4-S20, p. 6].
- Test paths used: step/corner paths with 45°, 90° and 135° corners [C4-S41, p. 14], a step-function path and a blind 90° corner repeated 10 times [C4-S07, pp. 7–8], serpentines and circles [C4-S37, PR #6154], straight crop rows [C4-S12, p. 17; C4-S22, p. 8], repeated laps and repeated teach-and-repeat trials [C4-S01; C4-S11].
- RPP's path-tracking comparison used simulation with ground-truth pose "to remove the contribution of odometric and localization error". [C4-S07, pp. 6–7]
- Sim-to-real: AutoRally parameters were first set in simulation and then fine-tuned with a small number of real runs [C4-S01, p. 13]; a tracker comparison added steering-actuator rate limits and delays to the standard CarSim model [C4-S14, PDF p. 10]; delay-compensation results in [C4-S16] are simulation-only (Gazebo). [C4-S16, p. 5]
- The Nav2 maintainer suggests measuring feedback latency by plotting the simulated "actual" speed against the speed given to the controller. [C4-S35, comment 2025-09-17]

### 11. Product and software specifics

#### Nav2 MPPI — Humble
- Motion models: DiffDrive, Omni, Ackermann (Ackermann adds a minimum turning radius). [C4-S25, feature list; C4-S26, motion_models.hpp]
- The Humble rollout seeds step 0 of every sample with the measured robot speed from odometry and sets each later step's velocity equal to the previous step's sampled command, i.e. the modelled vehicle reaches each command after one `model_dt` with no acceleration limit or lag. [C4-S26, optimizer.cpp `updateInitialStateVelocities`; motion_models.hpp `predict`]
- The Humble parameters include `vx_max`, `vx_min`, `wz_max`, `vx_std`, `wz_std`, `model_dt`, `time_steps`, `batch_size`, `temperature`, `gamma`; there are no `ax_max`/`az_max`/`open_loop`/`model_delay_*` parameters in the Humble source. [C4-S26, optimizer.cpp `getParams`]
- The final control sequence is clipped to the velocity limits after the weighted average (`applyControlSequenceConstraints`). [C4-S26, optimizer.cpp]
- If the controller period equals `model_dt`, control-sequence shifting is on and the sent command is element 1 of the sequence. [C4-S26, optimizer.cpp `setOffset`, `getControlFromSequenceAsTwist`]
- The Humble controller server passes the latest odometry twist message to the controller after dead-banding it with `getThresholdedTwist` (no averaging or age check). [C4-S34, controller_server.cpp line 478; odom_subscriber.hpp `odomCallback`]

#### Nav2 MPPI — later releases
- Acceleration limits `ax_max`, `ax_min`, `ay_max`, `az_max` were added in PR #4352 (Iron→Jazzy migration guide) so trajectories respect acceleration constraints. [C4-S30, "MPPI Controller: Addition of acceleration constraints"]
- In the rollout, each step's velocity is limited to within `model_dt × a_max` of the previous step; `clamp_raw_controls` also applies these limits to the raw samples, recommended "when the command output from MPPI is too noisy". [C4-S28, motion_models.hpp `predict`; C4-S29, `clamp_raw_controls`]
- `open_loop` (PR #5617, Kilted→Lyrical guide) uses the last command instead of odometry for the initial state; "useful when using low accelerations or when wheel odometry's latency (motor response is poor) causes issues". [C4-S30; C4-S29, `open_loop`]
- Per-axis delay compensation (`model_delay_vx`, `model_delay_vy`, `model_delay_wz`, PR #6154) fills the first round(delay/model_dt) rollout steps from a ring buffer of already-published commands and shifts the plan forward; it "works best in combination with setting `open_loop: true`"; it is in Lyrical and newer and was not backported. [C4-S30, "Per-Axis Delay Compensation"; C4-S37, maintainer comment 2026-06-08]
- The README advises that odometry publish "at least as fast as your control frequency (ideally much faster) when using low accelerations". [C4-S27, tuning notes]
- The Nav2 tuning guide describes MPPI as similar to DWB but notes it "does have moderately higher compute costs" than DWB. [C4-S33, controller section]
- The maintainer reported "some jitter in the output path" of MPPI and observed that reducing the noise standard deviation gave smoother actions; his impression (stated with low confidence) was that the obstacle critic can add jitter but most remaining noise came from the sampling. [C4-S47, maintainer comments 2023-03-07 and 2023-03-13]

#### Nav2 velocity smoother and RPP
- Velocity smoother defaults: `feedback: OPEN_LOOP`, `max_accel [2.5, 0.0, 3.2]`, `odom_duration 0.1` s (odometry averaging window in closed loop), `velocity_timeout 1.0` s, `deadband_velocity` to avoid commands the motors cannot achieve at stall torque. [C4-S31]
- RPP exposes `max_linear_accel`, speed-scaled lookahead (`lookahead_time`, min/max lookahead) and collision look-ahead in time. [C4-S32]

#### Autoware MPC lateral controller (automotive)
- Vehicle models: kinematic bicycle with first-order steering delay (default), kinematic without delay, and dynamic bicycle with slip angle; tuning starts from wheelbase and steering limits, then steering time constant and delay, acceleration limit and velocity time constant. [C4-S38, overview and "Set dynamics information"]
- Consistent lateral offset is "most often due to the offset of the steering sensor or self-position estimation"; an optional online steering-offset remover exists. [C4-S38, "Other tips for tuning"]

#### ros2_controllers diff_drive_controller
- `wheel_separation_multiplier` and per-side wheel radius multipliers correct nominal geometry; per-axis velocity/acceleration limits are off by default (`has_acceleration_limits: false`). [C4-S39]

## Recommended practice
1. Identify the command-to-motion response before tuning a predictive tracker: fit a delay plus first-order lag per actuator from step or logged commands, and validate on multi-step prediction, not one-step error. [C4-S17, pp. 4, 20; C4-S16, p. 5; C4-S03, p. 6]
2. Collect identification data that cover the whole reachable command space, including transients, in both turning directions; hold each command long enough to see steady state. [C4-S21, pp. 6–7; C4-S01, p. 17]
3. Set controller velocity and acceleration limits from measured chassis capability, and keep any downstream smoother limits equal to or looser than the controller's. [C4-S41, p. 16; C4-S30; C4-S40]
4. Make sure horizon × max speed fits the local map and `model_dt` does not exceed the control period. [C4-S25, "Prediction Horizon…"]
5. Choose velocity feedback deliberately: closed loop needs high-rate, low-latency odometry; open loop needs accurate acceleration limits and a responsive platform. [C4-S31; C4-S35]
6. Where delay is significant, compensate it in the model (forward prediction, actuator state, or replay of in-flight commands) rather than only by gain tuning. [C4-S16, pp. 1–2; C4-S15, p. 14; C4-S30]
7. For skid-steer/tracked robots, calibrate slip-aware kinematics per terrain, or estimate traction/slip online, or learn the residual. [C4-S19, p. 5; C4-S12, pp. 5–7; C4-S11, p. 1]
8. For a persistent fraction-of-command error, give the controller a way to estimate it rather than relying on feedback alone: an integrating-disturbance (offset-free) estimator [C4-S05, pp. 50–53; C4-S52, p. 1], an online-estimated gain/traction parameter in the model [C4-S12, pp. 5–7; C4-S50, pp. 2185–2186], or an adaptive inner loop that makes the vehicle behave like the controller's nominal model. [C4-S51, pp. 1, 5]
9. Keep the adaptation fast enough to follow sudden changes but check its effect on command smoothness: fast observers remove offset but pass more measurement noise. [C4-S50, p. 2186; C4-S52, pp. 5–6]
10. Check the controller's input signals (speed, yaw rate) against an independent sensor before blaming the controller. [C4-S38, "Confirmation of the input information"]

## Common mistakes
- Tuning or testing with a perfect-dynamics simulator and hard penalties: "effective in simulation with perfect dynamics, but fails on the actual system". [C4-S01, p. 13]
- Training a model on one direction or style of driving more than another; the controller then fails only in that direction. [C4-S01, pp. 17–18]
- Fitting one-step error without regularisation: lower one-step error but unstable multi-step simulation. [C4-S01, p. 12]
- Ignoring actuator delay: overshoot, oscillation after turns, or late turn-in. [C4-S16, p. 5; C4-S37; C4-S38]
- Adding acceleration limiting (a clip layer or the robot's own clipping) downstream of a pure-pursuit-type tracker that does not model it: the result is overshoot and inability to fully realise the planned motion [C4-S41, p. 4]. For MPPI, a Nav2 maintainer notes that relying on a downstream velocity smoother means MPPI does not itself respect the dynamics and that limits can be slightly broken when the smoother receives newer odometry [C4-S47].
- Using closed-loop velocity feedback with slow or delayed odometry, or with very small acceleration steps, which can stall or mis-shape acceleration. [C4-S31; C4-S35; C4-S42]
- Over-tuning pure pursuit lookahead to one course to cancel unmodelled slip. [C4-S14, PDF p. 73]
- Using the ideal differential-drive model on a skid-steer robot; its errors are much larger than any calibrated model. [C4-S20, p. 6]
- Driving identification runs with high random accelerations on high-traction ground: drivetrain and tyre wear. [C4-S21, p. 15]
- Filtering a noisy feedback signal (e.g. yaw rate) without accounting for the delay the filter adds: the stabilising effect is reduced and feedback gains must be lowered. [C4-S44, p. 627]
- Smoothing MPPI's output with an external filter: it delays the response and can break the actuator limits the optimiser assumed. [C4-S46, p. 3]
- Relying on wheel odometry alone to judge slip-affected motion: on a rover its error was at least 10% of distance, at least four times the visual odometry error (≤2.5%). [C4-S43, p. 3]
- Reusing a residual model learned in an earlier session without continuing to learn: about 20% larger prediction error. [C4-S45, p. 7]
- Running a predictive controller with no disturbance or parameter estimate when the plant persistently differs from the model: a steady tracking offset remains. [C4-S52, pp. 1, 5; C4-S54, p. 4]
- Relying on long-term (lap-to-lap) learning alone after a sudden change in the command gain: large error on the first run (repeat) after the change and slow convergence against an outdated prior. [C4-S50, p. 2186]

## Disagreements between sources
- **Open vs closed loop velocity feedback.** Nav2 smoother docs: open loop is good when acceleration limits are set appropriately [C4-S31]; maintainer: if acceleration limits are set accurately from empirical data and the robot responds fast, the two are largely the same unless odometry is delayed or inaccurate [C4-S35]; DWPP author: closed loop can fail to reach proper acceleration because odometry lags the command [C4-S35]; PR #5617 author: closed loop stalled low-acceleration motion [C4-S42]. Nav2 main now uses a blend (measured speed clamped toward the last command and predicted forward) [C4-S28].
  A later thread adds a contributor (non-maintainer) report that closed loop "snaked" on a high-delay robot even with delay compensation, although in a later simulation test closed loop with delay compensation had no oscillations but got stuck before a u-turn; the maintainer hoped open loop would become unnecessary [C4-S48].
- **Is a delay parameter needed?** The PR author reports clear gains from delay compensation on a hydraulic-steered vehicle [C4-S37], and the Nav2 migration guide shows a vehicle with 600 ms steering delay tracking "visibly better" with it [C4-S30]; the maintainer suspected it might mask poor low-acceleration handling that a separate branch addresses [C4-S36]. The GitHub material is level D.
- **One-step vs multi-step identification.** AutoRally used one-step error for convexity while calling multi-step "technically the correct objective" [C4-S01, p. 12]; IPEM argues integrated (multi-step) error is better for prediction over a horizon [C4-S17, p. 4].
- **Robust MPPI variants.** RMPPI outperformed MPPI and Tube-MPPI in the authors' tests [C4-S04, p. 1], but on hardware it was not faster per lap and MPPI and Tube-MPPI behaved similarly [C4-S04, p. 8].
- **How to get smooth MPPI commands.** The original MPPI smooths the output with a Savitzky–Golay filter [C4-S01, p. 7]; Smooth MPPI argues external smoothing delays the response and can violate limits, and instead samples command rates [C4-S46, p. 3]. Nav2's maintainer attributes output jitter mainly to sampling spread [C4-S47].
- **How robust is MPPI to model error?** The MPPI authors report that re-planning keeps learned-model performance within 10% of known-model performance in simulated tasks [C4-S03, p. 6]; other authors state MPPI "often lacks robustness" when simulated and true dynamics differ and show run failures under a 40% actuator-power loss [C4-S51, pp. 1, 5] or a persistent offset under persistent disturbance [C4-S54, p. 4] (the last is a level-C preprint). The cases differ in size and type of mismatch.
- **Where to correct a gain error.** One line adapts the prediction model online (traction factor by MHE [C4-S12]; linear-in-parameter model refitted every step [C4-S50]; integrating disturbance [C4-S05]); another keeps the nominal model and adds an adaptive inner loop so the vehicle matches it [C4-S51]. No source compared the two on the same vehicle.
- **Documentation mismatch.** The Nav2 MPPI configuration page names the angular delay parameter `model_delay_wx` [C4-S29], while the migration guide and PR use `model_delay_wz` [C4-S30; C4-S37].

## Open questions
- No open copy of Williams et al. ICRA 2016 (searched again 2026-09-27: Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex mark it closed, co-author page has no PDF, Google Scholar behind a bot check; the earlier-defined MPPI is now covered by C4-S49), Rajamani 2012, Martínez et al. IJRR 2005, Pentzer et al. JFR 2014 (online track-ICR estimation) or Huskić et al. IJRR 2019 (high-speed skid-steer path following) was found; their details are not used.
- No peer-reviewed paper on Nav2 MPPI itself was found; its behaviour here comes from source code, docs and GitHub discussion (levels B–D).
- Partly answered (2026-09-27 gap-fill): the size of the effect of a command gain error on a gradient-based MPC on a ground robot (turn rate ×0.5 [C4-S50]) and of actuator-power loss on MPPI (multirotor simulation, −40% [C4-S51]) is reported, and offset-free MPC theory says why the offset appears and how it is removed [C4-S05; C4-S52]. Still unanswered: (a) no source measured MPPI path-tracking error on a ground vehicle as a function of a constant linear-speed or turn-rate under-delivery fraction; (b) no source compared, on one vehicle and one gain error, a fixed gain correction (scaling commands by the identified ratio), an online-estimated gain/disturbance, a learned residual model and an adaptive inner loop; (c) the ground-robot results [C4-S50] are for turn-rate scaling only, not linear-speed scaling, and use gradient-based MPC, not MPPI.
- The primary offset-free MPC papers (Pannocchia & Rawlings, AIChE J. 49:426–437, 2003; Maeder, Borrelli & Morari, Automatica 45(10):2214–2222, 2009) and Pannocchia et al., IFAC-PapersOnLine 48(23), 2015 had no downloadable open copy (OpenAlex marks the first two closed; the IFAC paper is open access but the publisher site returned a bot-check page); offset-free theory is cited through the textbook [C4-S05] and the tutorial review [C4-S52].
- No source gave stability margins of pure pursuit or MPPI as a function of pure delay in closed form for differential-drive robots.
- How quickly slip parameters change on grass and how fast online adaptation must be for small skid-steer robots was not measured in the sources read.
- Effects of noisy (rather than delayed) odometry on MPPI's initial state were described only qualitatively, and only in GitHub discussion; the maintainer states noise is not modelled [C4-S48]. No peer-reviewed study was found.
- Closed-form delay-stability analysis of pure pursuit exists (Ollero & Heredia, IROS 1995; Heredia & Ollero, Advanced Robotics 21(1):23–50, 2007) but no open copy was found, so its results are not used.
- Lenain et al., "Adaptive and predictive path tracking control for off-road mobile robots," European J. Control 13(4):419–439, 2007 (slip-adaptive agricultural path tracking) could not be downloaded (author copy host unreachable, HAL behind a bot check); not used.
- Pentzer et al. JFR 2014 (online track-ICR EKF) still has no open copy; online ICR estimation fed to a controller is covered only indirectly (traction estimation [C4-S12], rover slip vector [C4-S43]).
