## Summary
- Structure: a velocity loop works best as feedforward (model-based voltage for friction, speed and acceleration) plus feedback that corrects only the leftover error, and when it sits inside a cascade it must be much faster than the outer loop that treats it as ideal (10× in Åström & Murray's example), an assumption to be checked after design. [C1-S16, "Choice of Control Strategies"; C1-S15, "The Permanent-Magnet DC Motor Feedforward Equation"; C1-S24, section 11.6, pp. 341–342]
- Delay sets the achievable gain and bandwidth: sampling adds about h/2 (often several periods), neglected lags and filters add to an "effective delay", and the robust SIMC choice τc = θ gives about 61° phase margin with crossover near 0.5/θ (for processes with τ1 ≤ 8θ); PI is preferred over PID when delay is significant. Smart-controller velocity filtering is a large delay in this sense (112 ms modelled for the NEO hall sensor at default settings), which SysId warns can make computed gains unstable if ignored. [C1-S28, section 6.7; C1-S29, section 2.1 and Table 2; C1-S37, section 2.2; C1-S20, `FeedbackControllerPreset.hpp` lines 131–139; C1-S18, `analyzing-gains.rst`, "Measurement Delays"]
- Any controller with integral action winds up when the actuator saturates; back-calculation (tracking time constant between Td and Ti, e.g. √(Ti·Td)) or inhibiting integration while saturated prevents it. FRC sources go further and discourage I altogether in favour of an accurate feedforward, tuning kS, then kV, then P. [C1-S28, section 6.5; C1-S30, section II; C1-S04, "Tuning"; C1-S17, "Integral Term Windup"; C1-S22, "Closed-Loop Overview"]
- Drives are identified from measured data as first-order-plus-delay models, e.g. from open-loop tests on an agricultural robot (τ = 0.15 s, L = 0.2 s) or by online extended-Kalman-filter fitting while a skid-steered UGV drove in circles (τc = 0.81 s, τd = 0.16 s), rather than taken from datasheets; in FRC tooling they are identified by quasistatic ramp plus dynamic step tests fitted by least squares to V = kS·sgn(v) + kV·v + kA·a, per drivetrain side. [C1-S37, section 3.1; C1-S35, section 4; C1-S18, "Creating an Identification Routine" and `loading-data.rst`; C1-S20, `Drive.java` lines 61–74]
- Friction is usually modelled as Coulomb plus viscous, with stiction and Stribeck effects at low speed; Coulomb friction can be direction- and method-dependent, no compensation method is intrinsically best, and at low speed stick-slip, integral-action limit cycles and encoder quantization (42 counts/rev on the NEO) limit accuracy. [C1-S31, sections II and IV; C1-S32, sections 4.1.3–4.1.6; C1-S26, ch. 2.1; C1-S25, abstract and section 6; C1-S12, "Motor Specifications"]
- Product-specific: on SPARK firmware/REVLib 2026 the feedforward terms are in **volts** (kS in V, kV in V per RPM, kA in V per RPM/s by default) while P, I and D still produce **duty cycle**; the old `kF`/`velocityFF` became `kV`, and REV's example changed from `1.0/5767` to `12.0/5767` ("kV is now in Volts, so we multiply by the nominal voltage (12V)"). [C1-S02, table "Default Units"; C1-S09, line 256; C1-S11, 2025 line 72 / 2026 lines 74–75]

## Findings

### Loop structure: cascades, feedforward and two-degree-of-freedom control (general)
- In an inner/outer ("cascade") design the inner loop is designed first to give "fast and accurate" control of an intermediate variable, and the outer loop is then designed as if the inner loop were perfect; this is valid only when the inner loop is fast relative to the outer loop's desired bandwidth. [C1-S24, section 11.6, p. 341]
- In Åström & Murray's worked cascade example the inner-loop bandwidth was chosen as 10 rad/s, "10 times that of the outer loop" (1 rad/s), and the outer loop was shaped so that |Lo| < 0.1 above 10 rad/s so the inner dynamics could be neglected; the combined design had a phase margin of 68° and a gain margin of 6.2. [C1-S24, section 11.6, pp. 341–343, Figs. 11.17–11.19]
- The "inner loop is perfect" approximation "may not be valid", so it must be checked once the full design is complete. [C1-S24, section 11.6, p. 342]
- PID is typically the lowest level of a hierarchy, with higher-level controllers (e.g. model predictive control) giving it setpoints; in process control "more than 95% of the control loops are of PID type, most loops are actually PI control". [C1-S28, section 6.1]
- Setpoint weighting (a weight b on the reference in the proportional term and c in the derivative term) gives a controller "with two degrees of freedom", because the signal path from reference to control signal differs from the path from measurement to control signal. [C1-S28, section 6.3, eq. 6.4 (and 6.5), Fig. 6.4]
- Model-based friction compensation can be applied as feedforward, using reference positions/velocities instead of measured ones; in Bona & Indri's manipulator tests this gave similar results to the measured-state version "with a significantly reduced on-line computational burden". [C1-S31, section IV]

### PI or PID for a velocity loop, and tuning from a delay model (general)
- The SIMC rules for a first-order-plus-delay model k·e^(−θs)/(τ1·s + 1) are Kc = (1/k)·τ1/(τc + θ) and τI = min(τ1, 4(τc + θ)), with the closed-loop time constant τc the only tuning parameter; derivative action (τD = τ2) is "primarily recommended" for processes with dominant second-order dynamics (τ2 > θ, approximately). [C1-S29, section 3.3, eqs. 23–25, p. 296]
- The recommended choice τc = θ ("fast response with good robustness") gives, for a first-order delay process with τ1 ≤ 8θ (so that τI = τ1), gain margin 3.14, phase margin 61.4°, sensitivity peak Ms = 1.59 and a gain-crossover frequency of 0.50 (normalised by θ); the source calls these margins "much better" than typical minimums of GM > 1.7 and PM > 30°. For lag-dominant processes (τ1 > 8θ, τI = 8θ) the margins are lower: GM 2.96, PM 46.9°, Ms 1.70. [C1-S29, eq. 28, p. 296; section 4.1.1 and Table 2, p. 297]
- A larger τc detunes the loop: it lowers the controller gain, which reduces input usage and sensitivity to measurement noise and makes operation smoother. [C1-S29, section 6.1, p. 303]
- If measurement noise is a problem with SIMC settings, the source suggests, in order: filter the measurement with a first-order filter (time constant up to about 0.5θ "without a large affect on performance and robustness"), remove derivative action, and finally detune with a larger τc. [C1-S29, section 6.2, pp. 303–304]
- For a DC motor speed model of first order with transport delay L and time constant τ, one agricultural-robot study states that Ziegler–Nichols rules suit L < τ/2, Cohen–Coon rules L < 2τ and a dead-time rule otherwise, and that when L > τ/2 "PI is preferred over PID to keep stability". [C1-S37, section 2.2, p. 898]
- In that study the Cohen–Coon tuning gave a higher proportional gain with "significant overshoot and slow damping", and the authors note that a high proportional gain "could be problematic when the sample time is long" (their speed loop ran at Ts = 150 ms). [C1-S37, section 4.1, p. 904]
- Ziegler and Nichols' ultimate-gain method finds the "ultimate sensitivity" Su (the proportional gain at which the loop just oscillates) and the oscillation period Pu, and then sets: proportional only, gain 0.5·Su; proportional plus reset (PI), gain 0.45·Su and reset rate 1.2/Pu; proportional plus reset plus pre-act (PID), gain 0.6·Su, reset rate 2/Pu and pre-act time Pu/8. ("Reset rate" is the inverse of the integral time and "pre-act time" is the derivative time in today's terms.) [C1-S38, p. 762 and "Summary of Controller Adjustments", p. 765]
- The same paper gives equivalent settings from the open-loop "reaction curve" (process reaction rate R1 and apparent lag L), e.g. PI gain 0.9/(R1·L) and reset rate 0.3/L per minute. [C1-S38, "Optimum Settings From Reaction Curve", p. 764, and p. 765]
- Ziegler and Nichols note that the proportional-only rule (half the ultimate sensitivity, with a 25 per cent amplitude ratio between successive oscillation peaks) "must be modified in some cases", and that pre-act (derivative) was "not generally useful on pressure- or flow-control applications" but had been used most widely on temperature control. [C1-S38, pp. 761 and 763]
- Åström and Hägglund replaced the manual ultimate-gain experiment, which they describe as difficult to automate while keeping the oscillation amplitude under control, with a relay in the loop: a process with at least 180° phase lag at high frequencies may then oscillate at approximately the critical period, and the critical gain follows from the relay amplitude d and the output amplitude a as kc = 4d/(πa). [C1-S39, section 2, p. 646, eq. 1]
- The relay experiment generates its own test signal with strong content at the critical frequency, a relay with hysteresis makes it less sensitive to measurement noise, and the method is described as "not sensitive to modelling errors and disturbances"; the describing-function approximation of the period is in many cases within "a few per cent". [C1-S39, abstract, p. 645; section 2, p. 646; section 3, p. 647]

### Integrator windup and anti-windup (general)
- Windup happens because "all actuators have limitations": when the control signal hits a limit the feedback loop is broken, the integral keeps integrating the error, and the error must then have the opposite sign "for a long period" before the controller recovers, so "any controller with integral action may give large transients when the actuator saturates". [C1-S28, section 6.5]
- Limiting setpoint changes so the actuator never saturates "frequently leads to conservative bounds and poor performance" and "does not avoid windup caused by disturbances". [C1-S28, section 6.5, "Setpoint Limitation"]
- In incremental ("velocity") PID algorithms, windup is avoided by inhibiting integration whenever the output saturates, which the source states is equivalent to back-calculation. [C1-S28, section 6.5, "Incremental Algorithms"]
- Back-calculation feeds the difference between the controller output and the actual (measured or modelled) actuator output into the integrator through a gain 1/Tt; this signal is zero when the actuator is not saturated, so normal operation is unaffected. [C1-S28, section 6.5, "Back-Calculation and Tracking" and Fig. 6.7]
- The tracking time constant Tt "should be larger than Td and smaller than Ti", with a suggested rule of thumb Tt = √(Ti·Td); too small a Tt with derivative action lets spurious errors saturate the output and reset the integrator by accident. [C1-S28, section 6.5, "Back-Calculation and Tracking"]
- Modern anti-windup design states two goals: "small signal preservation" (no change to the response while the actuator is not saturated) and "large signal recovery" (after saturation, stay as close as possible to the unsaturated response). [C1-S30, section II-A, p. 307]
- Anti-windup design always assumes that the unconstrained (unsaturated) closed loop is asymptotically stable. [C1-S30, section II-B, p. 307]
- Saturation can cause loss of performance "or even stability", and the tutorial argues for accounting for saturation rather than oversizing actuators. [C1-S30, section I, p. 306]
- Peng, Vrančić and Hanus define windup as the performance loss when "the real plant input is temporarily different from the controller output" because of input limitations (their examples include "a motor driven actuator has a limited speed"), and state that the most suitable anti-windup strategy "for usual applications" is the conditioning technique using the "realizable reference", except when the input limits are too restrictive. [C1-S41, abstract and introduction]
- The same review says it is a misconception that anti-windup aims to reduce the step-response overshoot or is a synonym of bumpless transfer (smooth switching between controllers or modes). [C1-S41, introduction]
- A rate limit (a cap on how fast the actuator input can change, which is what an output ramp does) is its own kind of saturation: it matters "in mechanical systems where the inertia in various components of the actuator prevents it from moving very fast"; a useful model is a magnitude saturation cascaded with an integrator and gain inside a feedback loop, and anti-windup schemes exist for combined magnitude and rate saturation. [C1-S30, section III-E, item 3, pp. 316–317]
- Rate-limited actuators have been linked to pilot-induced oscillations and the loss of several aircraft. [C1-S30, section III-E, item 3, p. 317]
- In incremental (velocity-form) PID algorithms "it is also easy to limit the rate of change of the control signal". [C1-S28, section 6.5, "Incremental Algorithms"]

### Delay, sampling and measurement filtering limit the loop (general)
- Digital implementation adds dead time: if the input is read with sampling period h and the output is written right after the computation, the average measurement delay is about h/2; many systems do not organise the computation this way, so the delay is "often several sampling periods". [C1-S28, section 6.7, "Sampling"]
- Sampling can alias high-frequency disturbances into low-frequency signals inside the loop bandwidth, so an anti-aliasing prefilter is used; for attenuation by a factor 16 at half the sampling frequency with a second-order filter, the filter bandwidth is ωs/8, and the prefilter dynamics should be included in the control design. [C1-S28, section 6.7, "Aliasing", "Prefiltering" and Example 6.3]
- With a backward-difference derivative the filter coefficient Td/(Td + N·h) always lies between 0 and 1, which guarantees a stable difference equation. [C1-S28, section 6.7, "Discretization", eq. 6.17]
- The "half rule" folds fast dynamics into an effective delay: the largest neglected time constant is split evenly between the effective delay and the smallest retained time constant, and a digital implementation with sampling period h adds h/2 to the effective delay. [C1-S29, section 2.1, eqs. 10–11, p. 293]
- The effect of a delay on control performance "is worse than that of a lag of equal magnitude". [C1-S29, section 2.1, p. 293]
- A zero-order hold behaves, for small sampling periods, like a time delay of half a sampling interval; together with a second-order anti-aliasing filter this reduces the phase margin, and allowing a 5° to 15° loss gives the rule of thumb h·ωc ≈ 0.05 to 0.14 (ωc the continuous-time crossover frequency in rad/s), i.e. a Nyquist frequency about 23 to 70 times the crossover frequency. [C1-S42, "Selection of Sampling Interval and Antialiasing Filters", pp. 39–40, eq. 36]
- The same brief states that "Antialiasing filters are important in all cases". [C1-S42, p. 40, summary list]
- Low-resolution Hall sensors split each 360° electrical cycle into six 60° sectors. [C1-S36, section 2.1, p. 2]
- The simplest Hall speed estimate (average speed over the previous sector) assumes constant speed within a sector and fluctuates with sensor misplacement; the average-acceleration method is more accurate but causes pulsation when speed fluctuates greatly, and higher-order extrapolation leads to "considerable delays in the estimation results". [C1-S36, section 3.1, pp. 4–5, and Table 1]
- Observer-based Hall speed estimation has its own trade-off: back-EMF observers work poorly at low speed, and for the vector-tracking observer a high bandwidth makes the estimated speed fluctuate while a low bandwidth can undermine stability; extending the speed-loop bandwidth at low speed "remains problematic in practical applications". [C1-S36, section 3.3, pp. 8–9]

### System identification of motor drives (general)
- Neglecting inductance and friction, the DC motor speed response to voltage reduces to a first-order model with transport delay, ω(s)/V(s) = K·e^(−Ls)/(τs + 1), whose K, τ and L "can be obtained by means of open-loop tests". [C1-S37, section 2.2, eqs. 12–15, p. 898]
- On a differential-drive agricultural robot ("Agribot"), open-loop step tests gave K = 49.3 rad/s/V, τ = 0.15 s and L = 0.2 s (datasheet static gain 52.77 rad/s/V); the time constant and delay "must be obtained from measurements because they depend on the utilization conditions of the system". [C1-S37, section 3.1, eq. 35, p. 902]
- The same drive had a dead zone: the motor started only when the control signal exceeded ±20% of its nominal value. [C1-S37, section 3.1, p. 902]
- The response of many field-robot powertrains "can be modeled adequately" as a time delay plus first-order response, ω̇(t) = (ω̃(t − τd) − ω(t))/τc, because "No powertrain can instantaneously drive wheels from one velocity to another", although "Most WMR motion models in related work omit powertrain dynamics"; such a model is needed when future wheel speeds must be predicted from planned commands, as in model predictive control. [C1-S35, section 4, p. 20, eq. 50]
- τc and τd can be identified online with an extended Kalman filter that compares predicted and encoder-measured wheel velocities; on a skid-steered hydraulic "LandTamer" the fit gave τc = 0.81 s and τd = 0.16 s. [C1-S35, section 4, pp. 20–21, Fig. 5]
- Fitting the integrated prediction (wheel velocity) was "more accurate and robust to noise than calibration to ω̇ residuals", and the calibrated powertrain models were "instrumental in removing large prediction errors associated with transients". [C1-S35, section 4, p. 21]
- Constant-voltage (step) tests at many voltage levels in both directions give steady-state speeds from which viscous and Coulomb friction are fitted by least squares; ramp-voltage tests give them from the slope and intercept of the speed response; inertia is then found from the coast-down transient after the input is removed. [C1-S32, sections 3.1–3.3 and 4.1.5]
- In those experiments viscous friction agreed within 0.43% between the step and ramp methods, but Coulomb friction differed by 36% (0.5141 vs 0.3293 N·m), which the authors attribute to the ramp keeping the motor out of the static-friction regime. [C1-S32, sections 4.1.3 and 4.1.9, Tables 4 and 10]
- Coulomb friction was significantly direction-dependent (ANOVA p = 0.0014) while viscous friction was not (p = 0.067), so the authors recommend identifying across multiple operating quadrants and averaging. [C1-S32, section 4.1.6]
- The inertia estimate depended on which friction values were used (0.1346 vs 0.1178 kg·m², a 12.5% difference), showing that inertia identification is sensitive to the friction estimate. [C1-S32, sections 4.1.5 and 4.1.8, Tables 6 and 9]
- The 12 V test motor needed about 2.3 V before it began rotating. [C1-S32, section 4.1.2]
- For a differential-drive AGV (TurtleBot 2), offline Levenberg–Marquardt identification used to initialise online recursive least squares gave the smallest laboratory errors: velocity error 0.001 m/s and regulation time 1.6 s, versus 0.023 m/s and 2.9 s for offline-only and 0.011 m/s and 7.5 s for online RLS from random initial values. [C1-S33, Table 4]
- Offline identification is "sufficient only" for robots whose payload does not change; carrying loads changes mass and inertia, which calls for online (adaptive) identification. [C1-S33, section 2]
- Commercial mobile robots are usually commanded with linear and angular velocity, and a dynamic model with those velocities as inputs is described as "a much better solution" than one using torques or voltages. [C1-S33, section 3.1]
- General identification theory rests on a few concepts: a model, the information in the data, estimation (fitting the model to estimation data) and validation (checking the model on separate validation data); a good model agrees with the estimation data but "should not be too complex". [C1-S43, section 2 "The Core", report pp. 3–4]
- Identifying a system from data recorded while a feedback controller is running is a special problem: the input is correlated with the unmeasured noise, so a least-squares fit is "typically ... biased", and spectral analysis, instrumental-variable and subspace methods "give erroneous results when applied directly to closed-loop data". [C1-S44, section 1, report pp. 2–3]
- Closed-loop experiments are still used when the plant is unstable, must stay under control for safety or production reasons, or when the model is wanted for control design ("identification for control"). [C1-S44, section 1, report p. 4]

### Disturbance observers: robust speed control under model–plant mismatch (general)
- A disturbance observer (DOb, proposed by Ohnishi in 1983) lumps plant uncertainty (e.g. wrong inertia) and external load into one "fictitious disturbance", estimates it through a low-pass filter using a nominal plant model, and feeds the estimate back; if the observer bandwidth is large enough, the outer (performance) controller "can be designed by considering only the nominal plant dynamics". [C1-S45, abstract, p. 1; section IV-A, eq. 1, p. 5]
- The observer bandwidth and filter order are limited by measurement noise (which depends on encoder resolution and controller sampling time) and by the waterbed effect (which depends on plant dynamics such as right-half-plane zeros and poles); raising the bandwidth makes the loop more noise-sensitive. [C1-S45, section IV-A, p. 5]
- DOb-based control gives a two-degree-of-freedom structure: Ohnishi's 1985 work showed "theoretically and experimentally" that robustness and performance "can be independently adjusted" by the observer and the performance controller respectively. [C1-S45, section II-B, p. 2]
- Commercial motor drives embed disturbance observers, e.g. Panasonic MINAS-A5 drivers use one "to diminish the impact of the disturbance torque, reduce vibration, and offset any speed decline". [C1-S45, section III-C, p. 4]
- Tuning of the nominal inertia is still "an open problem": experiments show robust stability and performance "may significantly deteriorate when the nominal inertia matrix changes", and design parameters are "generally tuned by trial and error". [C1-S45, section VI, p. 9]

### Friction modelling and compensation (general)
- The simplest static friction model is Coulomb plus viscous, F(v) = Fc·sgn(v) + β·v; stiction adds a higher breakaway force Fs at rest, and the Stribeck effect describes the continuous fall of friction with speed in the low-velocity region. [C1-S31, section II]
- Poor or absent friction compensation leads to tracking errors (especially at low velocity), stick-slip, "hunting" when stopping, and limit cycles at velocity reversals; friction is quoted as causing "50% error in some heavy industrial manipulators". [C1-S31, section I]
- Identifying a dynamic friction model such as LuGre is hard: its internal state cannot be measured, stick-slip is very sensitive to the dynamic parameters, and high-precision sensors are needed to capture pre-sliding behaviour. [C1-S31, section II]
- Compensation methods fall into four classes: (A) fixed model-based terms identified offline, (B) compensation of a few main features such as the maximum stick force, (C) adaptive model-based schemes, and (D) methods not tied to a friction model (gain choice, disturbance observers). [C1-S31, section III]
- Fixed (class A) compensation is the cheapest to run but requires accurate offline identification and "cannot, obviously, account for friction variations". [C1-S31, section III]
- A friction model with zero force at zero velocity cannot compensate static friction; that part can then only be removed by integral action. [C1-S31, section III-A]
- A large integral gain that would destabilise the loop during sliding can be used in the final stick phase of positioning. [C1-S31, section III-D]
- In a two-joint direct-drive arm, a static LuGre-based compensation gave 0.5 mm RMS error at low speed but 3.5 mm at high speed, while a polynomial model gave 1.8 mm and 2.9 mm, showing that the best friction model depends on the operating speed range. [C1-S31, section IV]
- The reviewers conclude that no compensation method is "intrinsically superior"; the choice depends on sensor accuracy, actuator characteristics and real-time constraints. [C1-S31, section IV]
- On a small geared DC motor, a two-parameter Coulomb–viscous model explained more than 98% of the steady-state torque–speed variance (R² = 0.98, RMSE 0.29 N·m); Stribeck and LuGre fits reduced RMSE (0.18 and 0.20 N·m) only in the low-speed region, and above about 5 rad/s all models behaved nearly identically. [C1-S32, section 4.1.4, Table 5]
- Temperature drift of ±1 °C was estimated to contribute about 1.5% to friction-coefficient variability through lubricant viscosity. [C1-S32, section 4.1.9]
- The LuGre model represents friction as the average deflection z of elastic "bristles" between the surfaces: dz/dt = v − |v|·z/g(v) and F = σ0·z + σ1·dz/dt + σ2·v, with the Stribeck curve σ0·g(v) = Fc + (Fs − Fc)·e^(−(v/vs)²); it has six parameters (σ0, σ1, σ2, Fc, Fs, vs), and g(v) need not be symmetric, so direction-dependent friction can be captured. [C1-S40, section II, eqs. 1–4, p. 420]
- Its authors state that the model captures "the Stribeck effect, hysteresis, spring-like characteristics for stiction, and varying break-away force", with the hysteresis loop widening at higher rates of velocity change. [C1-S40, abstract, p. 419; section IV-B, Fig. 3, p. 422]
- Friction "may give rise to limit cycles in servo drives where the controller has integral action" ("hunting"), and a simulation of the model under PID position control reproduces such limit cycles. [C1-S40, section V-A, Figs. 7–8, p. 423]
- An observer-based friction compensator (estimating the unmeasurable bristle state) is also given for velocity control, with asymptotic convergence of the velocity error when the friction parameters are known exactly; the authors call that assumption "strong" and leave sensitivity to parameter errors and velocity-measurement accuracy open. [C1-S40, section V-B, eq. 15 and Theorem 2, p. 424]

### Actuator limits outside FRC: thermal current limiting and supply-voltage compensation (general)
- Kawaharazuka et al.'s basic two-node thermal model of a motor has two heat capacities (winding "core" and housing) and two thermal resistances (core to housing, housing to ambient), with heat input Q = Re·i² from the winding resistance; it applies to "brushless and brushed DC motors". [C1-S46, section II-A, eqs. 1–3, p. 2]
- Real thermal parameters differ from datasheet values, attributed to "heat dissipation to the attached metal parts, error of the ambient temperature, deterioration or burnout of motors"; a new motor and one used for over half a year differed by about 15 °C in housing temperature after 90 s at the same load, so the authors update the model online. [C1-S46, section II-A, p. 2, Fig. 3]
- Earlier software methods that cap current or torque at a fixed value that is safe for a set time either suit only very short motions or use a value "lower than the actual possible value". [C1-S46, section I, p. 1]
- maxon's drives (ESCON, EPOS) protect the winding with an "I2t" algorithm that measures motor current every current-control cycle (40 µs on EPOS4) and estimates the winding's thermal load from the configured nominal current and "thermal time constant winding"; when the limit is reached the current is limited to the nominal current (no shutdown, reduced torque). [C1-S47, section 2]
- With that algorithm a current of twice nominal is limited to nominal after 0.3 thermal time constants, and four times nominal after 0.1 thermal time constants; the allowed overload time adapts to the current level and to cyclic overload/cool-down. [C1-S47, section 2, "Example"]
- maxon contrasts this with the fixed-overload model of AC frequency inverters ("110% or 160%", sometimes for a configurable time), which it says does not fit compact servo motors that are often run "many times above the specified nominal data for a short period". [C1-S47, section 1]
- A typical "Maximum output current" is 2–3 times the nominal current, and it must also respect the torque limits of gearboxes and other mechanical parts. [C1-S47, section 3]
- Supply (bus) voltage compensation outside FRC: a Freescale controller for mains-fed three-phase AC induction motors senses the DC bus and scales the PWM modulation by Vnorm/Vbus(t), so that the phase voltage becomes independent of bus ripple and of slower changes from regeneration or brown-out; the correction is applied only to the modulation terms, not the bias term, to keep the waveform centred and avoid clipping at low bus voltage. [C1-S48, section 2.2 ("Dynamic Bus Ripple Cancellation"), p. 6; section 3.10, eqs. 16–20, pp. 46–47]
- In that controller a new bus-voltage reading is taken every PWM interrupt, and the note states that compensation both removes line-frequency ripple and handles "slower bus voltage changes resulting from regeneration or brown out conditions". [C1-S48, section 2.2, p. 6; section 3.10, p. 47]
- On a skid-steered wheeled robot, a trajectory can be impossible even though total vehicle power is within limits, because one side's motor exceeds its own drive limit: at 0.7 m/s and 1.2 m turn radius the outer motor needed about 58 W against a 51 W per-side limit (102 W total), and the outer wheel could not reach the commanded velocity (tests on a lab vinyl surface). [C1-S51, section 5.2, pp. 310–311]

### Wheel and track velocity control on other ground vehicles (general)
- Before its traction-control update, the Curiosity Mars rover turned each wheel at a constant speed along a commanded arc assuming flat terrain, which caused slip as the other wheels pushed a wheel climbing a rock. [C1-S34, section 1, p. 700]
- The update ("TRCTL") instead commands "idealized, no-slip wheel angular rates" computed from a rigid-body kinematic model, the measured attitude rates and the rocker/bogie suspension angles, because the rover has no wheel force/torque sensors and its visual-odometry slip measurements come only about once per metre. [C1-S34, abstract and section 1, p. 700]
- Wheel speeds are capped, so to speed up the wheel crossing an obstacle the other wheels are slowed. [C1-S34, section 1, p. 700]
- The wheel-rate function runs at 8 Hz inside the flight software. [C1-S34, section 4.1]
- Results: ground tests showed wheel forces reduced by 19% (leading wheels) and 11% (middle leading wheels); in flight, over 3.6 km and 149 drives, wheel current (used as a proxy for wheel torque) fell by 18.7%, drive duration rose by about 10% (up to 25% was predicted), and CPU use rose by less than 3%. [C1-S34, abstract and section 4.2]
- In the agricultural-robot study each drive motor ran a discrete PI speed controller with sampling period 150 ms, tuned initially from the first-order-plus-delay model and then fine-tuned on the robot. [C1-S37, sections 3.2 and 4.1, pp. 903–904]
- The dynamic model that Siwek et al. identify for the TurtleBot 2 AGV assumes a low-level PD controller that sets the motor supply voltages from the desired linear and angular velocity (taken from their reference [60]). [C1-S33, Appendix A]
- On a tracked field robot driving in wet, bumpy sorghum fields, the track motor speeds were held by a commercial "self-tuning PID" motion controller at 50 Hz, under a nonlinear receding-horizon controller running at 5 Hz (set by the GNSS rate); encoders gave speed "with an accuracy of 0.035 m/s". [C1-S49, abstract, p. 1; section 2.1, p. 4]
- That robot's model scales commanded speed and yaw rate by two traction parameters μ and κ between 0 and 1 (longitudinal and side slip 1 − μ and 1 − κ), estimated online; the authors report only that the low-level speed controller's performance "is sufficient", with a mean path error of 0.0423 m. [C1-S49, section 2.2, p. 5; section 5, p. 15; abstract]
- A tracked skid-steer robot (tests: straight line at a constant 0.75 m/s, then turning at 45 deg/s) drew different average straight-line motor currents on different ground: sand 26.17 A, mud 24.01 A, gravel 21.77 A, asphalt 21.23 A. [C1-S50, section 4.1, p. 7; section 4.2, Table 2, p. 8]
- On that robot the currents showed wide and narrow peaks on asphalt and gravel but an almost flat, higher trace on mud and sand, which the authors explain by the larger contact area and higher tractive effort on deformable ground; the offset between left and right currents was attributed to "different intrinsic motor characteristics and to power dissipation". [C1-S50, section 4.2, p. 8]
- The same paper uses motor current as a proxy for traction effort, since tractive effort, thrust and torque "can be considered as roughly proportional to the DC motor current". [C1-S50, section 3.3, eq. 18, p. 6]
- For their skid-steered test vehicle (a modified Pioneer 3-AT), Yu et al. replaced the manufacturer's "nontransparent" speed controller with their own PID controller and motor controller, running on a real-time system at a 1 kHz control sampling rate, and added current sensors on each side. [C1-S51, section 5, p. 309]

### Smart motor controllers and FRC tooling (REV SPARK MAX, CTRE, WPILib) — product-specific
The sub-sections below cover one product family and its tooling; several findings (e.g. on encoder velocity estimation and friction) are general but were gathered for this hardware.

#### How the controller-side velocity loop works
- REV describes closed-loop control as "a process that uses feedback to improve the accuracy of its outputs". [C1-S01, section "Closed-Loop Control Basics"]
- The SPARK MAX and SPARK Flex run their PID loop on the motor controller and update it every 1 ms. [C1-S01, section "Closed-Loop Control with SPARK Motor Controllers"]
- The SPARK loop is a standard PID algorithm with several feedforward terms added; an optional "arbitrary feedforward" is added to the output after all other calculations, in units of either voltage or duty cycle. [C1-S01, section "Closed-Loop Control with SPARK Motor Controllers"]
- In Velocity Control mode the setpoint is a speed in RPM, or in the units set by the velocity conversion factor. [C1-S05, "Velocity Control Mode"]
- REV notes that velocity-loop constants "are often of a very low magnitude" and suggests decreasing gains if the mechanism misbehaves. [C1-S05, hint box]
- Each SPARK has 4 closed-loop gain "slots" (numbered 0–3), each with its own set of constants; the slot is chosen with each setpoint command. [C1-S07, section "Slots"]
- P multiplies the error and "does the heavy lifting"; I accumulates error over time to remove steady-state error; D resists motion and damps oscillation. [C1-S04, section "The Constants"]
- The permanent-magnet DC motor feedforward ("voltage balance") equation is V = kS·sgn(ḋ) + kV·ḋ + kA·d̈, where ḋ is velocity and d̈ is acceleration. [C1-S15, section "The Permanent-Magnet DC Motor Feedforward Equation"]
- kS is the voltage needed to overcome static friction (applied in the direction of motion); kV is the voltage per unit speed needed to cruise against back-EMF (the voltage a spinning motor generates) and speed-dependent friction; kA is the voltage per unit acceleration. [C1-S15, same section]
- The voltage–speed and voltage–acceleration relationships are "almost entirely linear" for permanent-magnet DC motors of the kind used in FRC. [C1-S15, same section]
- For velocity control, the feedforward velocity comes directly from the setpoint, and acceleration (often omitted) can be taken from the change between successive velocity setpoints. [C1-S15, section "Using the Feedforward"]
- A pure-feedback (PID-only) velocity controller is flawed because non-zero effort is needed just to keep the wheel spinning at the setpoint, while PID output shrinks as the error shrinks. [C1-S16, section "Issues with Feedback Control Alone"]
- A pure feedforward velocity controller "works reasonably well" but cannot reject disturbances. [C1-S16, section "Pure Feedforward Control"]
- kD "is not useful for velocity control with a constant setpoint - it is only necessary when the setpoint is changing". [C1-S16, section "Choice of Control Strategies"]
- For an ideal first-order motor/flywheel model a P controller alone can place the single closed-loop pole anywhere on the real axis, so a derivative term is unnecessary on an ideal flywheel. [C1-S27, section 6.10.7 "Do flywheels need PD control?", pp. 71–73]
- In a velocity loop, overshoot "typically occurs ... only as a result of loop delay". [C1-S16, section "Velocity and Position Control"]
- CTRE Phoenix 6 (a comparable smart controller) documents velocity-loop gains as: kS = output to overcome static friction, kV = output per requested rps, kA unused in plain velocity mode, kP = output per rps of error, kI = output per rotation of integrated error, kD = output per (rps/s) of error derivative. [C1-S22, `ctre_2026_phoenix6_basic_pid_control.md`, section "Velocity Control"]

- REVLib 2026.0.0 added feedforward parameters "`kV` (formerly `kF`), `kA`, `kS`, `kG`, `kCos`, and `kCosRatio`". [C1-S09, line 256]

- The same units table gives kP as "Duty cycle per rotation", kI as "Duty cycle per (rotation*ms)" and kD as "(Duty cycle*ms) per rotation", listed as affected by the position conversion factor; the table does not name a control mode, but its per-rotation units suggest the position form. [C1-S02, table "Default Units"]

- MAXMotion Velocity mode honours the maximum acceleration but not the cruise velocity, so any top-speed clamp must be applied before the setpoint is sent. [C1-S07, section "MAXMotion Parameters"]

#### Measuring feedforward (system identification)
- System identification fits a model to measured input–output data; real data contain measurement noise (e.g. timing errors, encoder resolution limits) and system noise (e.g. vibration). [C1-S18, `introduction.rst`, "What is System Identification?"]
- The SysId "simple motor" fit is V = kS·sgn(ḋ) + kV·ḋ + kA·d̈. [C1-S18, `introduction.rst`, "Simple Motor Identification"]
- A standard routine has a quasistatic test (slow voltage ramp so the acceleration term is negligible) and a dynamic test (constant step voltage), each run forward and backward — four tests in total. [C1-S18, `creating-routine.rst`, "Types of Tests"]
- Default test settings are a ramp of 1 volt per second and a step of 7 volts, with a 10 s safety timeout. [C1-S18, `creating-routine.rst`, "Routine Config"]
- The drive identification needs at least 10 ft of space, ideally close to 20 ft, and "can not be accurately characterized while on blocks". [C1-S18, `running-routine.rst`, note]
- In WPILib's example drivetrain routine the same voltage is sent to both sides, but the left and right sides are logged as separate motors ("drive-left", "drive-right") with their own voltage, position and velocity. [C1-S20, `Drive.java` lines 54–74]
- SysId analyses one motor's position, velocity and voltage entries at a time, chosen by the motor name used in the log. [C1-S18, `loading-data.rst`]
- SysId regresses acceleration on velocity, voltage and sgn(velocity) (ordinary least squares) and converts the coefficients to kS = −γ/β, kV = −α/β, kA = 1/β. [C1-S20, `FeedforwardAnalysis.cpp` lines 25–41, 223–240]
- SysId's analysis code has "Drivetrain" and "DrivetrainAngular" model types that use the same fitted equation as the simple motor. [C1-S20, `FeedforwardAnalysis.cpp` lines 25–30]
- A good dataset needs "at least 2 steady-state velocity events to separate Ks from Kv" and "at least 1 acceleration event to find Ka". [C1-S20, `FeedforwardAnalysis.cpp` lines 166–171]
- Fit-quality guides: acceleration r² rarely exceeds 0.5 even on good data and below about 0.2 kA is "of dubious quality"; simulated-velocity r² above 0.9 indicates a good fit. [C1-S18, `viewing-diagnostics.rst`, "Goodness-of-Fit Metrics"]
- A successful quasistatic plot is nearly linear and a successful dynamic plot is an approximately exponential approach to a steady speed. [C1-S18, `viewing-diagnostics.rst`, "Time-Domain Plots"]
- A velocity threshold set too low lets pre-motion samples into the fit (a "leading tail"); too high leaves a gap in the acceleration–velocity plot. [C1-S18, `viewing-diagnostics.rst`, "Improperly Set Motion Threshold"]
- If the mechanism is light relative to motor power, kA tends toward zero and can be ignored for feedforward, but then computed feedback gains are likely inaccurate. [C1-S18, `viewing-diagnostics.rst`, "Acceleration-Velocity Plot"]
- kS "is nearly impossible to model, and must be measured empirically"; kV, kA and kG can be estimated from motor data (e.g. with ReCalc). [C1-S19, section "The WPILib Feedforward Classes"]
- WPILib gain units are kS in volts, kV in volts·seconds/distance and kA in volts·seconds²/distance. [C1-S19, note under "SimpleMotorFeedforward"]
- Manual kS measurement: slowly increase voltage until the mechanism starts to move; kS is the largest voltage applied before motion. [C1-S16, section "Feedforward Simplifications"]
- REV's manual kS method: find the smallest output that makes the mechanism move, then reduce it slightly so it no longer moves. [C1-S03, section "kS - Static Gain"]
- For a mechanism with a constant load (REV's elevator example), measure the upper and lower voltages V1 and V2 at which it starts to move each way; then kS = (V1 − V2)/2 and the constant term = V2 + kS. [C1-S03, section "kG - Static (Elevator) Gravity Gain"]
- A drivetrain can be modelled with separate linear and angular gains (Kv,lin, Ka,lin, Kv,ang, Ka,ang); if linear and angular gains are equal, left and right sides decouple into the one-dimensional case. [C1-S27, section 14.3 "Drivetrain left/right velocity state-space model"]
- The SysId "Spark Max" preset assumes the controller is already set to the analysis units through the SPARK MAX conversion factors. [C1-S18, `analyzing-gains.rst`, note in "Enter Controller Parameters"]

#### Tuning order and what limits the feedback gains
- REV's order: set all constants to 0; check motor direction; set up feedforwards; set P very small and increase until motion reaches the target at the desired speed; if it oscillates, reduce P or add a little D. [C1-S04, section "Tuning", steps 1–10]
- CTRE's manual order: all gains zero; kS "until just before the motor moves"; kV until output velocity matches setpoint; kP until output starts to oscillate; kD as much as possible without jitter. [C1-S22, `ctre_2026_phoenix6_closed_loop_requests.md`, "Closed-Loop Overview"]
- WPILib's flywheel tutorial: tune the feedforward first, then the PID; "PID portion of the controller is *much* easier to tune 'on top of' an accurate feedforward". [C1-S16, section "Combined Feedforward and Feedback Control"]
- REV: "I ... is not often recommended in FRC"; feedforward is recommended instead to remove steady-state error, and if I is used it should be limited to prevent windup. [C1-S04, section "I - Integral Gain"]
- WPILib: "systems that seem to require integral control to respond well probably have an inaccurate feedforward model". [C1-S17, "Integral Term Windup"]
- Integrator windup: when the actuator saturates the loop is effectively open, the integral keeps growing, and large overshoot follows; anti-windup limits this. [C1-S24, section 10.4, pp. 306–308]
- Windup mitigations named by WPILib: lower kI, reset the integrator when far from the setpoint (I-zone), or cap the integrator. [C1-S17, "Integral Term Windup"]
- On the SPARK, `iZone` limits where the integrator accumulates and `iMaxAccum` caps the I accumulator. [C1-S08, row kIZone_0; C1-S10, `ClosedLoopConfig.java` lines 393–402]
- Delay limits gain: a time delay τ behaves like a right-half-plane zero at 2/τ, and such a zero limits the gain-crossover frequency (for a 60° admissible phase lag, ωgc < 0.6·z). [C1-S24, section 11.5, pp. 332–333, eq. 11.16 and Padé approximation]
- Aggressive (LQR) gains can become unstable when sensor measurements are delayed too long. [C1-S27, section B.5 "Latency compensation"]
- Smart motor controllers "apply substantial low-pass filtering to their encoder velocity measurements, which can introduce a significant amount of phase lag", which "can cause the calculated gains for velocity loops to be unstable" unless the delay is entered in SysId. [C1-S18, `analyzing-gains.rst`, "Measurement Delays"]
- Delay of a moving-average filter with N taps and sample period T is (N − 1)·T/2; delay of a backward finite difference with period T is T/2. [C1-S18, `analyzing-gains.rst`, "Measurement Delays"; C1-S27, Theorems B.5.1–B.5.2; C1-S20, `FeedbackControllerPreset.hpp` lines 79–107]
- SysId presets: REV NEO built-in sensor 112 ms delay; REV non-NEO (quadrature) 81.5 ms; CTRE Phoenix 5 81.5 ms; CTRE Phoenix 6 about 1 ms (Kalman filter "default-tuned to lowest latency"). [C1-S20, `FeedbackControllerPreset.hpp` lines 109–152]
- SysId's Controller Period field should be 1 ms for most "smart controllers", because their onboard loops "run at 1Khz, or a period of 0.001s"; the SPARK and CTRE presets set it to 1 ms. [C1-S18, `analyzing-gains.rst`, "Controller Period"; C1-S20, `FeedbackControllerPreset.hpp` lines 119–158]
- SysId feedback gains are "educated guesses" and "a starting point for further tuning". [C1-S18, `analyzing-gains.rst`, "Feedback Analysis"]
- The "Max Acceptable Control Effort" in SysId should never exceed 12 V and ideally be lower; smaller allowed error and larger allowed effort give larger gains. [C1-S18, `analyzing-gains.rst`, "Specify Optimality Criteria"]
- If gains ask for more than the actuator can deliver, the mechanism saturates and "behave[s] as if the control gains were smaller than they are". [C1-S17, "Actuator Saturation"]
- Derivative action amplifies high-frequency noise; a filtered derivative kd·s/(1 + s·Tf) with Tf = (kd/kp)/N, N in 2–20, is the standard fix. [C1-S24, section 10.5 "Filtering the Derivative", p. 308]
- The SPARK exposes a per-slot derivative filter parameter (`kDFilter`). [C1-S08, row kDFilter_0; C1-S10, `ClosedLoopConfig.java`, `dFilter()`]
- REV MAXMotion tip: a loop tuned for high speed can look "wobbly" at low speed and vice versa; tuning separate gains in different slots may be easier. [C1-S06, "Tips for Smooth Motions"]

#### Velocity measurement filtering
- NEO hall sensor (REVLib 2026): `uvwMeasurementPeriod` is in ms, range [8, 64], default 32 ms; `uvwAverageDepth` must be 1, 2, 4 or 8 (default 8). [C1-S10, `EncoderConfig.java` lines 146–176]
- Quadrature encoder (REVLib 2026): `quadratureMeasurementPeriod` range [1, 100] ms, default 100 ms; `quadratureAverageDepth` range [1, 64], default 64. [C1-S10, `EncoderConfig.java` lines 118–136]
- The SPARK MAX parameter table describes the quadrature velocity as the difference between the current sample and the sample x·500 µs earlier, divided by that time, averaged over `kEncoderAverageDepth` samples (1–64). [C1-S08, rows kEncoderAverageDepth and kEncoderSampleDelta]
- Per REV support (quoted in a SysId issue), the NEO hall sensor "is sampled every 32ms, and the sampling window is 8 samples", does not use a backward finite difference, and the resulting delay is (8 − 1)/2 × 32 = 112 ms. [C1-S21]
- Hall-sensor velocity measurement settings were added in SPARK MAX firmware 1.6.0. [C1-S09, line 709]
- REVLib 2027 alpha 7 (September 2026) "Removes hall sensor velocity averaging configurations in favor of new firmware filtering system" and changes the default encoder average depth to 8 and sample delta to 20 (the sample delta is counted in 500 µs steps, so 20 corresponds to 10 ms). [C1-S09, lines 473–474; C1-S08, row kEncoderSampleDelta]
- CTRE Phoenix 5 comparison: a velocity sample is taken every 1 ms as the position change over the past 100 ms and fed into a 64-sample rolling average (defaults). [C1-S23, section "Velocity Measurement Filter"]
- CTRE's procedure for choosing these filters: start with period 1 ms and window 1, sweep the motor across its speed range, increase the period until the velocity is no longer coarse "stair-stepping", then increase the window until it is smooth "but still responsive enough". [C1-S23, section "Changing Velocity Measurement Parameters"]
- CTRE notes that unless sensor velocity is "hundreds of sensor units per sampling period" the measurement "will be very coarse". [C1-S23, same section]
- Poorly installed encoders and inappropriate filter settings produce noisy velocity signals that hurt both identification and control. [C1-S18, `viewing-diagnostics.rst`, "Noisy Velocity Signals"]

#### Current limits, voltage compensation and ramps
- Smart Current Limit: the SPARK reduces its output voltage to keep the motor phase current at the limit; it is on by default, brushless-only, and "highly recommended" for the NEO because of its low internal resistance. [C1-S10, `SparkBaseConfig.java` lines 213–228; C1-S13, section "Limiting Current"]
- The Smart Current Limit can scale linearly with speed (stall limit below `limitRpm`, reducing toward a free-speed limit above it) "to help with controllability in closed loop control"; leaving the RPM limit at 0 keeps the response linear across speed. [C1-S10, `SparkBaseConfig.java` lines 244–278]
- Default Smart Current Limit is 80 A; REV suggests 40–60 A for the NEO. [C1-S13, section "Suggested Current Limits"; C1-S08, row kSmartCurrentStallLimit]
- The secondary current limit is a simple on/off limit that briefly disables the output (in 50 µs PWM cycles) when exceeded; it is set higher than the Smart Current Limit by default. [C1-S10, `SparkBaseConfig.java` lines 286–306]
- Motor torque is proportional to phase current, not the controller's input current; average input current = average phase current × duty cycle. [C1-S14, section "Locked-rotor Testing with SPARK MAX Smart Current Limit"]
- REV locked-rotor tests (motor held stalled at a fixed Smart Current Limit): the NEO 550 survived the full 220 s test at 20 A but failed after about 27 s at 40 A, 5.5 s at 60 A and 2.0 s at 80 A; NEO (full-size) results are given only as graphs at 40, 50, 60 and 80 A. [C1-S14, NEO and NEO 550 tabs, "Time to Failure Summary"]
- Battery voltage sag changes mechanism performance; "voltage compensation" keeps the control-loop output voltage constant despite bus-voltage changes, but cannot raise the output above the available bus voltage. [C1-S17, "Voltage Sag"]
- Voltage compensation rescales the command as V = Vcmd·Vnominal/Vrail. [C1-S27, section 6.10.6 "Voltage compensation", eq. 6.23]
- REVLib `voltageCompensation(nominalVoltage)` "Sets the voltage compensation setting for all modes on the SPARK and enables voltage compensation" (writes the nominal voltage and sets compensation mode 2). [C1-S10, `SparkBaseConfig.java` lines 389–399]
- CTRE: duty-cycle output is affected by battery voltage, while voltage output "often results in more stable and reproducible behavior", and with torque-current output kV "is generally unnecessary" because the torque request is directly proportional to acceleration. [C1-S22, `ctre_2026_phoenix6_closed_loop_requests.md`, "Choosing Output Type"]
- Closed-loop ramp rate is "the maximum rate at which the motor controller's output is allowed to change", set as seconds from 0 to full output and stored as its reciprocal (duty cycle per second); 0 disables it. [C1-S10, `SparkBaseConfig.java` lines 372–386; C1-S08, rows kRampRate and kClosedLoopRampRate]
- MAXMotion Velocity honours a configured maximum acceleration and uses an internal velocity loop; REV says this speeds the mechanism up "in a controlled way, reducing power draw and increasing consistency". [C1-S06, "MAXMotion Velocity Control"]
- The idle-mode parameter sets the half-bridge state (coast or brake) when the controller commands zero output or is disabled. [C1-S08, row kIdleMode]

#### Low-speed precision
- NEO V1.1: 42 counts per revolution hall-sensor encoder, free speed 5676 RPM, motor Kv "473 Kv" (RPM per volt), stall torque 2.6 N·m, stall current 105 A. [C1-S12, table "Motor Specifications"]
- Reading an incremental encoder at a fixed sample rate gives a position quantization error of up to half a count, and numerical differentiation of the quantized position "largely amplif[ies]" these errors into the velocity. [C1-S25, abstract and section 2]
- Time stamping estimates velocity by fitting a polynomial through recent encoder edges and their times. Extending it with a "skip" option (storing only every (r+1)th encoder event) improved velocity estimation by 54% and acceleration estimation by 92% compared with time stamping without skip (r = 3 vs r = 0) in experiments on a motion system. [C1-S25, abstract and sections 5–6]
- Friction can cause steady-state errors, limit cycling and hunting. [C1-S26, Introduction, p. 3]
- Basic friction models combine Coulomb friction (constant, opposing motion), viscous friction (proportional to speed) and the Stribeck effect (friction falling with increasing speed at low speed); at zero speed friction cannot be described as a function of speed alone. [C1-S26, sections 1.1–1.1.1, pp. 4–5]
- With PD control, stick-slip may occur at low velocity; removing it with high gains risks instability and needs a strong actuator. [C1-S26, section 2.1, p. 14]
- Integral control removes steady-state error but can cause limit cycling at low or zero velocity and problems at velocity reversal; a deadband and anti-windup help but add their own steady-state errors. [C1-S26, section 2.1, p. 14]
- Dither (a small high-frequency added signal) can smooth the friction discontinuity at low velocity. [C1-S26, section 2.1, p. 14]
- Model-based friction feedforward uses the reference trajectory, does not affect closed-loop stability, and needs no extra real-time loop; friction feedback uses the measured state but requires a stability analysis. [C1-S26, section 2.2.1, pp. 15–16]
- The kS term cancels static friction; without it a mechanism with much static friction "will not have a linear control voltage-velocity relationship". [C1-S16, section "Feedforward Simplifications"]
- kS is applied in the direction of the desired velocity on SPARK; CTRE applies kS using the sign of the velocity setpoint in velocity loops, and warns that when using the error sign instead, a too-large kS causes dithering near the target. [C1-S03, section "kS - Static Gain"; C1-S22, `ctre_2026_phoenix6_closed_loop_requests.md`, "Static Feedforward Sign"]
- The SPARK MAX `kInputDeadband` parameter (default printed as "%0.05") is described as the "Percent of the input which results in zero output for PWM mode". [C1-S08, row kInputDeadband]

## Recommended practice
**General (any velocity loop)**
1. Identify each wheel drive with open-loop step tests as a first-order-plus-delay model (gain, time constant, transport delay), measured on the real system rather than taken from the datasheet, and note any dead zone. [C1-S37, sections 2.2 and 3.1; C1-S35, section 4]
2. Add sampling (about h/2, or more if the computation is not scheduled right after sampling) and measurement-filter lags to the effective delay, using the half rule for neglected lags. [C1-S28, section 6.7; C1-S29, section 2.1]
3. Start with PI (derivative only for dominant second-order dynamics), tuned from the delay model, e.g. SIMC with τc = θ; detune with a larger τc if noise or input activity is too high. [C1-S29, sections 3.3–3.4 and 6.1–6.2; C1-S37, section 2.2]
4. Make the inner velocity loop much faster than any outer loop that treats it as ideal (10× in Åström & Murray's example), and verify the approximation after the full design. [C1-S24, section 11.6, pp. 341–342]
5. Add anti-windup whenever integral action is used: back-calculation with Td < Tt < Ti (e.g. Tt = √(Ti·Td)) or stop integrating while saturated; do not rely on setpoint limiting alone. [C1-S28, section 6.5]
6. Identify friction in both directions and at several speeds (steady-state steps plus ramps), average across quadrants, and use Coulomb plus viscous unless the low-speed region matters. [C1-S32, sections 3.1–3.2, 4.1.4 and 4.1.6; C1-S31, section IV]
7. Apply friction compensation as feedforward from the reference where possible; it needs no extra stability analysis and less computation. [C1-S26, section 2.2.1; C1-S31, section IV]
8. Choose the controller sampling period so that h·ωc ≈ 0.05–0.14 relative to the intended crossover frequency, and use an anti-aliasing filter. [C1-S42, pp. 39–40]
9. When identifying the drive, note that standard methods generally work well on open-loop data; if data must be taken with the speed loop closed, use a method intended for closed-loop data, because naive least squares is typically biased and spectral, instrumental-variable and subspace methods give erroneous results on it. Check every model on separate validation data. [C1-S44, section 1; C1-S43, section 2]
10. For compact servo motors, protect the winding with a thermal (I²t-type) model based on nominal current and winding thermal time constant rather than a fixed overload time, and keep the peak current within the mechanical limits of the gearbox. [C1-S47, sections 1–3; C1-S46, section I]
11. If the supply voltage varies, scale the PWM command by Vnominal/Vbus measured at the PWM rate. [C1-S48, section 3.10]

**Smart motor controllers (SPARK MAX / FRC tooling)**
12. Confirm firmware/library versions and units before entering gains; on firmware 26/REVLib 2026 enter feedforward in volts and PID in duty cycle, in the units set by the conversion factors. [C1-S02, table "Default Units"; C1-S09, line 256; C1-S03, "kV - Velocity Gain"]
13. Set a Smart Current Limit suited to the motor (REV suggests 40–60 A for the NEO) and persist it. [C1-S13, "Suggested Current Limits"]
14. Set all gains to zero and check motor direction so positive output moves the mechanism the intended way. [C1-S04, "Tuning", steps 1–3]
15. Identify kS, kV (and kA) with a quasistatic + dynamic test, forward and backward, on the ground (not on blocks), logging each drivetrain side as its own motor. [C1-S18, `creating-routine.rst` and `running-routine.rst`; C1-S20, `Drive.java` lines 61–74]
16. Check the fit: simulated-velocity r² above 0.9, sensible velocity threshold, and at least two steady-speed events plus one acceleration event. [C1-S18, `viewing-diagnostics.rst`; C1-S20, `FeedforwardAnalysis.cpp` lines 166–171]
17. Enter the controller's real velocity-measurement delay (e.g. 112 ms for default NEO hall settings) when computing feedback gains, and recalculate it if the filter settings change. [C1-S18, `analyzing-gains.rst`, "Measurement Delays"; C1-S20, `FeedbackControllerPreset.hpp` line 138]
18. Tune feedback on top of the feedforward: raise P until the output starts to oscillate, then back off (decrease P) or add a little D; avoid I, and if used bound it with I-zone / max accumulator. [C1-S22, "Closed-Loop Overview"; C1-S04, "Tuning", step 9; C1-S16, "Combined Feedforward and Feedback Control"; C1-S17, "Integral Term Windup"]
19. Choose velocity filter settings by trading coarseness (too short a period) against lag (too long a window). [C1-S23, "Changing Velocity Measurement Parameters"]
20. Use voltage compensation (or voltage-based output) so gains behave the same as battery voltage drops. [C1-S17, "Voltage Sag"; C1-S22, "Choosing Output Type"]
21. Graph setpoint and measured value during every tuning test. [C1-S04, "Tuning"]

## Common mistakes
- Assuming an inner loop is "perfect" in the outer-loop design without checking that it is fast enough; the approximation "may not be valid". [C1-S24, section 11.6, p. 342]
- Using a high proportional gain with a long sampling period, giving large overshoot and slow damping. [C1-S37, section 4.1, p. 904]
- Tuning from datasheet motor constants and ignoring transport delay and dead zone, which "must be obtained from measurements". [C1-S37, section 3.1, p. 902]
- Ignoring the delay added by sampling and filtering; a delay harms control more than a lag of equal size. [C1-S28, section 6.7; C1-S29, section 2.1]
- Relying on setpoint limits to prevent windup; this does not help with disturbance-induced windup. [C1-S28, section 6.5]
- Choosing a very small anti-windup tracking time constant in a loop with derivative action, so noise resets the integrator. [C1-S28, section 6.5]
- Identifying Coulomb friction in only one direction or with only one method; it was direction-dependent and differed 36% between step and ramp methods. [C1-S32, sections 4.1.6 and 4.1.9]
- Leaving powertrain dynamics out of a predictive vehicle model, which leaves "large prediction errors associated with transients". [C1-S35, section 4, p. 21]
- Using only offline-identified parameters on a vehicle whose payload changes. [C1-S33, section 2]
- Entering a pre-2026 `kF`/`velocityFF` value (duty cycle per RPM) as the 2026 `kV` (volts per RPM) without multiplying by the nominal voltage — REV's example changed the number by a factor of 12. [C1-S11, 2025 line 72 vs 2026 lines 74–75]
- Forgetting that kV/kA are per motor RPM by default, "prior to any gear ratio", when copying gains from calculators that use mechanism units. [C1-S03, "kV - Velocity Gain"]
- Setting only one conversion factor: the velocity factor is independent of the position factor. [C1-S02, "Velocity Conversion Factor"]
- Using the default SysId feedback gains after changing the controller's velocity filter; the measurement delay must be recalculated or the gains may be unstable. [C1-S18, "Measurement Delays"]
- Using PID-only velocity control; it cannot hold speed without a steady error. [C1-S16, "Issues with Feedback Control Alone"]
- Relying on integral gain to hide a bad feedforward, leading to windup and overshoot. [C1-S17, "Integral Term Windup"; C1-S24, section 10.4]
- Characterizing a drivetrain on blocks. [C1-S18, `running-routine.rst`]
- Running a NEO near stall at the default 80 A limit. REV suggests 40–60 A for the NEO. REV's numeric locked-rotor summary is for the NEO 550 (about 27 s to failure at 40 A, 5.5 s at 60 A, 2.0 s at 80 A); the full-size NEO results are published only as graphs and raw data. [C1-S13, "Suggested Current Limits"; C1-S14, NEO 550 tab, "Time to Failure Summary"]
- Setting P so high in MAXMotion Velocity that the loop outruns the acceleration target, giving jittery motion. [C1-S06, "Tips for Smooth Motions"]
- Assuming parameters set over CAN survive a power cycle without persisting/burning them to flash. [C1-S13, "Limiting Current"]
- Fitting a drive model to data logged while the speed loop is closed with ordinary least squares or spectral analysis: the input is correlated with the noise and the estimate is biased. [C1-S44, section 1]
- Treating anti-windup as a way to reduce step-response overshoot, or as the same thing as bumpless transfer. [C1-S41, introduction]
- Using datasheet thermal parameters and a fixed ambient temperature for motor temperature estimation; real parameters differ and drift with age. [C1-S46, sections I and II-A]
- Checking only total vehicle power on a skid-steered vehicle; one side's motor drive can saturate while the total is within limits. [C1-S51, section 5.2]

## Disagreements between sources
- **Does LuGre capture hysteresis?** Cardona Soto et al. state the LuGre model replicates "the Stribeck effect, hysteresis, spring-like properties associated with stiction, and variations in break-away force" [C1-S32, section 2.6], while Bona & Indri state it "does not take into account possible hysteresis effects" [C1-S31, section II]. The original LuGre paper sides with Cardona Soto et al.: it claims hysteresis and shows a velocity–friction hysteresis loop that widens with frequency [C1-S40, abstract and section IV-B, Fig. 3, p. 422].
- **Is Coulomb plus viscous friction enough?** Cardona Soto et al. find it explains more than 98% of variance and is adequate above about 5 rad/s [C1-S32, section 4.1.4]; Bona & Indri report that pure Coulomb plus viscous compensation "cannot provide accurate tracking and positioning results" in servo positioning [C1-S31, section III-A]. The two concern different tasks (speed regulation vs precise positioning).
- **Ramp slope for friction identification.** Cardona Soto et al. derive that the ramp slope must be small (an upper bound) and say an earlier source's requirement that it be "large enough" leads to overestimating Coulomb friction [C1-S32, section 3.2]; the earlier source was not downloaded.
- **How much integral action?** General PID practice treats PI as the default ("most loops are actually PI control") and SIMC always includes integral action [C1-S28, section 6.1; C1-S29, section 3.3], while REV and WPILib discourage kI in FRC velocity loops in favour of accurate feedforward [C1-S04; C1-S17]. The FRC sources assume a good feedforward model is available.
- **Derivative action in velocity loops (general view).** SIMC recommends D only for dominant second-order dynamics and an agricultural-robot study prefers PI when delay exceeds τ/2 [C1-S29, section 3.3; C1-S37, section 2.2]; this sides with WPILib/Veness against the REV/CTRE procedures that add D (see the product-specific item below).
- **Name and units of parameter ID 16.** The SPARK MAX parameter reference page still lists ID 16 as "kF_0 — Feed Forward gain constant" with no units [C1-S08, row kF_0], while REVLib 2026 names the same ID `kV_0` and documents it as volts per velocity [C1-S10, `SparkParameters.java` line 52; `FeedForwardConfig.java` line 81]. REV's own example code implies the value's meaning changed by a factor of the nominal voltage (12 V) between 2025 and 2026 [C1-S11].
- **Quadrature velocity defaults.** REVLib 2026 source and the parameter page say 100 ms / 64 samples [C1-S10, lines 118–136; C1-S08], while REVLib 2027 alpha 7 changes the defaults to 8 samples and sample delta 20 [C1-S09, line 474].
- **Is D useful in a velocity loop?** REV's general procedure adds D to damp oscillation [C1-S04], CTRE says increase kD "as much as possible without introducing jittering" [C1-S22], while WPILib says kD is not useful for a constant velocity setpoint [C1-S16] and Veness shows it is unnecessary for an ideal flywheel model [C1-S27, 6.10.7].
- **How to measure kS manually.** WPILib: largest voltage before motion begins [C1-S16]; REV: smallest output that causes motion, then decreased slightly [C1-S03]; CTRE: increase until "just before the motor moves" [C1-S22]. All describe the breakaway voltage, approached slightly differently.
- **Which anti-windup scheme is best?** Åström recommends back-calculation with a tracking time constant Tt between Td and Ti (e.g. √(Ti·Td)) [C1-S28, section 6.5]; Peng, Vrančić and Hanus conclude that the conditioning technique with the realizable reference is the most suitable "for usual applications", with a tunable compromise when limits are very restrictive [C1-S41, abstract]; FRC sources use integrator zones and caps [C1-S17; C1-S10]. Only the abstract and introduction of Peng et al. were available, so their comparison itself was not read.
- **Fixed overload time vs thermal model.** AC frequency inverters commonly allow a fixed overload (e.g. 110% or 160%) for a set time, while maxon argues this does not suit compact servo motors and uses an I²t model instead [C1-S47, sections 1–2]; REV's Smart Current Limit holds phase current at a configured limit (optionally scaled with speed), and REV publishes locked-rotor times to failure rather than a thermal model [C1-S10, `SparkBaseConfig.java` lines 213–278; C1-S14].

## Open questions
- No open source found gives a general quantitative rule for how much faster a wheel velocity loop must be than a trajectory or navigation controller above it; the only number found is the 10× in Åström & Murray's worked example [C1-S24].
- No peer-reviewed source found reports velocity-loop performance (tracking error, bandwidth) for tracked or skid-steer vehicles on grass. The tracked sources found report the loop structure and path error on wet soil without wheel-speed metrics [C1-S49] and motor currents on sand, mud, gravel and asphalt [C1-S50]; the skid-steer power study used a lab vinyl surface [C1-S51]; the rover, AGV and agricultural-robot sources are wheeled [C1-S34; C1-S33; C1-S37]. Searches with direct terms (tracked, skid-steer, grass, track speed, PID) and general terms (terrain-dependent load disturbance, traction estimation, slip compensation) found nothing more specific.
- The Hall-sensor review is aimed at PMSM field-oriented control and does not quantify speed-estimate error or delay versus speed for a given sensor count [C1-S36].
- The peer-reviewed friction-compensation surveys Armstrong-Hélouvry et al. (1994) and Olsson et al. (1998) remain undownloaded (paywall; Lund repository behind a bot check); friction findings rely on Bona & Indri [C1-S31], Cardona Soto et al. [C1-S32], a TU/e traineeship review that summarizes them, van Geffen [C1-S26], and (added 2026-09-28) the original LuGre paper [C1-S40].
- A peer-reviewed open survey comparing conditional integration with back-calculation on motor speed loops was not obtained; anti-windup findings come from Åström's text [C1-S28], the modern-design tutorial [C1-S30] and the abstract and introduction of Peng, Vrančić & Hanus [C1-S41]. The originating papers (Fertik & Ross 1967; Hanus et al. 1987) have no open copy.
- REV's units table gives kP/kI/kD only in position form (per rotation). No REV source found states the velocity-mode units of kP/kI/kD explicitly (presumably duty cycle per RPM of error, etc.), nor whether kI's "per ms" factor applies in velocity mode.
- No REV source found explains how firmware 26 converts the volt-based feedforward to duty cycle — whether it divides by measured bus voltage (implicit voltage compensation) or by a fixed nominal value, and how this interacts with `voltageCompensation`.
- REV does not document the exact hall-sensor velocity algorithm (how edges within the 32 ms window are counted or interpolated), so the effective velocity resolution at low speed cannot be derived from the sources. The new "firmware filtering system" announced for 2027 is not yet documented.
- No source found gives SPARK-specific guidance on setting the closed-loop ramp rate for a drivetrain velocity loop, or on how the ramp interacts with the feedforward.
- No open source was found for Kessler's symmetrical optimum or modulus optimum, the standard tuning rules for cascaded current and speed loops in electrical drives; the current/torque inner loop of a drive and its bandwidth relative to the speed loop therefore remain covered only indirectly (C1-S22 on torque-current output). Searched as symmetrical/modulus optimum, cascade drive tuning, and servo-drive speed-loop tuning.
- No general (non-vendor, non-FRC) source was found on how a supply-voltage drop below the voltage the loop demands interacts with integral action; the general windup literature covers it only as actuator saturation [C1-S28; C1-S30]. The only voltage-compensation source outside FRC is an application note for mains-fed AC drives [C1-S48].
- Physical models of PM DC/BLDC motors from a drives textbook (Leonhard, Krishnan) were not obtained; motor physics is covered only through the FRC feedforward derivation [C1-S15; C1-S27] and first-order identification models [C1-S37].
- No general source was found on measuring the closed-loop bandwidth of a speed loop (frequency sweep procedure and pass criteria).
