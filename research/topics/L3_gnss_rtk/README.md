## Summary
- Differencing rover and base measurements reduces or eliminates clock and hardware errors; residual atmospheric and orbit errors stay small only while the baseline is short (NGS: ≤ 20 km, with about 1 ppm growth per km); after differencing and ambiguity resolution, multipath remains unmodelled in the position error. [L3-S11, pp. 22, 28, 32, 66; L3-S37, p. 25]
- Resolving the integer carrier-phase ambiguities turns a float solution with a spread of several metres into a fixed solution with a spread of about 1 cm (a repeated 5 s, two-epoch experiment in the LAMBDA paper; the paper's precision example in the same section uses a 2.2 km dual-frequency baseline with seven satellites, and that the experiment used the same set-up is implied, not stated). [L3-S03, p. 3]
- Time to fix and fix reliability depend on baseline length, multipath and satellite visibility at rover and base; manufacturers quote "1 cm + 1 ppm" style accuracy, and NGS recommends single-base baselines of ≤ 20 km and correction latency ≤ 2 s. [L3-S01, p. 18; L3-S11, pp. 28, 42, 44]
- The antenna is often the limiting component: a smartphone-grade antenna loses on average 11 dB C/N0 against a survey-grade one and its multipath is the main barrier to fast ambiguity resolution; u-blox says specified performance needs the strongest satellites at ≥ 44 dBHz. [L3-S23, pp. 1, 5; L3-S02, p. 5]
- Wrong fixes do happen: an urban vehicle RTK study measured a 2.4 % per-epoch wrong-fix probability even though the validation test was set for 0.1 %, and rising to 7.7 % on a 15 km baseline; the popular fixed-threshold ratio test does not test correctness and should be replaced by a fixed-failure-rate test. [L3-S22, pp. 8–9; L3-S05, pp. 3, 12; L3-S06, p. 11]
- RTK is fragile on the road: over 30,000 km of highway driving, RTK fixed was available in 49.9 % of epochs, and 95th-percentile RTK outages could exceed a minute, versus < 7 s for standalone code positioning. [L3-S21, pp. 14–16]
- In ROS, `navsat_transform_node` removes the antenna offset using the `base_link`→GNSS frame transform named by `NavSatFix.header.frame_id`; it accepts every fix whose status is not `STATUS_NO_FIX` and copies the receiver covariance, and `NavSatStatus` has no RTK FIXED/FLOAT values. [L3-S29, `navsat_transform.cpp` lines 517–561, 606–652; L3-S30, `NavSatStatus.msg` lines 7–10]

## Findings

### 1. GNSS positioning fundamentals and error budget
- Code-based (pseudorange) positioning gives positions within a few metres; carrier-phase RTK uses the carrier wave (L1 at 1575.42 MHz, about 19 cm wavelength) and gives ranges "orders of magnitude more precise". [L3-S09, pp. 52–53]
- NovAtel's pseudorange error budget per satellite: satellite clocks ±2 m, orbit errors ±2.5 m, ionospheric delays ±5 m, tropospheric delays ±0.5 m, receiver noise ±0.3 m, multipath ±1 m. [L3-S09, p. 33, Table 1]
- Position error ≈ DOP × range error (dilution of precision, DOP, is a number describing satellite geometry); NovAtel states that a DOP above 6 gives generally unacceptable accuracy for DGPS and RTK. [L3-S09, p. 54]
- PDOP² = HDOP² + VDOP² and GDOP² = PDOP² + TDOP²; more satellites generally give smaller DOP; vertical DOP exceeds horizontal because all satellites are above the receiver, and the gap grows at high latitudes because GPS orbits are inclined about 55°. [L3-S08, pp. 3–4]
- The GPS SPS Performance Standard commits to ≤ 8 m horizontal and ≤ 13 m vertical 95 % global-average position error, and ≤ 15 m / ≤ 33 m at the worst site. [L3-S10, p. 68, Table 3.8-3]
- These SPS numbers exclude errors outside the space and control segments, including residual ionospheric and tropospheric errors, receiver noise and interference, multipath and user antenna effects. [L3-S10, p. 42, §2.4.5]
- Differencing between receivers reduces or eliminates satellite and receiver clock errors and hardware errors; residual orbit and atmospheric errors grow with baseline length, which manufacturers model as about 1 ppm (1 mm per km). [L3-S11, pp. 22, 32]
- After differencing and ambiguity resolution, the multipath error is still unmodelled and remains in the position error. [L3-S11, p. 66]
- Xsens states the same for differential RTK: it compensates satellite clock, orbit, ionosphere and troposphere errors but does not compensate multipath. [L3-S37, p. 25]
- An RTK rover outputs coordinates in the reference frame of the correction source; a datum transformation is needed if another frame is required, and when the receiver drops from corrected to standalone/SBAS solutions the output frame switches too (SBAS is aligned within a few cm of WGS84/ITRF). [L3-S01, pp. 17, 37, 115]
- Precise Point Positioning (PPP) uses one receiver plus precise satellite orbit and clock corrections, with no nearby reference station and no baseline-length limit; its main drawback is a long solution convergence time that "can range from tens of minutes to several hours". [L3-S49, pp. 2–3]
- For static PPP, the recommended convergence periods are 35 minutes for 20 cm, 50 minutes for 10 cm and 60 minutes for 5 cm horizontal accuracy, and convergence is much longer for kinematic processing. [L3-S49, pp. 4–5, Table 1]
- Even when external ionosphere maps are used to help PPP ambiguity fixing after a cold start, fixing the initial ambiguities "still requires a considerable time (more than 10 minutes)". [L3-S49, p. 8]

### 2. Carrier-phase ambiguity resolution theory
- Historical origin: in 1985 Remondi reported two kinematic surveys with TI-4100 receivers showing that centimetre-level relative surveys "can be performed in seconds", without needing a priori coordinates of the roving antenna; at the time, static carrier-phase surveys of 10–100 km baselines took 1 to 3 hours. [L3-S52, PDF p. 5 (printed p. 1)]
- Double-differenced carrier-phase measurements are ambiguous by an unknown integer number of cycles; LAMBDA solves this in steps: (1) a float least-squares solution ignoring integerness, (2) an integer least-squares search for the ambiguities, (3) adjustment of the baseline with the fixed integers to give the fixed solution. [L3-S03, pp. 1–2]
- With short observation spans the float baseline is poor because the ambiguities are hard to separate from the coordinates. The paper's formal-precision example (Fig. 1) uses a 2.2 km baseline with dual-frequency data to seven satellites; in a separate experiment repeated 100 times with two epochs 5 s apart (Fig. 2), the float scatter was several metres and the fixed scatter about 1 cm. The paper does not restate the baseline for Fig. 2; linking it to the 2.2 km set-up is an inference. [L3-S03, p. 3]
- LAMBDA decorrelates the ambiguities with an integer transformation, which flattens and lowers the spectrum of conditional variances and makes the integer search efficient. [L3-S03, pp. 1, 5]
- Integer rounding, integer bootstrapping and integer least squares (ILS) are members of one class of estimators; ILS has the highest success rate of all admissible estimators, and the bootstrapped success rate, easy to compute from the float covariance, is a sharp lower bound on the ILS success rate when applied to decorrelated ambiguities. [L3-S04, pp. 1, 5, 8]
- Partial ambiguity resolution fixes only the most precise subset of ambiguities, adding ambiguities in order of precision until the success rate becomes unacceptably small. [L3-S04, p. 8]
- A cycle slip is a jump of an integer number of cycles in the carrier-phase count, generally caused by a break in tracking. [L3-S09, p. 81]
- TurboEdit detects and corrects cycle slips automatically from undifferenced dual-frequency data using wide-lane and ionospheric combinations; it was tested on data with over 2500 cycle slips and needed analyst intervention on 1 % of passes. [L3-S07, pp. 1–2]
- The wide-lane combination (L1−L2) has a 0.862 m wavelength and is easier to resolve, but adds about 6 times the noise; the narrow lane (L1+L2, 0.107 m) is hard to resolve but helps detect cycle slips and validate the fix; the ionosphere-free combination is noisier and is used on long baselines. [L3-S11, p. 22]
- After a loss of fix, a new integer search is much faster if two or more satellites have been tracked continuously; a complete loss of lock restarts ambiguity resolution from the beginning. [L3-S11, p. 23]
- RTKLIB offers "Continuous", "Instantaneous" (epoch-by-epoch) and "Fix and Hold" modes; Fix and Hold tightly constrains the ambiguities to validated integers and was introduced to improve fixing ratio for moving receivers. [L3-S16, pp. 40, 169]
- Single-epoch integer resolution is insensitive to cycle slips between epochs, which are common in urban driving; carrying integer-conditioned states forward is "perilous" because one wrong fix can corrupt the state and make the next fixes wrong for several seconds. [L3-S22, p. 6]

### 3. What drives RTK fix quality and time to fix
- Single-base RTK assumes base and rover see nearly the same atmosphere, so baselines should be kept relatively short (≤ 20 km); atmospheric noise can prevent initialization or cause a wrong fix, and near solar maximum users can expect inability to initialize. [L3-S11, p. 28]
- NGS recommends an elevation mask of 10°–15° (10° recommended); low satellites have 3–5 times more noise, but masks above 15° lose too much data and can raise PDOP. [L3-S11, pp. 32–33]
- u-blox: the convergence time from RTK float to RTK fixed "is affected by the baseline length as well as by multipath and satellite visibility at both rover and base station". [L3-S01, p. 18]
- In a network-RTK test, a 16 km baseline with network corrections initialized about as fast as single-baseline RTK under 10 km, while single-base 16 km and 32 km baselines had the worst time-to-initialize. [L3-S12, pp. 6–7]
- In an urban vehicle study, lengthening the reference baseline from under 4 km to 15 km cut the correct-fix rate from 84.8 % to 78.9 % and raised the wrong-fix rate from 2.4 % to 7.7 %. [L3-S22, pp. 8–9, Table I]
- In the same study, removing Galileo E1 cut validated-fix availability from 87.2 % to 77.4 %, and removing SBAS signals cut it to 78.6 %. [L3-S22, p. 9, Table I]
- Signals were screened at C/N0 ≥ 37.5 dB-Hz, phase-lock statistic ≥ 0.5 and elevation ≥ 15° before entering the RTK solution. [L3-S22, p. 8]
- Correction age of data (latency) from 0 to 1000 ms barely changed availability (87.2 % to 87.0 %) but raised the wrong-fix rate from 2.4 % to 5.0 %. [L3-S22, p. 9, Table I]
- With a low-quality or smartphone antenna, gentle wavelength-scale random antenna motion (2–5 cm/s, a hand-moved antenna rather than vehicle driving) shortened time to ambiguity resolution (for the smartphone antenna the 90 %-success time fell from 400 s to 215 s) because motion decorrelates multipath errors; for a survey-grade antenna motion lengthened it. [L3-S23, p. 9]
- Static multipath errors have decorrelation times of hundreds of seconds (100–200 s for a static antenna), which lengthens time to fix. [L3-S23, pp. 1, 9]
- A single-frequency low-cost RTK receiver (u-blox LEA-4T + RTKLIB) on a 6 km baseline with 5–8 satellites fixed 56.9 % of epochs, with fixed-solution RMS errors of 3.0 cm E-W, 4.9 cm N-S and 7.6 cm U-D, and some mis-fixes. [L3-S17, p. 6]
- Low-cost u-blox receivers using L1 GPS + B1 BeiDou can reach ambiguity resolution and positioning performance competitive with survey-grade dual-frequency GPS receivers. [L3-S24, p. 1]
- Foliage attenuates GNSS signals, usually expressed in dB per metre of foliage, depending on tree type and height; masking causes fading, multipath and blockage. [L3-S25, p. 6]
- The MTi-680G specification gives an RTK convergence time of < 10 s, with the footnote "< 30 s for GPS only"; the constellation set behind the 10 s value is not stated for this row (the "GPS + GLONASS + Galileo + BeiDou" footnote on the same page belongs to cold-start acquisition). [L3-S32, p. 15]
- Ionospheric activity: on an 8.9 km baseline, the single-epoch integer-least-squares success rate of a low-cost single-frequency 4-constellation receiver was 100 %, 99.1 % and 68.2 % for low (mean Kp-index 0+), medium (3o) and high (5o) geomagnetic disturbance; a dual-frequency GPS survey receiver gave 100 %, 99.4 % and 82.5 %. (Kp is a three-hourly index of global geomagnetic disturbance running from 0o to 9o; 7 marks a strong storm.) [L3-S47, pp. 2, 15–16, Fig. 13]
- When residual ionospheric delays grow, instantaneous (single-epoch) RTK is not possible for any of the tested receivers and a multi-epoch model is needed; at the high disturbance level both receiver types needed on average about 2 min to first fix, and about 5 min when Kp reached 7−. [L3-S47, pp. 1, 19]
- In calm ionospheric conditions (Kp 1+), a 21.8 km single baseline still allowed instantaneous ambiguity resolution with 100 % success rate for both receiver types. [L3-S47, p. 19]
- Obstruction: in network-RTK tests at seven stations ranging from open sky to tree branches overhead, trees plus a hill, and an urban canyon, a ZED-F9P with a low-cost patch antenna gave centimetre accuracy "only in conditions with sufficient horizon exposure"; the same receiver with a geodetic antenna gave clearly better results. [L3-S48, pp. 1, 5, 16]

### 4. Antenna design, placement and the RF chain
- A smartphone-grade antenna lost on average 11 dB C/N0 relative to a survey-grade antenna (about 8 % of the signal power), while a low-quality patch lost about 0.6 dB; double-difference phase residuals were 3.4, 5.5 and 11.4 mm for survey-grade, low-quality patch and smartphone antennas. [L3-S23, pp. 5–6]
- Pesyna et al. conclude that poor multipath suppression of the antenna, not the chipset, is the primary impediment to fast integer ambiguity resolution with low-cost hardware. [L3-S23, pp. 1, 9–10]
- For low-cost u-blox receivers, the BeiDou B1 code standard deviation was 74 cm with a patch antenna versus 33 cm with a Trimble Zephyr geodetic antenna, attributed to better multipath suppression. [L3-S24, p. 4]
- In an urban-vehicle RTK system, calibrating the rover antenna's phase-centre variation reduced the standard deviation of L1 and L2 undifferenced carrier-phase residuals by 11 % and 15 % in open-sky conditions; running the system without the calibration raised the wrong-fix rate from 2.4 % to 4.2 %. [L3-S22, pp. 8–9]
- Radomes can shift the antenna phase centre by several cm, an effect that was "more or less ignored up to November 2006". [L3-S26, p. 3]
- u-blox: specified performance requires the average C/N0 (carrier-to-noise density ratio) of the strongest satellites to reach at least 44 dBHz; well-designed systems reach 44–50 dBHz for high satellites, and 47 dBHz "should easily be achieved" with an off-the-shelf active antenna. [L3-S02, p. 5]
- Under open sky a good design should reach up to 50 dBHz; 45 dBHz is acceptable but should be investigated; designs whose strongest signals stay below 40 dBHz usually perform poorly (long TTFF, lower accuracy). [L3-S02, p. 32]
- Patch antennas should be parallel to the horizon with full sky view; windows and roofs reduce the signal and obstruct the sky, and the antenna should be as far as possible from radiating or jamming sources. [L3-S02, p. 6]
- Patch ground plane: a good allowance is 50 × 50 to 70 × 70 mm²; small ground planes leave a back lobe that picks up ground-reflected multipath, and larger ground planes reduce this. [L3-S02, p. 17]
- In one u-blox comparison, a chip antenna on an 80 × 40 mm board averaged 43.4 dBHz and one on a 24 × 15 mm board averaged 34.7 dBHz. [L3-S02, p. 10]
- Active antennas (with a built-in low-noise amplifier, LNA) are advisable when the cable exceeds about 10 cm; for u-blox receivers LNA gain above 26 dB is not needed and above 35 dB with short cables may overload some receivers. [L3-S02, p. 7]
- The ZED-F9P requires an active antenna: gain 17–50 dB including passive losses, noise figure < 4 dB, phase-centre variation < 10 mm, and a suitable ground plane; unsuitable vehicle locations include under the dash or at the rear-view mirror. [L3-S01, pp. 90–91]
- Xsens recommends a metal ground plane of at least 150 mm diameter under the RTK antenna to help reject multipath, and says the antenna "must be properly fixed with respect to the MTi". [L3-S37, p. 25]
- GNSS signals arrive at about −128 dBm, below the −111 dBm thermal noise floor, so nearby RF sources matter; digital equipment (PCs, cameras, LCD screens) emits broadband noise up to several GHz that falls in the GNSS band. [L3-S01, pp. 98–99]
- In-band interference mitigations: grounding, shielding, layout, low-pass filtering of noise sources, placing the antenna far from noise sources, and band-pass filtering; out-of-band (LTE, Wi-Fi, BT) needs a GNSS band-pass filter. [L3-S01, p. 99]
- The ZED-F9P reports jamming and interference and has a low-resolution spectrum analyzer (UBX-MON-SPAN) for comparing the RF spectrum during integration. [L3-S01, pp. 69, 83]
- NGS lists as multipath sources to avoid: tree canopy, structures within 30 m higher than the antenna, nearby vehicles, metal objects, water bodies and signs. [L3-S11, p. 37]
- Tallysman (antenna manufacturer): a ceramic patch should be mounted horizontally on a ground plane ideally 100–120 mm across; a circular, symmetric, unbroken plane is best for axial ratio, improving rejection of cross-polarised (LHCP) signals and mitigating multipath, and a larger plane can reduce gain through diffraction at its edges. [L3-S51, pp. 1–2]
- Tallysman: the coaxial cable can act as a reflector, so it should be routed underneath the ground plane; GNSS antennas close to other antennas can be detuned or interfered with, so separate, isolated ground planes are advised. [L3-S51, p. 2]
- Janos & Kuras attribute the better results of a geodetic antenna on a ZED-F9P to its smaller phase-centre variations and more efficient multipath reduction than a low-cost patch. [L3-S48, p. 16]

### 5. Correction sources: single base, networks and CORS
- Network RTK uses several reference stations to model regional systematic errors, which extends distance to the reference, improves reliability and shortens initialization. [L3-S12, p. 1]
- In the Virtual Reference Station (VRS) method the rover sends its approximate position as an NMEA GGA string; the server creates corrections that appear to come from a station a few metres from the rover. [L3-S12, p. 2]
- VRS needs two-way communication; broadcast network formats (such as FKP area corrections) send the error terms one-way. [L3-S12, pp. 1–2]
- NovAtel describes VRS as reducing the number of RTK base stations needed. [L3-S09, p. 53]
- Manufacturers and ISO testing state RTK accuracies of about 1 cm + 1 ppm horizontal and 2 cm + 1 ppm vertical (1 sigma). [L3-S11, p. 42]
- NGS accuracy classes tie baselines to precision: RT1 (baselines ≤ 10 km, PDOP ≤ 2.0, ≥ 7 satellites) gives 0.01–0.02 m horizontal at 95 %; RT4 (any baseline if fixed, PDOP ≤ 6.0, ≥ 5 satellites) gives 0.1–0.2 m. [L3-S11, pp. 50–51]
- u-blox distinguishes observation-space RTK (OSR, RTCM corrections) from state-space PPP-RTK (SSR, SPARTN or CLAS corrections). [L3-S01, p. 7]
- The ZED-F9P warns when the baseline exceeds 100 km (50 km with firmware HPG 1.12). [L3-S01, p. 20]
- Base coordinates propagate into the rover: the RTK vector from base to rover is applied to the base coordinates, so the rover's position "can never by RT practice be more accurate than that of the base". [L3-S11, pp. 20, 40]
- u-blox: the accuracy of the configured base coordinates affects the rover's absolute accuracy; a base in fixed-position mode issues the warning "Base station position seems incorrect" when its configured coordinates are wrong by more than about 50 m (up to 25 km). [L3-S01, p. 21]
- In a real-time network, datum readjustments or changes of reference frame (e.g. to ITRF) are done at the network level and broadcast to users. [L3-S11, p. 56]

### 6. Correction transport: RTCM 3 and NTRIP
- NTRIP is an application-level protocol based on HTTP/1.1 with three parts: NtripServer (sends data), NtripCaster (the HTTP server) and NtripClient (receives data); it runs over TCP/IP and so works on mobile IP networks. [L3-S13, p. 3]
- The caster is adapted to low-bandwidth streams of 50–500 bytes/s per stream. [L3-S13, p. 7]
- A client requests a stream by its mountpoint; requesting an unavailable mountpoint makes the caster reply with the source-table (list of streams) instead of data. [L3-S13, p. 10]
- Mountpoints may be protected by HTTP Basic authentication (user:password in base64) or Digest authentication, which avoids sending the password in the clear. [L3-S13, pp. 10–11]
- If the source-table field `nmea` = 1, the caster must receive at least one NMEA GGA string before it starts sending; clients may send further GGA at any time (used for VRS or nearest-station selection). [L3-S13, pp. 11, 14]
- The original NTRIP paper gives typical stream rates (2005) of about 5 kbit/s for RTK corrections in RTCM and about 0.5 kbit/s for DGNSS corrections, and notes that NTRIP's use of HTTP "slightly differs from the HTTP standard" and that, although it works with many proxy servers, "their use should be avoided when possible". [L3-S38, p. 3]
- The ZED-F9P accepts RTCM 3.4 input including MSM4/5/7 observation messages for GPS (1074/1075/1077), GLONASS (1084/85/87), Galileo (1094/95/97) and BeiDou (1124/25/27), reference station position 1005/1006, antenna description 1033 and GLONASS biases 1230. [L3-S01, pp. 17–18]
- The rover needs both observations (MSM4 or MSM7) and station position (1005 or 1006) to attempt fixes; the station ID must match between them; GLONASS ambiguities stay float unless 1230 or 1033 is present. [L3-S01, pp. 18–19]
- Xsens states the MTi-680G supports RTCM v3 "with a standard 1Hz frequency". [L3-S34, p. 32]
- In a 2009 field test, the NTRIP base-station input to RTKLIB used 1.1–1.4 kbps. [L3-S17, p. 5]
- The MTi-680G RTCM input port outputs a GGA message at 1 Hz, "often required by NTRIP providers". [L3-S32, p. 20]
- u-blox recommends a base in default constellation configuration output RTCM 1005, 1074, 1084, 1094, 1124 (MSM4 for GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou) and 1230; all observation messages must be broadcast at the same rate, 1005/1006 may be sent less often (but the rover cannot compute a position until it has one), and MSM4 and MSM7 should not be mixed. [L3-S01, pp. 21–22]
- EUREF (European permanent network) expects observation messages at 1 Hz and recommends that station-metadata messages (1005, 1006, 1007, 1008, 1033) are streamed "with an update rate of at least 60 seconds or higher"; MSM7 is recommended as most precise, MSM5 acceptable when bandwidth is limited. [L3-S42, p. 17]
- IGS real-time stations should send 1-Hz data with a latency of less than two seconds; MSM7 is recommended, MSM4 or MSM5 acceptable if bandwidth is limited. [L3-S41, p. 10]
- NTRIP 2.0 (RTCM 10410.1, 2009) fixed design problems and HTTP protocol violations, replaced non-standard directives, added chunked transfer encoding, improved header records, and added source-table filtering and RTSP communication. [L3-S39, p. 2; L3-S40, section 3.1.2]
- NTRIP 2.0 is backwards compatible with 1.0 and can run over TCP/IP, TLS/SSL (usually port 443), RTSP/RTP or UDP; BNC notes that RTSP/UDP cut latency by "in the order of 0.5 sec or less" but are not accepted by proxy servers and may not be supported by mobile Internet providers, and advises NTRIP 2 over TCP when streams are blocked by a proxy. [L3-S40, sections 2.20.1.1.5, 3.1.2]
- NMEA GGA uploads for VRS streams may be blocked by a proxy, firewall or virus scanner when NTRIP 2 is not used, and "some VRS systems need GGA sentences at regular intervals". [L3-S40, sections 2.18.1, 2.10.11]

### 7. Correction delivery robustness: outages, latency and reconnection
- Reid et al. report that networked-RTK corrections are "typically" considered stale after 10 to 15 s; on highways 96.0 % of corrections were < 2 s old and 97.3 % < 10 s, and the rest could be minutes old due to cellular outages. [L3-S21, p. 15, Table 7]
- NGS: the displayed position can be up to 5 s old (typically 2–3 s at most) and it recommends latencies no greater than 2 s; intermittent radio/cellular links can degrade accuracy without failing. [L3-S11, pp. 44, 47]
- u-blox: the rover stops using RTCM corrections older than 60 s (default, configurable via CFG-NAVSPG-CONSTR_DGNSSTO) and drops to 3D or 3D/DGNSS mode, "to prevent the computation of grossly misleading differential solutions". [L3-S01, p. 19]
- RTKLIB's default maximum age of differential corrections is 30 s. [L3-S16, p. 41]
- NTRIP 1.0 notes that a lost TCP connection is detected by the sockets and can trigger automated reconnection. [L3-S13, p. 5]
- BKG's BNC client (current version 2.13.7) treats a stream as broken when no incoming data are detected for more than 20 s and reconnects after 1 s, then 2 s, 4 s and so on, doubling up to a maximum wait of 256 s; the historical BNC Version 1 manual documents the same rule. [L3-S40, section 2.11; L3-S14, p. 11]
- BNC buffers decoder outputs over a short time span to decide whether a stream is valid or corrupted, and can pass outage and corruption events to a shell script or batch file to alert the operator. [L3-S40, section 2.11; L3-S14, pp. 11–12]
- RTKLIB (v2.4.3-b34) disconnects a TCP/NTRIP client after an inactivity timeout (default 10000 ms) and reconnects at a fixed interval (default 10000 ms); a source-table reply to a mountpoint request is treated as "no mountpoint" and triggers reconnection. [L3-S15, lines 271–272, 1432–1436, 1626–1638]
- The MicroStrain ROS `ntrip_client` (tag ros2-1.4.1) reconnects if no RTCM arrives for 4 s, after 5 zero-byte reads, or after 5 failed GGA sends; it retries every 5 s for up to 10 attempts, then raises an exception. [L3-S18, `ntrip_client.py` lines 28, 58–60, 182–197, 220–229; `ntrip_base.py` lines 10–11, 48–62]
- It treats a "SOURCETABLE 200 OK" reply as a probable invalid mountpoint and a "401" as bad credentials, and checks the RTCM 3 CRC-24 of every frame before publishing it. [L3-S18, `ntrip_client.py` lines 13–23, 116–128; `rtcm_parser.py` lines 78–94]
- The Xsens ROS 2 NTRIP client (commit e145fb5) sends an `Ntrip-Version: Ntrip/2.0` request, accepts any response line containing "200", enables TCP keep-alive, retries every `reconnect_delay` (default 5 s, unlimited attempts), and reconnects on read errors or server close; the file has no timer for "connected but no RTCM received". [L3-S19, `ntrip_client.cpp` lines 218, 265, 304–316, 328–362, 413–421; `ntrip-param.yaml`]
- It forwards only `$GPGGA`/`$GNGGA` sentences, decimated from `nmea_input_rate` (default 4 Hz) to `update_rate` (default 1 Hz); GGA send failures are logged but not treated as fatal. [L3-S19, `ntrip_client.cpp` lines 365–410; `ntrip-param.yaml`]
- NTRIP's developers reported that latencies below two seconds are typical over the Internet and GSM/GPRS networks in Germany and Europe, "so low that no perceptible influence on the achievable positioning accuracy exists", while "considerably delayed, missing or irregularly arriving correction data entail a lack of accuracy". [L3-S38, pp. 3–4]
- EUREF asks station operators to restore the data flow after a communication outage "as quickly as possible, preferable using an automated procedure". [L3-S42, p. 17]
- For operator alarms, BNC 2.13.7 reports a failure or corruption event only after it lasts longer than a "Failure threshold" whose default is 15 minutes. [L3-S40, section 2.11.2]

### 8. Solution types, accuracy and how they are reported
- Fixed RTK gives cm-level, float gives dm-level and standard code positioning gives m-level errors; on highways only 55 % of positions were better than 10 cm horizontal. [L3-S21, p. 9]
- Highway position modes: RTK fixed 49.9 %, RTK float 14.1 %, differential code 0.3 %, standard positioning 33.0 %, none 2.7 %. [L3-S21, p. 15, Table 7]
- Solution fragility is inversely related to accuracy; median RTK fixed outages were 11 s versus 2 s for float, and at the 95th percentile RTK outages exceeded a minute versus < 7 s for standalone. [L3-S21, p. 16]
- RTK fixed availability of 50 % came with a probability of continuity loss of 0.54 over 4 s, versus 0.045 for standard code positioning at 98 % availability. [L3-S21, p. 19]
- The MTi-680G specifies RTK horizontal position 0.01 m + 1 ppm CEP (< 0.05 m + 1 ppm in the dynamic column, car at 25 m/s), vertical 0.1 m + 1 ppm, and standalone (PVT) horizontal 1.5 m; RTK numbers were measured with a 1 km baseline and patch antennas on good ground planes and exclude antenna phase-centre offset errors. [L3-S32, pp. 13, 15; L3-S33, p. 14]
- NMEA GGA quality is 4 for RTK fixed and 5 for RTK float; UBX-NAV-PVT `carrSoln` is 1 for float and 2 for fixed; GGA also carries age of corrections and base station ID. [L3-S01, p. 19]
- RTKLIB solution files use Q = 1 fix, 2 float, 4 DGPS, 5 single. [L3-S16, p. 105]
- ROS `sensor_msgs/NavSatStatus` defines only NO_FIX (−1), FIX (0), SBAS_FIX (1) and GBAS_FIX (2), so RTK fixed and float have no distinct value. [L3-S30, `NavSatStatus.msg` lines 7–10]
- ROS drivers encode RTK in `NavSatStatus` differently: the KumarRobotics u-blox driver (3.0.0) reports `STATUS_FIX` for any valid 2D/3D fix including RTK float and `STATUS_GBAS_FIX` only when the carrier-phase solution is fixed, and fills a diagonal covariance from the receiver's horizontal and vertical accuracy estimates (squared, m²). [L3-S43, `ublox_firmware7plus.hpp` lines 92–111; `NavPVT.msg` lines 55–61]
- The Septentrio driver (v1.4.8) maps DGPS, RTK fixed, RTK float, moving-base RTK and PPP all to `STATUS_GBAS_FIX` in `NavSatFix` (SBAS → `STATUS_SBAS_FIX`); in its ROS 2 `gps_msgs/GPSFix` output it uses separate `STATUS_RTK_FIX` and `STATUS_RTK_FLOAT` values. [L3-S44, `message_handler.cpp` lines 1381–1437, 1613–1631]
- `NavSatFix.position_covariance` is in m², in an East-North-Up tangent plane; if only DOP is available, the message comment says to approximate the covariance from it and set the type accordingly. [L3-S30, `NavSatFix.msg` lines 28–45]
- NGS notes that data-collector RMS values measure precision, not accuracy, and that under multipath the firmware "will continue to display the false precision as if multipath was not present". [L3-S11, pp. 37, 39]
- REP-105: the `map` frame may jump discretely as new observations arrive, while the `odom` frame must be continuous. [L3-S31, sections "odom", "map"]

### 9. Integrity: validation, false fixes and treatment by navigation software
- The ratio test is not a test of the correctness of the integer solution: adding any integer vector to the float solution leaves its outcome unchanged, so integer biases can pass unnoticed. [L3-S05, p. 3]
- Fixed critical values (commonly 3, but 1.5, 2 and 5–10 have been proposed) have no rigorous basis; the failure rate then varies with model strength and from epoch to epoch in kinematic use. [L3-S05, pp. 3–5]
- With a fixed critical value, strong models get unnecessarily high false-alarm rates and weak models get high failure rates; Verhagen & Teunissen propose the fixed-failure-rate ratio test with look-up tables instead. [L3-S06, pp. 1, 4, 11]
- The fixed-failure-rate approach belongs to integer aperture estimation, which guarantees the failure rate does not exceed a user-set value while shortening time to first fix. [L3-S05, pp. 1, 7]
- RTKLIB's ratio test uses a fixed threshold (default 3.0) and the manual notes it "only supports a fixed threshold value". [L3-S16, pp. 40, 168]
- There is an unavoidable trade-off between probability of correct fix (P_S) and wrong fix (P_F): widening the acceptance region raises both. [L3-S22, p. 4]
- An urban RTK system with the aperture test set for a 0.1 % failure rate measured P_F = 2.4 % (24 times the target), and disabling data-bit prediction raised P_F to 25 % [L3-S22, p. 8]; the authors explain that multipath, foliage and other urban impairments make the empirical P_F exceed the target when the threshold is set under the usual Gaussian error assumptions, and note that urban errors have thick-tailed distributions. [L3-S22, pp. 4, 6]
- Most manufacturers state 99.9 % confidence for an on-the-fly fix; NGS warns that a fix taking much longer than normal may be a wrong fix and that the data collector gives no specific indication beyond higher RMS. [L3-S11, pp. 23, 47]
- Multipath can cause wrong ambiguity fixes with gross errors, especially vertical; height errors above 2 dm have been seen. [L3-S11, p. 37]
- u-blox offers a conservative ambiguity-fix mode (RTK_CAR, firmware HPG 1.50+) that trades longer time to first fix or more float epochs for "near absolute certainty" when fixed. [L3-S01, p. 19]
- A protection level (PL) bounds the true position error at a target misleading-information risk (TMIR); if the PL exceeds the application's alert limit, the system should change mode (stop, slow, reverse) or declare itself unavailable, and PL values must not be used unless the validity flags are set. [L3-S01, pp. 43–45]
- For robotic lawn mowers, u-blox describes reducing speed near boundaries when the PL rises, and stopping or backing up if no valid PL is available. [L3-S01, p. 46]
- Urban RTK screened measurements by C/N0, lock statistic and elevation, applied a χ²-type innovation test to double differences, excluded worst-scoring measurements one at a time, and allowed an exclusion depth of 8 signals, after which the estimator was reset or the integers marginalized. [L3-S22, pp. 6–8]
- Highway RTK continuity-loss probabilities are on the order of 10⁻¹, compared with the 10⁻⁶ assumed for critical aviation manoeuvres. [L3-S21, p. 16]
- A low-cost receiver can report a wrong fix without warning: in an urban-canyon station, a ZED-F9P with a patch antenna marked a solution as fixed while it differed from the reference coordinates by more than 13 m; the authors argue that insufficient measurement confidence "should be alerted". A geodetic receiver in the same place either kept its accuracy when fixed or dropped to float. [L3-S48, pp. 14, 16]
- Integrity methods from aviation cannot be carried over directly to urban vehicles: aviation assumes high measurement redundancy and a single fault at a time, whereas multipath and non-line-of-sight (NLOS) signals cause large and frequent errors on the ground. [L3-S45, pp. 2–3]
- Classic RAIM (receiver autonomous integrity monitoring) checks the consistency of redundant ranges; fault detection needs at least five and fault exclusion at least six pseudoranges, and nominal errors are modelled as zero-mean Gaussian with known variance. Advanced RAIM (ARAIM) handles multiple faults and allocates integrity risk explicitly. [L3-S45, p. 6]
- Fault detection and exclusion based on Kalman-filter innovations performs better for dynamic platforms when the models are correct, but is model-dependent: with unmodelled errors or unexpected dynamics it "is prone to high false alarm". [L3-S45, p. 13]
- For road vehicles, Reid et al. derive alert limits from lane geometry: on freeways a lateral bound of 0.57 m (0.20 m, 95 %), longitudinal 1.40 m (0.48 m, 95 %) and vertical 1.30 m (0.43 m, 95 %); on local streets lateral and longitudinal bounds of 0.29 m (0.10 m, 95 %). They draw comparisons with aviation and rail, where similar numbers are derived at 10⁻⁸ probability of failure per hour of operation. [L3-S46, p. 1]
- In the same framework, availability requires the protection level to be below the alert limit (Fig. 5: "AL < PL resulting in no availability"); if the protection level exceeds the alert limit, it can no longer be guaranteed that the vehicle is within its lane. The text on p. 3 words availability as "how often our protection levels are larger than our alert limits", which contradicts the figure and appears to be a typo. [L3-S46, pp. 1, 3, 4, Fig. 5]
- `navsat_transform_node` treats every fix with status ≠ `STATUS_NO_FIX` and non-NaN coordinates as good and copies the NavSatFix covariance into its output; it does not look at fix type. [L3-S29, `navsat_transform.cpp` lines 618–651]

### 10. Lever arm, frames and ROS GNSS processing
- In loosely and tightly coupled GNSS/INS, antenna position = IMU position + C_b^n × lever arm (rotated into the navigation frame); antenna velocity adds rotation terms, and the source states that the Earth-rate and transport-rate term "can be neglected in most cases", leaving the body-rate (ω_ib × lever arm) term. [L3-S27, pp. 63–64, Eqs. 2.67–2.68]
- In Shin's in-motion INS initialization, velocities derived from DGPS position differences had errors within 1.5 m/s in most cases and over 2 m/s in extreme cases, including uncompensated lever-arm effects. [L3-S27, p. 171]
- Xsens: the GNSS lever arm is essential for reliable cm-level position, velocity and orientation; it is stored in the MTi as X-Y-Z in metres in the sensor frame, should be measured to centimetre accuracy, and sign matters (antenna to the right → negative Y). [L3-S37, p. 25; L3-S32, p. 25]
- The lever arm can also be estimated online: Chauchat et al. build an invariant EKF for a wheeled vehicle with unknown wheel radius and unknown GNSS lever arm; in simulation (circles at 7°/s then straight at 5 m/s, which makes the lever arm observable) it converged where a standard EKF mostly failed. [L3-S28, pp. 2, 6]
- Borko et al. note that in practice the lever arm between a GNSS antenna (outside the platform) and an INS (inside) is difficult to measure accurately and its error degrades the navigation solution; one remedy is to add lever-arm error states to the filter (18 error states). [L3-S50, p. 2]
- In their simulation of a velocity-aided INS, the lever-arm elements were not observable during a stationary phase and a straight accelerating phase (first 80 s) and the horizontal elements converged only once the platform rotated about the vertical axis. [L3-S50, p. 8]
- ROS `NavSatFix.header.frame_id` is "the frame of reference reported by the satellite receiver, usually the location of the antenna", a frame relative to the vehicle. [L3-S30, `NavSatFix.msg` lines 9–12]
- `navsat_transform_node` looks up `base_link`→`frame_id`, rotates that offset by the robot orientation and removes it, so outputs refer to the robot origin. [L3-S29, `navsat_transform.cpp` lines 517–550, 564–589]
- When that transform is missing, the behaviour differs by path: for the initial Cartesian pose it assumes the antenna is at the robot origin (logging an error only if `frame_id` is non-empty); on the per-fix path it logs "Will not remove offset of navsat device from robot's origin" and leaves the output pose at identity rather than substituting the antenna pose; an empty `frame_id` logs "Will assume navsat device is mounted at robot's origin". [L3-S29, `navsat_transform.cpp` lines 551–560, 568, 590–603, 609–615]
- navsat converts latitude/longitude to UTM (or a local ENU frame), and a manual datum can be given as latitude, longitude and heading (radians). [L3-S29, `navsat_transform.cpp` lines 102, 148–170, 337, 438–439, 633–640, 848–851; `integrating_gps.rst` lines 42–52]
- The navsat yaw used for the UTM→world transform includes `magnetic_declination`, `yaw_offset` and the UTM meridian convergence; headings are assumed zero facing east. [L3-S29, `navsat_transform.cpp` lines 284–292; `navsat_transform_node.rst` line 25]

### 11. Testing and performance evaluation of RTK systems
- Urban RTK evaluation used four metrics: 95th-percentile fixed error, validated-fix availability P_V, correct-fix rate P_S and wrong-fix rate P_F (P_V = P_S + P_F), with a fix counted correct if within 30 cm of ground truth. [L3-S22, pp. 3, 8]
- Reid et al. used a tactical-grade INS with dual survey-grade receivers and networked RTK as ground truth and reported accuracy, availability, continuity (probability of losing a mode) and correction age. [L3-S21, pp. 1, 6–7]
- Antenna comparisons used C/N0 differences against a survey-grade antenna, double-difference phase residuals with the antenna position held fixed, and Monte-Carlo time-to-ambiguity-resolution curves. [L3-S23, pp. 5–7, 9]
- Odolinski & Teunissen estimated code/phase variances by least-squares variance component estimation and compared zero- and short-baseline setups, noting that a realistic stochastic model is needed or ambiguity resolution deteriorates. [L3-S24, pp. 1, 4, 6]
- Low-cost RTK evaluation logged ~2 h (76,971 epochs) of kinematic solutions and compared them with a dual-frequency geodetic static reference, reporting fixing ratio and RMS of fixed solutions. [L3-S17, p. 6]
- NGS recommends redundant observations with different satellite geometry (e.g. hours apart) and checks on known control to catch wrong fixes and multipath. [L3-S11, pp. 37–38, 48]
- u-blox recommends checking C/N0 against a "golden device", ideally with a GNSS simulator set so the golden device reports 38–40 dBHz; outdoor go/no-go tests need an excellent sky view (HDOP < 3.0), and indoor re-radiation tests are unreliable. [L3-S01, pp. 84–85]
- A good-sky cold start should take 30–40 s averaged over 10–20 tests; supply ripple at the module should be below 50 mV p-p. [L3-S02, p. 32]
- Janos & Kuras compared receiver/antenna set-ups on seven stations of increasing obstruction against coordinates from a precise total station (Leica MS50), with measurement series run alternately so that each hardware set-up saw a similar satellite geometry. [L3-S48, pp. 1, 5, 17]
- Post-processing: raw receiver data can be converted to RINEX (a receiver-independent observation format) with RTKLIB's RTKCONV and processed with RTKPOST in kinematic, static and other modes; post-processing needs no real-time correction link, and base data can often be downloaded from permanent networks, or replaced by PPP with downloaded precise orbits and clocks. [L3-S16, pp. 31, 52; L3-S09, pp. 56–57]
- BNC can log the message types, observation types and latencies of incoming RTCM streams; correct latencies require the host computer's clock to be properly synchronised. [L3-S40, sections 2.12, 2.12.2]

## Recommended practice
1. Mount a multipath-rejecting antenna level, with full sky view, away from radiators and digital electronics, on a metal ground plane (Xsens: ≥ 150 mm diameter). [L3-S02, p. 6; L3-S37, p. 25; L3-S01, p. 99]
2. Check open-sky C/N0: strongest satellites ≥ 44 dBHz, ideally up to 50 dBHz; investigate designs stuck near 45 dBHz. [L3-S02, pp. 5, 32]
3. Use an elevation mask of 10°–15°. [L3-S11, p. 33]
4. Keep single-base baselines short (NGS: ≤ 20 km; ≤ 10 km for its tightest class) or use network RTK. [L3-S11, pp. 28, 50; L3-S12, pp. 6–7]
5. Supply matching RTCM 3 observation messages plus 1005/1006 (and 1230/1033 for GLONASS fixing). [L3-S01, pp. 18–19]
6. Keep correction latency ≤ 2 s and treat older corrections with caution. [L3-S11, p. 44; L3-S21, p. 15]
7. Detect dead correction streams with a no-data timer and reconnect with back-off. [L3-S40, section 2.11; L3-S18, `ntrip_client.py` lines 194–197]
8. Validate fixes with a fixed-failure-rate test rather than a fixed ratio threshold. [L3-S05, p. 12; L3-S06, p. 11]
9. Where a receiver reports a protection level, compare it with an alert limit and degrade operation when it is exceeded or invalid. [L3-S01, pp. 44–45]
10. Measure the antenna lever arm to centimetre accuracy in the sensor frame and enter it in the GNSS/INS; in ROS, publish the `base_link`→antenna transform matching `NavSatFix.header.frame_id`. [L3-S37, p. 25; L3-S29, `navsat_transform.cpp` lines 517–561]
11. Stream all observation messages at the same rate (1 Hz in EUREF/IGS practice) using one MSM type per stream, and stream station metadata (1005/1006, 1033) with the "update rate of at least 60 seconds or higher" that EUREF recommends. [L3-S01, p. 22; L3-S42, p. 17; L3-S41, p. 10]
12. Prefer NTRIP 2 over TCP when a proxy or firewall is in the path, and send GGA at regular intervals for VRS mountpoints. [L3-S40, sections 2.20.1.1.5, 2.10.11, 2.18.1]
13. Route the antenna cable beneath the ground plane and keep other antennas on separate, isolated ground planes. [L3-S51, p. 2]
14. Restore a lost correction stream automatically and as quickly as possible. [L3-S42, p. 17]

## Common mistakes
- Using a fixed ratio-test threshold as if it tested correctness, which lets wrong fixes through on weak models and over-rejects on strong ones. [L3-S05, p. 3; L3-S06, p. 11]
- Trusting displayed RMS or accuracy under multipath; firmware shows "false precision". [L3-S11, p. 37]
- Omitting RTCM 1230/1033, so GLONASS ambiguities remain float, or mismatched station IDs, so the rover cannot compute a position. [L3-S01, p. 19]
- Leaving `NavSatFix.header.frame_id` empty or the `base_link`→antenna transform missing: navsat then assumes the antenna is at the robot origin (empty `frame_id`, or the initial Cartesian pose), or skips offset removal on the per-fix path and leaves its output pose at identity. [L3-S29, `navsat_transform.cpp` lines 551–560, 568, 590–603, 609–615]
- Getting lever-arm signs wrong (antenna to the right of the MTi must have negative Y). [L3-S37, p. 25]
- Mounting the antenna inside a vehicle (under the dash, behind the mirror) or without a ground plane. [L3-S01, p. 91; L3-S02, p. 6]
- Requesting a wrong mountpoint and receiving a source-table instead of data. [L3-S13, p. 10; L3-S18, `ntrip_client.py` lines 116–118]
- Assuming `NavSatStatus.STATUS_GBAS_FIX` means RTK fixed: one driver sets it only for fixed, another also for float, DGPS and PPP. [L3-S43, `ublox_firmware7plus.hpp` lines 92–99; L3-S44, `message_handler.cpp` lines 1400–1428]
- Broadcasting observation messages at different rates (u-blox: they "must" share one rate), or mixing MSM4 and MSM7, which "will possibly lead to incorrect setting of the multiple message bit". [L3-S01, p. 22]
- Trusting the base-station coordinates blindly: any error in them passes into every rover position. [L3-S11, p. 40; L3-S01, p. 21]
- Carrying aviation integrity assumptions (single fault, Gaussian errors) into cluttered ground environments. [L3-S45, pp. 2–3]

## Disagreements between sources
- Maximum usable correction age: u-blox default 60 s [L3-S01, p. 19], RTKLIB default 30 s [L3-S16, p. 41], "stale" after 10–15 s for network RTK [L3-S21, p. 15], and NGS recommends ≤ 2 s [L3-S11, p. 44].
- Dead-stream detection: BNC waits 20 s of no data with exponential back-off to 256 s [L3-S40, section 2.11]; RTKLIB uses a 10 s inactivity timeout and fixed 10 s reconnect [L3-S15, lines 271–272]; MicroStrain uses 4 s and fixed 5 s retries capped at 10 attempts [L3-S18]; the Xsens client has no receive-inactivity timer [L3-S19].
- Ground plane size: u-blox app note 50×50 to 70×70 mm² for patches [L3-S02, p. 17]; Xsens recommends ≥ 150 mm diameter for RTK [L3-S37, p. 25] and u-blox measures antenna specs on a 150 mm plane [L3-S01, p. 91].
- Wrong-fix confidence: manufacturers state 99.9 % fix confidence [L3-S11, p. 23]; a measured urban wrong-fix rate was 2.4 % per epoch [L3-S22, p. 8]; a ZED-F9P with a patch antenna reported a fixed solution more than 13 m off [L3-S48, p. 14].
- Ground plane size, continued: Tallysman recommends 100–120 mm across and warns larger planes can reduce gain [L3-S51, p. 2], against Xsens' "at least 150 mm" [L3-S37, p. 25].
- Encoding RTK in ROS `NavSatStatus`: the u-blox ROS driver maps float to `STATUS_FIX` and fixed to `STATUS_GBAS_FIX` [L3-S43]; the Septentrio driver maps DGPS, float, fixed and PPP all to `STATUS_GBAS_FIX` [L3-S44]; the Xsens driver outputs GGA quality instead [L3-S20]. The message definition itself has no RTK values [L3-S30].
- Correction bandwidth: 50–500 bytes/s per stream (NTRIP 1.0 description) [L3-S13, p. 7]; about 5 kbit/s for RTK in RTCM (2005) [L3-S38, p. 3]; 1.1–1.4 kbps measured in a 2009 test [L3-S17, p. 5].

## Open questions
- What the MTi-680G fusion filter does differently with RTK FIXED vs FLOAT (weights, covariances, transitions) is not documented in the sources read.
- NTRIP 2.0 is now described at the level of its changes and transport modes (L3-S39, L3-S40), but the exact HTTP status codes, header records and any normative GGA interval in RTCM 10410.1 could not be read (paywalled).
- Message rates are now sourced from reference-network guidelines (EUREF and IGS: 1 Hz observations; EUREF: metadata "at least 60 seconds or higher"), but the rates recommended in RTCM 10403 itself, and rates for rover-side network (VRS) streams, were not found in an open source.
- Quantified C/N0 loss from specific on-vehicle noise sources (USB 3, cameras, DC-DC converters, motor drives) was not found in an open A/B source.
- How accurately the lever arm must be known (error budget in cm per cm of lever-arm error) was not quantified; sources only state that lever-arm error degrades the solution and needs rotation to become observable (L3-S50).
- Performance of RTK on grass fields under partial tree canopy for small moving ground robots was not covered; the closest open study (L3-S48) used static tripod occupations near trees and buildings.
- No open standard or REP was found that defines how ROS drivers should map RTK fixed/float into `NavSatStatus`; the drivers read map it differently (see Disagreements).
- The Springer Handbook, Misra & Enge, Kaplan & Hegarty, Groves, Counselman & Gourevitch 1981, Hatch 1990, Teunissen's 1995 J. Geodesy LAMBDA paper, Teunissen's 1998 J. Geodesy success-probability paper, Teunissen & Khodabandeh's 2015 PPP-RTK review and the IAG-Symposia version of the NTRIP paper could not be downloaded. The NGS *Guidelines for Real Time GNSS Networks* and REP-103 are open but were not downloaded (see Foundational references).
