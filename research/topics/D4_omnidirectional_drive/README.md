## Summary

- Mecanum (45°-roller) and omni (90°-roller) wheels replace a conventional wheel's no-sideslip rolling constraint with a controlled-sliding one, giving a 3-wheel or 4-wheel platform a fully holonomic 3-DOF (vx, vy, ω) chassis velocity from only one motor per wheel — but the wheel-ground contact point hands off discretely from roller to roller, which is the root cause of the vibration, odometry error, and terrain sensitivity documented throughout this topic's literature. [D4-S09, ch. 13 pp. 513–518; D4-S08, pp. 50–52; D4-S11, abstract]
- Every rough-terrain-focused source found — a 2009 TARDEC/MIT/IIT paper explicitly rejecting mecanum/omni rollers for a man-portable UGV, a 2012 rough-terrain omnidirectional robot paper, and a 2015 University of Calgary thesis's literature review of prior all-terrain mecanum trials — treats small, slender, independently-contacting rollers as the mechanical reason mecanum/omni wheels lose traction and control on uneven, loose, or debris-laden ground, in contrast to their native habitat of flat, hard, indoor floors. [D4-S14, abstract and §1; D4-S15, abstract; D4-S16, pp. 12–16]
- Four IGVC AutoNav design reports that actually built mecanum-wheeled competition robots show a split outcome: Oakland University's 2009 "Moonwalker" ran the experiment explicitly to see whether a Mecanum vehicle "can drive on grass," Bluefield State's ARCHON reported its mecanum-wheeled predecessor "had problems in grass fields" and with "the complications of a mecanum controller," while University at Buffalo's custom large-roller "Big Blue" wheels reported no competition-terrain problems and a battery-life gain over their prior rigid-wheel skid design (the report itself states this gain two different, inconsistent ways — see Key numbers). [D4-S18, p. 16; D4-S20, p. 2; D4-S19, pp. 4–5]
- Published calibration methods can recover a large share of mecanum/omni odometry's systematic error — a flower-shaped-trajectory procedure on a human-sized 3-wheel omnidirectional robot reported an 82.14% average improvement in final position/orientation estimate — but this corrects systematic (repeatable) error, not the terrain-dependent roller-contact loss that is this topic's central outdoor concern. [D4-S12, abstract]
- `ros2_controllers` only gained an official `mecanum_drive_controller` in the `4.17.0` release (2024-12-07), backported to Humble in `2.43.0` (2025-03-17) — it was not present in ROS 2 Humble's original 2022 release set — while `nav2_mppi_controller` has shipped a dedicated `OmniMotionModel` (`motion_model: "Omni"`) alongside `DiffDrive` and `Ackermann` for longer, and Nav2's DWB controller's trajectory generators are documented as working for "omnidirectional and differential drive robots" already. [D4-S26a; D4-S26b; D4-S27; D4-S29, line 11]
- No source in this research reports a mecanum/omni wheel product or redesign that is both validated for sustained outdoor grass/dirt/mud use and sold as an off-the-shelf competition-robot component; the rough-terrain-capable redesigns found (semicircular/elliptical double wheels, spring-loaded forks, the US Navy's 39″ ODV wheel) are research prototypes or one-off builds, not catalog products, while the catalog products found (AndyMark, Rotacaster) document indoor/competition-mat load ratings or, for Rotacaster's all-terrain line, a hand-truck caster application rather than a 4-wheel autonomous-robot drivetrain. [D4-S16, pp. 12–16; D4-S23; D4-S24]
- (Gap check, 2026-10-06) The "intuitive" Mecanum roller shape (an ellipse or torus) is confirmed, by the field's primary geometric source, to be only an approximation — exact only for the degenerate 90°-roller omni wheel, not the 45°-roller mecanum wheel — and a full dynamic model incorporating the real roller geometry predicts the same vibration and control-efficiency loss that bench measurements show; separately, the clearest quantified same-robot evidence found for how a real paved-but-rougher surface (asphalt) changes mecanum behavior versus a smooth one (concrete) is a 2022 industrial heavy-duty robot study, which measured roughly 20–60% higher drive-motor current and about 13 dB/Hz higher vibration noise power on asphalt. [D4-S31, Theorem 1; D4-S33, p. 306; D4-S34, "Experimental results"]

## Findings

### 1. Omnidirectional wheel kinematics theory

- A "Swedish wheel" (the textbook term covering both mecanum and omni wheels) is a standard fixed wheel augmented with free-spinning rollers around its rim; the roller axis is at angle γ to the main wheel axis, and the wheel's rolling constraint is modified from the standard fixed-wheel constraint by adding γ, with zero sliding resistance in the roller-axis direction. For γ = 0 the design is the "Swedish 90" (plain omni wheel): the rolling constraint reduces exactly to the standard fixed-wheel constraint, and the rollers add a fully decoupled, orthogonal free-sliding direction. For γ = π/2 the rollers' axes become parallel to the main wheel axis, which removes any lateral benefit — this is the degenerate case, so practical designs keep γ ≠ π/2 (typically 45° for mecanum). [D4-S08, pp. 50–52, eq. 3.18–3.19]
- Lynch & Park's equivalent framing: in the wheel's own frame, the ground-contact-point velocity decomposes into a driven component (vx + vy·tan γ) and a free "sliding" component (vy / cos γ); for an omniwheel γ = 0, and for a mecanum wheel typically γ = ±45°. Stacking each wheel's resulting scalar equation (relating chassis twist (φ̇, ẋ, ẏ) to that wheel's driven angular speed uᵢ) into one m-row matrix H(φ) gives the full forward-kinematic map u = H(φ)·q̇ for any omnidirectional platform with m ≥ 3 wheels; a minimum of 3 wheels is required to command an arbitrary 3-DOF chassis velocity because each wheel contributes only one independently driven speed. [D4-S09, ch. 13, pp. 515–518, eq. 13.3–13.6]
- In the Campion/Bastin/D'Andrea-Novel five-class (δm, δs) taxonomy — where δm is the degree of mobility (dimension of the instantaneously achievable body-velocity space) and δs is the degree of steerability — a mecanum/omni platform is type (3,0): δm = 3, δs = 0, the only type the paper calls "omnimobile," because it has no conventional fixed or centre-orientable (no-sideslip) wheels at all. The paper's own examples of this type are URANUS and UCL (both Swedish/omni-wheel robots). The paper separately defines a type (1,2) class — ≥2 independently-oriented, centrally-orientable conventional wheels, giving the same degree of mobility (δm = 1) as a car despite independent per-wheel steering — which this research reads as describing a swerve chassis; the paper itself never uses the word "swerve," so that equivalence is this research's own technical inference about the (1,2) class, not the source's own terminology. [D4-S04, p. 742]
- Campion et al.'s worked-examples section gives a fully numeric type-(3,0) example: a 3-wheel platform with Swedish (omni) wheels mounted at the vertices of an equilateral triangle, each wheel offset by 120° (listed as α = π/3, π, 5π/3, with the wheel's other two parameters β and γ both zero in their notation) — the standard "Kiwi drive" layout — for which the paper tabulates these per-wheel geometric parameters and gives the resulting constraint matrices J₁ and J₂ = diag(r) explicitly, rather than leaving the 3-wheel case only as an abstract formula. [D4-S04, p. 743]
- A roller's own passive rotation is an unactuated, unsensed extra degree of freedom at the contact point that a conventional wheel does not have; both Siegwart's and Lynch & Park's constraint derivations explicitly separate the "driven" component (read by the drive-wheel's encoder) from the "free sliding" component (the roller's own spin, which is not instrumented on either platform type documented in these texts). Neither text states this in so many words, but this split is this research's own reading of why mecanum/omni dead-reckoning is structurally more exposed to unmodeled slip than a conventional wheel's: the odometry model must assume the free-sliding component truly moved the contact point, with no sensor to confirm it. [D4-S08, pp. 50–52; D4-S09, ch. 13, p. 516]
- A 2022 kinematic-comparison survey models the chassis-velocity-vs.-wheel-speed-limit relationship for three omnidirectional architectures using a dimensionless "velocity space" approach, formally comparing a 4-mecanum-wheel robot (4WD), a 3-omni-wheel robot (3WD), and a 2-unit swerve-drive robot (2SWD); its stated conclusion is that a 3WD platform's mobility is least affected by wheel-speed limits (though at the cost of under-using one or two motors in many cases, raising per-motor torque demand), a 4WD robot is most appropriate when there is a preferential direction of travel, and both 3WD and 4WD require "high angular velocities of the passive rollers," which the authors state directly causes "lower efficiency and a higher level of vibration, strongly related to the construction quality of the wheels." [D4-S10, Abstract; §4 Conclusion]

### 2. Wheel/mechanism design variants and roller geometry

- The mecanum wheel (peripheral rollers at 45° to the hub axis, standard count 4 wheels per robot) and the plain omni wheel (rollers at 90° to the hub axis, standard count 3 wheels per robot in the "Kiwi" layout) are kinematically the two endpoints of the same γ parameter (45° vs. 0°/90° framing varies slightly by author convention), but mecanum wheels are reported as "usually used for a wheel chair or fork lift" owing to higher load capacity, while the omniwheel/mecanum choice is also tied to wheel count: "four-wheeled vehicle is generally preferred for the stability of the platform" even though 3-wheel omni suffices kinematically. [D4-S11, Introduction]
- Diegel et al.'s original 1973 Ilon mecanum design mounts rollers "held in place from the outside," which has good load capacity but means that on an inclined or uneven surface the wheel's rim itself can contact the ground instead of the roller, stopping the wheel from working correctly; Ilon's own alternative — splitting each roller in two and mounting them centrally on the hub — keeps a roller (not the rim) in ground contact on uneven surfaces. (The characterization that this split-roller design is "the lineage most later 'improved' mecanum designs build on" is this research's own editorial framing, not a claim D4-S07 itself makes about literature that postdates it — it is not independently cited to a source surveying that later lineage.) [D4-S07, §2, "The conventional Mecanum wheel"]
- A conventional (unmodified) mecanum wheel wastes a substantial fraction of motor torque to internal, self-cancelling roller forces: when the platform drives in a straight line, the forward-canted and backward-canted roller force components from opposing wheels largely cancel rather than contributing to motion, and in the extreme case of pure diagonal travel, two of the four wheels do not even need to spin while their rollers passively drag against the motor's resistance. Diegel et al. propose two fixes: (1) a mechanically simple lockable-roller mechanism that locks the peripheral rollers during straight-line travel (turning the wheel into an ordinary tire for efficiency) and unlocks them for sideways motion, and (2) a mechanically more complex design where the roller pivot axis itself can be dynamically rotated through 135° to track the platform's current direction of travel. [D4-S07, §3–4]
- A mecanum wheel's vertical and lateral vibration is attributed, across the vibration-analysis literature found, to the sequential, discontinuous handoff of ground contact from one peripheral roller to the next as the wheel turns — this is distinct from, and in addition to, the rim-vs.-roller contact failure on uneven ground described above. [D4-S11, Abstract]
- Lynch & Park's textbook distinguishes the omniwheel (rollers spin about axes in the plane of the wheel, tangential to its outer circumference) from the mecanum wheel (roller spin axes are *not* in the plane of the wheel, typically γ ≈ ±45°) and note explicitly that "the usage of, and the differentiation between, the terms 'omniwheel,' 'mecanum wheel,' and 'Swedish wheel' is not completely standard" across the field — both of the authors' own named example robots (a 3-omniwheel platform and the KUKA youBot's 4-mecanum-wheel base) are shown as representative omnidirectional designs, and the same paragraph states plainly that "because of their small diameter rollers, omniwheels and mecanum wheels work best on hard, flat ground." [D4-S09, ch. 13, pp. 513–515]
- Roller counts at hobby/competition scale: AndyMark's 8″ "MK" mecanum wheel uses 12 rollers per wheel on a brass-tube axle, nylon-core rollers with an 80A-durometer TPU overmold. [D4-S23]
- TENTE (a European castor/wheel manufacturer) markets a Mecanum wheel line as fully custom rather than fixed-catalog: diameter, load capacity, and roller polyurethane grade ("Novatech," "Ultratech," "Ultratech plus") are stated as tailored per customer application through an analysis → concept → prototype → test → implementation process, explicitly targeting AGV/AMR applications; the document makes no claim, implicit or explicit, about outdoor, rough-terrain, or all-weather use, framing the product instead around "Industry 4.0" flexibility and manoeuvrability in confined industrial spaces. [D4-S35]
- (Gap-check addition, 2026-10-06) The "intuitive" assumption that a Mecanum roller's rolling surface is simply an arc of an ellipse or circle is explicitly identified as wrong by the field's primary source on the question: A. Gfrerrer (2008) derives the roller surface's exact parametric generatrix from the condition that the wheel's ground-contact point must trace a perfect circle (so the wheel can roll without vertical motion of its center), and proves that this exact surface is **not** a torus in general — a coaxial torus only has third-order ("osculating") contact with the true roller surface near the rollers' common equator, which the paper formalizes as Theorem 1; practical manufacture therefore uses the torus only as a close approximation, not the literal exact shape. [D4-S31, §1 Abstract, §3 "Approximation of the roll by a torus," Theorem 1]
- Gfrerrer's analysis identifies one exception where the torus approximation becomes the exact shape: at the degenerate roller angle δ = ±π/2 — i.e., the **plain omni wheel** ("Swedish 90"), not the 45°-roller mecanum wheel — the roll surface R and its coaxial torus T coincide exactly. This means the "ellipse/torus roller is only approximate" caveat applies specifically to mecanum-angle (45°) rollers, not to 90°-roller omni wheels, which have an exact torus-shaped roller by construction. [D4-S31, §3, Theorem 1 and surrounding discussion]
- Adamov's 2024 unified treatment (covering both flat-ground and spherical-surface Mecanum rollers) explicitly surveys this history and states that, for a Mecanum wheel designed to move on a flat surface specifically, "the geometry of the rollers... has been studied in sufficient detail" — i.e., the exact-generatrix problem for the ordinary ground-vehicle case is treated in the literature as a solved problem, in contrast to the roller geometry needed for a Mecanum wheel mounted on a curved (spherical) surface, which the same paper states "requires further solutions." [D4-S32, p. 46]
- (Gap-check addition, 2026-10-06) WPILib — the official software framework for the FIRST Robotics Competition — states directly, in the course of explaining swerve drive, that because a swerve module's wheel is independently steerable it "can be driven with [its] force vector pointing straight forward, rather than at a 45 degree angle as in mecanum drive," and that "this results in more efficient movement and better traction than mecanum drive." This is an official-framework statement of the same 45°-force-vector efficiency/traction penalty documented mechanically by Diegel et al. (Findings §2 above) and by the Tagliavini survey's vibration/efficiency finding (Findings §1 and §3), stated here as a direct comparison against an alternative omnidirectional architecture (swerve, covered in sibling topic D1) rather than only against a conventional non-omnidirectional wheel. [D4-S37]

### 3. Precision-limiting mechanical and control factors

- A 2016 experimental/simulation study (Bae & Kang, Kyungpook National University) built a mecanum-wheel prototype, measured vertical vibration directly, and found the vertical acceleration signals were *asymmetric* about their mean — the upward and downward vibration half-cycles have measurably different shapes — a phenomenon the authors reproduced in RecurDyn dynamic simulation, confirming the asymmetry is a real mechanical effect of the roller-to-roller contact handoff and not a measurement artifact. [D4-S11, Abstract]
- The same study quantified vibration using peak-to-peak and RMS values of vertical displacement and acceleration as a function of "roller curvature" (the profile of the individual roller's contact surface), concluding the optimal curvature for minimizing vibration lies "between the 3rd and 7th curvatures" of the range they tested, and separately found an optimal spring stiffness, for a spring-loaded fork design intended to attenuate the impact force of each roller handoff, "between 200 and 250 N/mm." A companion finite-element analysis of the wheel fork's bending stiffness (varying a fillet radius at the fork's corner) found the fork's equivalent stiffness matched that optimal 250 N/mm target at a fillet radius "between 2 and 3 mm." [D4-S11, §3–4, Table 2]
- Diegel et al.'s straight-line efficiency loss (Findings §2) is a form of unavoidable internal force cancellation that exists even on a perfectly hard, flat, debris-free floor — i.e., some of the "slip" and energy loss intrinsic to mecanum/omni geometry is not a terrain effect at all, but a built-in consequence of resolving a 3-DOF velocity command through 45°-angled rollers. [D4-S07, §3]
- The 2022 kinematic-comparison survey's stated conclusion directly links high passive-roller angular velocity (required by both 3-omni and 4-mecanum architectures to realize certain commanded chassis velocities) to "lower efficiency and a higher level of vibration, strongly related to the construction quality of the wheels" — explicitly tying the vibration/precision problem to how well-built a given wheel is, not only to its basic geometry. [D4-S10, §4 Conclusion]
- (Gap-check addition, 2026-10-06) Going beyond the pure-kinematic velocity map (Findings §1), Adamov & Saipulaev (2020) derive a full rigid-body dynamic model of a 4-mecanum-wheel platform (using the KUKA youBot as the worked example) that explicitly represents each wheel's actual roller geometry and the resulting discrete contact-point switching, rather than treating each wheel as an idealized single point of contact; they compare three progressively more detailed models: (1) a no-slip nonholonomic kinematic model, (2a) a linear-viscous contact-friction model, and (2b) a Coulomb contact-friction model (friction coefficient *f* = 0.5 assumed for roller-floor contact in their numerical study). [D4-S33, §4–§5, p. 291 Abstract]
- Their stated conclusion: as a Mecanum wheel turns, the roller-ground contact point physically shifts along the wheel's axle, and numerical simulation shows this shift **reduces the efficiency of controlling both the platform's angular velocity and its center's lateral velocity**, which in turn **increases the energy consumption** needed to track a reference trajectory exactly and **deforms the predicted path** relative to what a simple no-slip kinematic model would predict; separately, "switching the contact rollers causes vibrations" in the simulation results, consistent with the experimentally measured vibration in Findings §3 above (Bae & Kang) despite coming from an independent dynamic-model derivation rather than a bench measurement. [D4-S33, p. 306, §6 Conclusion]
- (Gap-check addition, 2026-10-06) A 2022 peer-reviewed field validation on a real 200 kg industrial Mecanum-wheeled robot ("Omnibot," Galati, Mantriota & Reina, Polytechnic of Bari) measured the wheel's own rolling radius varying periodically with wheel rotation — from 0.1480 m to 0.1524 m over one full roller-pitch (30°) cycle on a 12-roller wheel — and states this radius variation "affects not only the wear of the rollers but also the total path of the vehicle that becomes not predictable," requiring the path-error contribution to be corrected by integrating the radius-vs-angle function over time rather than assuming a constant effective wheel radius as a conventional-wheel odometry model would. [D4-S34, "Kinematics and dynamics modeling" §, "Rolling radius" subsection, Table 3]
- The same study derives a wheel-slip onset condition directly from Coulomb friction (a wheel starts to slip once the commanded driving torque would require a contact force exceeding µ·Fz·sin 45°) and reports the specific static-friction coefficients it used for two real ground surfaces: µ = 0.5 for industrial concrete floor and µ = 1.0 for asphalt (i.e., the paper's own tested surfaces give asphalt *higher* available friction than concrete, not lower) — illustrating that "rougher" and "lower-traction" are not the same property, and that which one dominates mecanum wheel behavior depends on which specific failure mode (slip onset vs. vibration/wear, see next finding) is being considered. [D4-S34, "Wheel slipping" subsection, eq. 16]
- To address slip-and-vibration-induced pose-estimation error without redesigning the wheel, the same paper proposes and experimentally validates an **adaptive estimation framework**: a "surface classifier" infers which ground-surface type the robot is currently on from the pattern of current drawn by the drive motors, then adjusts the pose-estimation/control parameters (denoted *Ac*, *Bc* in the paper) to match that surface — i.e., a software-side, disturbance/surface-adaptive compensation layer for the same mecanum slip problem that the mechanical redesigns in Findings §2 (locking rollers, spring-loaded forks) address at the hardware level. [D4-S34, "Omnibot pose estimation and trajectory tracking" §]

### 4. Odometry and calibration methods for omnidirectional platforms

- A 2022 study (Palacín, Rubies, Clotet, Universitat de Lleida) on a human-sized 3-wheeled omnidirectional personal-assistant robot proposes a general procedure to evaluate and correct *systematic* odometry error: 36 individual calibration trajectories, together tracing a flower-shaped figure, are driven, the odometry-estimated and ground-truth final position/orientation of each are compared, and the robot's effective kinematic parameters are iteratively adjusted to close the gap. [D4-S12, Abstract]
- That flower-trajectory calibration procedure produced "an average improvement of 82.14% in the estimation of the final position and orientation of the mobile robot" — a large reduction in *systematic* (repeatable, calibratable) odometry error specifically; the paper's own scope is explicitly systematic-error correction, not terrain-induced slip, so this figure does not characterize how much of mecanum/omni odometry error outdoor terrain itself introduces. [D4-S12, Abstract]
- Lynch & Park's book-wide chapter preview (not chapter 13's own body text, but the authors' front-matter summary of what chapter 13 covers) states that once the kinematic model is derived, "the odometry problem — the estimation of the chassis configuration based on wheel encoder data — can be solved in the same way for both" omnidirectional and nonholonomic mobile robots, i.e. the same general dead-reckoning integration approach applies; this is a claim about *method*, not about *resulting accuracy* being equal between the two classes. [D4-S09, preprint front-matter chapter-13 preview, p. 10]

### 5. Terrain interaction and traction limits (smooth indoor floor vs. unpaved outdoor ground)

- Lynch & Park's textbook states plainly, in the course of introducing omniwheels and mecanum wheels, that "because of their small diameter rollers, omniwheels and mecanum wheels work best on hard, flat ground" — presented as a basic property of the mechanism, not a terrain-specific research finding. [D4-S09, ch. 13, p. 515]
- A 2009 paper by researchers at MIT, the Illinois Institute of Technology (IIT), and the U.S. Army's TARDEC, designing a man-portable omnidirectional UGV, states directly: "nearly all designs and analyses of omnidirectional robots have considered the case of motion on flat, smooth terrain," and that existing omnidirectional vehicles "rely on friction drives or specialized wheels (e.g. the Mecanum wheel) that are not suitable for outdoor environments because their slender rollers can easily become clogged with dirt and debris." The paper's own design response is to abandon roller wheels entirely in favor of an "active split offset caster" (ASOC) mechanism built from two independently driven conventional wheels per module. [D4-S14, Abstract and §1 Introduction]
- A related 2012 Field and Service Robotics conference paper (Ishigami, Pineda, Overholt, Hudas, Iagnemma — overlapping authorship with D4-S14) frames the same point generally: "most [omnidirectional vehicles] are designed for the case of motion on flat, smooth terrain, and are not feasible for outdoor usage," citing roller, Mecanum, and spherical wheel designs collectively as the category of "specialized wheel designs" this applies to; their ASOC-based robot instead uses a parallel-link suspension with shock absorbers per module specifically "to conform to uneven terrain." [D4-S15, Abstract and §1 Introduction]
- A University of Calgary MSc thesis's literature review (Guzman Franco, 2015) summarizes two detailed prior outdoor studies of *conventional, unmodified* mecanum wheels: (1) a US Navy study (year 2000) tested a 39″-diameter Mecanum wheel for cargo-handling vehicles and reported "an excellent capability to negotiate ramps, obstacles, mud and sand," successfully crossing 3-inch obstacles (chains, boards, ropes) — but that obstacle height was only 7.5% of the wheel's diameter, so the thesis concludes the demonstrated obstacle-climbing capability, while real, is "very limited" relative to wheel size; (2) an EADS Astrium study of the "Mars Cruiser One" Mars-exploration-platform prototype tested Mecanum wheels on a sand/loam/stone mixture meant to mimic Martian soil, and found the vehicle could traverse hard gravel and dirt furrows in frontal and lateral motion but had to move slowly on dirt because high torques were needed to hold trajectory, that loose sand caused "a considerable amount of sinkage over time," that lateral (sideways) motion on sand produced "a significant level of slippage and trajectory deviation," that obstacles up to the peripheral roller's own diameter were crossed successfully, but obstacles up to 40% of the wheel's full diameter could not be climbed (commonly only one wheel of the vehicle climbed while others slipped and threw the vehicle off its intended path), and that the best obstacle-climbing results came from approaching the obstacle at a 45° angle to the vehicle's longitudinal axis. [D4-S16, pp. 12–13]
- The same thesis's overall assessment of unmodified mecanum wheels on rough terrain, drawing on both studies above plus other, less detailed studies it cites: "Both of the studies detailed above section confirmed the expected limited capabilities and reduced performance of Mecanum wheels while traversing on rough terrain, being the most critical of the shortcomings its ability to climb obstacle higher than the diameter of the peripheral rollers." [D4-S16, p. 13]
- A 2025 competition-preparation report (Gugumuck & Paugger, a Botball-focused secondary-school technical report, not an academic venue — see evidence-level note in Sources) comparing small (2.5″) rubber-tread "Solarbotics" wheels against mecanum wheels on a Botball table, grass, stone, and LEGO-brick terrain measured a 36-inch target-path "Target Accuracy" and "Average Deviation" for each wheel/terrain combination, 20 runs per wheel type. On grass specifically, the two wheel types scored close to each other: mecanum 92.17% accuracy (2.82 in average deviation) versus Solarbotics 90.90% accuracy (3.23 in average deviation) — consistent with the report's own framing that the two "performed similarly on flat and grassy surfaces." On stone and LEGO tiles the Solarbotics wheels could not be tested at all ("N/A" in the paper's own results table) because they "failed completely," while mecanum scored 84.71% accuracy on stone and 82.47% on LEGO tiles — its two worst terrains, but still a completed, scoreable test. The same report separately notes that during testing "the Mecanum wheels occasionally lost grip or one wheel didn't fully contact the ground due to uneven weight distribution," and that extended use on demanding terrain like grass or dirt caused the (unrelated, rubber-tread) Solarbotics wheels to accumulate debris and their tread to separate from the hub. [D4-S13, §III, §IV.B and Tables I–II]
- Rotacaster (manufacturer) markets an "All Terrain Rotatruck" hand-truck product, stating: "With a 250mm (10″) puncture proof rear wheel the All Terrain Rotatruck navigates with ease over rough surfaces and through difficult terrains. Rotacaster multidirectional wheels guarantee high manoeuvrability." This claim applies to a two-wheeled hand truck whose *front* caster (not drive) wheel is the Rotacaster omni-roller wheel and whose large rear wheel is a conventional puncture-proof pneumatic-style wheel — i.e. it is not a claim about an omni/mecanum-wheeled 4-wheel autonomous drivetrain traversing rough ground on its own roller wheels alone. [D4-S24]
- (Gap-check addition, 2026-10-06) The clearest quantified, instrumented, same-robot comparison found in this research between two real ground surfaces of differing roughness is Galati, Mantriota & Reina's 2022 test of their 200 kg industrial Mecanum robot on industrial concrete floor versus asphalt (both are prepared, paved surfaces — the paper's own stated target environment is "warehouses," not open/unpaved ground, so this is a smoother-vs-rougher *paved* comparison, not a paved-vs-grass/dirt one): during straight-line motion, average drive-motor current was 5.4 A on concrete versus 6.5 A on asphalt; during sideways (lateral-roller-dominant) motion it was 10.4 A on concrete versus 16.6 A on asphalt (peak 25 A); and the measured vertical-vibration power spectral density averaged −49.29 dB/Hz on concrete versus −36.54 dB/Hz on asphalt — the paper attributes the higher current and vibration on asphalt to its documented higher static-friction coefficient and its irregular, debris-bearing surface texture compared with the flat, regular, debris-free concrete. [D4-S34, "Experimental results" §, Figs. 9, 11–13]
- The same paper's sideways-vs-forward current asymmetry (sideways motion drawing roughly double the forward-motion current on both surfaces) is attributed directly to the Mecanum mechanism itself, not the surface: "the sideways motion requires more power from the motors due to the sliding of the rollers placed at 45° and the ground" — i.e., even the smoother of the two tested surfaces (concrete) shows the lateral-roller-slip power penalty that Diegel et al. and the Tagliavini survey describe as intrinsic to the geometry (Findings §2–§3), and this penalty is then amplified, not created, by the rougher surface. [D4-S34, "Experimental results" §]

### 6. Failure modes, durability and maintenance

- The debris/dirt clogging failure mode of "slender rollers" is the specific, named mechanical reason given by the IIT/MIT/TARDEC team (Findings §5) for rejecting mecanum/roller wheels in a UGV meant for sustained outdoor operation, rather than a generic concern — it is stated as the deciding design factor. [D4-S14, §1 Introduction]
- The Buffalo "Big Blue" IGVC team's custom in-house mecanum wheel build (12 rollers on 8.5″ rims, two-part cast urethane rollers with grooves added specifically "to provide greater traction for the competition's outdoor environment") reports a mobility-related failure mode tied directly to roller size rather than debris: with a high roller count the individual rollers must be small in diameter, which "decreases their ability to climb over obstacles," and specifically causes difficulty when a wheel is moving sideways alongside a vertical step during a zero-point turn; the team's own conclusion is that the wheels "have no problems moving over the terrain presented in the competition," but that "larger rollers would improve the robot's mobility in rougher terrain." [D4-S19, p. 5]
- Component-count implication for spares/field repair: a 12-roller-per-wheel, 4-wheel mecanum drivetrain has 48 individual rollers (each typically on its own small bearing and axle pin), versus effectively one rolling-contact surface per wheel on a conventional wheel or one continuous belt per side on a track — this multiplies the number of small, wear-prone parts that can individually fail, jam, or need replacement, though no source quantifies a specific wear life or failure rate at this scale. [Inference from component counts documented in D4-S19, p. 5 and D4-S23; no field reliability/MTBF study specific to mecanum rollers at this scale was found in this research — see Open questions]

### 7. Field reports and comparative studies from other robot/vehicle domains

- Competition robotics (Botball/FRC-adjacent, hobby scale): the Gugumuck & Paugger report (Findings §5) is the only controlled, same-test-rig comparison found in this research between a mecanum wheel and a non-omnidirectional wheel type across multiple terrains including grass; its scope is small, hobby-scale robots and a non-peer-reviewed competition-preparation report, so it is included as a lower-confidence data point, not a controlling result. [D4-S13]
- Agricultural robotics: no source meeting this project's evidence-acceptance checklist (downloadable, verifiable full text) was found describing a mecanum- or omni-*roller*-wheeled agricultural or greenhouse robot's outdoor/unstructured-terrain performance; a candidate platform (AGRIMARO.Q, Politecnico di Torino) was identified via search as an "omnidirectional" reconfigurable greenhouse rover, but its full text is access-restricted behind institutional single-sign-on at the only repository copy found, and — based only on unverified search-result summaries, not on reading the paper — it appears to use independently steered hub-motor ("swerve-drive") units rather than mecanum/omni rollers, which would place it outside this topic's scope entirely. This is listed under Open questions rather than treated as a finding.
- Industrial/AGV logistics: Rotacaster's own product literature (Findings §5, §2) documents multiple roller-durometer options explicitly marketed for different indoor floor/traction trade-offs (conveyor transfer, floor-wheel use), consistent with these wheels' primary documented market being indoor material handling rather than outdoor ground vehicles; TENTE's Mecanum product literature (Findings §2) is marketed the same way, around AGV/AMR flexibility with no outdoor claim. No industrial-AGV source found in this research documents mecanum-wheel maintenance/reliability experience specific to *outdoor* (non-warehouse) deployment. [D4-S24; D4-S35; see Open questions]
- (Gap-check addition, 2026-10-06) The closest primary data point found for industrial mecanum-wheel performance on anything other than an ideal indoor floor is Galati, Mantriota & Reina's 2022 study (Findings §3, §5): a 200 kg industrial robot explicitly built and tested for "heavy-duty and long-duration tasks," instrumented and run on both industrial concrete floor and asphalt. The paper frames this itself as filling a literature gap — stating that most prior omnidirectional-robot work targets "research purposes or lightweight applications... where no payload or limited payload is present, whereas no heavy duty industrial example has been discussed" — but it still explicitly scopes its own target environment as "warehouses, where concrete and asphalt are the two typical pavements," not an outdoor/unpaved yard, so it narrows this topic's AGV-outdoor-reliability gap without closing it. [D4-S34, Introduction and "Experimental results" §]
- Planetary/field robotics: the EADS Astrium "Mars Cruiser One" Mars-rover-concept test (Findings §5) is the one planetary-rover-adjacent mecanum-wheel evaluation found, and its results (sinkage on loose sand, slippage during lateral motion, failure on obstacles over 40% of wheel diameter) are reported secondhand via the Calgary thesis's literature review, not read from Astrium's own primary publication (not located as an open, separately citable source in this research). [D4-S16, p. 13]
- Military/field UGV programs: the TARDEC-sponsored MIT/IIT ASOC work (Findings §5, §6) is a direct instance of a U.S. Army-funded field-robotics program evaluating and then explicitly rejecting roller-based omnidirectional wheels for an off-road man-portable UGV, choosing a conventional-wheel-based (ASOC) mechanism instead specifically to avoid the dirt/debris failure mode. [D4-S14, Abstract and §1]

Wheels are marketed across robotic, conveyor-transfer, and hand-truck (Rotatruck) product lines; the company's own "robotic wheels" product category exists but neither a detailed robot-scale drivetrain spec sheet (motor pairing, load rating at robot scale) nor a per-size roller-durometer spec page was found as an openly downloadable document in this research — see Open questions. - (Gap-check addition, 2026-10-06) A third wheel-only vendor found: TENTE (Germany), whose Mecanum wheel line is offered as a fully customized product (diameter, load capacity, and roller polyurethane grade built to the customer's stated application through an analysis/concept/prototype/test process) rather than a fixed catalog SKU with a published price — so, unlike AndyMark's or Rotacaster's catalog listings, no diameter/load/price figure can be reported for TENTE's line from the document found. ### 9. Complete mobile robot platforms built on mecanum/omni drive

- Clearpath Robotics' Ridgeback is a commercial 4-mecanum-wheel ("omnidirectional 'Swedish' wheels") development platform: 960 × 793 × 296 mm, 135 kg, 100 kg payload, 1.1 m/s maximum speed, 2000 W peak / 800 W continuous drive power, 24V 100Ah AGM battery (5–8 hr operating time with max payload), encoders with >250,000 counts per meter (1 per wheel), onboard IMU, and only 18mm of obstacle clearance. Its own marketing and datasheet describe it exclusively as a "Development Platform for Mobile Manipulation and Custom Payloads" and lists sample applications as mobile manipulation, human-robot interaction, and indoor "materials transport" (factory/facility use with WiFi to facility infrastructure) — the datasheet documents no outdoor or unpaved-terrain application, and its 18mm obstacle clearance is consistent with a flat, indoor-floor design target. [D4-S22a (datasheet); D4-S22b (brochure)]
- Research platforms built specifically for rough/unstructured terrain (the IIT/MIT/TARDEC ASOC vehicle, Findings §5–§7) use conventional wheels inside an actively-driven offset-caster module plus a parallel-link suspension with shock absorbers, instead of mecanum/omni rollers, explicitly to gain dirt/debris robustness and ground-contact-conforming compliance that roller wheels do not provide; this is a 25 kg, ~1 m-characteristic-length research prototype, not a catalog product. [D4-S14, Abstract; D4-S15, Abstract]
- Of the four IGVC AutoNav design reports reviewed that used mecanum wheels (see Summary and Findings §5, §6), all four are custom student-built platforms, not instances of a catalog mecanum drivetrain product (Oakland built custom wheels; Buffalo built custom large-roller wheels in-house; UBC paired catalog AndyMark wheels with a catalog AndyMark gearbox but built a custom chassis; Bluefield's predecessor vehicle used mecanum wheels on a custom four-wheel-drive independent-suspension chassis before the team abandoned the approach). [D4-S18; D4-S19; D4-S21; D4-S20]

### 10. ROS 2 / ros2_controllers / Nav2 software maturity for a holonomic mecanum base

- `ros2_controllers`' `mecanum_drive_controller` package implements "shared functionalities for mobile robot controllers with mecanum drive (four mecanum wheels)," including generic odometry computation and the main command/state interfaces; it accepts a `geometry_msgs/TwistStamped`-style command where linear x, linear y, and angular z are used (other Twist fields ignored), and publishes odometry as both a `nav_msgs/Odometry` message and a TF transform, with a configurable command-timeout parameter. [D4-S25]
- This controller was added to `ros2_controllers` in release `4.17.0` (merged 2024-12-07, PR #512, "Add Mecanum Drive Controller"), and was backported to the Humble-targeted release line in `2.43.0` (2025-03-17) — i.e., it was **not** part of ROS 2 Humble's original (2022) release set and only became available to Humble users roughly three years after Humble's release. [D4-S26a, entry "4.17.0 (2024-12-07)"; D4-S26b, entry "2.43.0 (2025-03-17)"]
- `nav2_mppi_controller`'s `motion_model` parameter (type string, default `"DiffDrive"`) accepts three named options: `"DiffDrive"`, `"Omni"`, or `"Ackermann"`. The documentation's own one-line description of each: "Differential drive robots may use forward/reverse and angular velocities; Omni add in lateral motion; and Ackermann adds minimum curvature constraints." Selecting `"Omni"` additionally activates the `vy_max` (lateral-velocity) parameter, whose own documentation states explicitly that it applies "if using 'Omni' motion model." The related `vy_std` parameter (lateral-velocity sampling standard deviation) is documented only as "Sampling standard deviation for Vy," without that same explicit Omni-only wording in its own doc text — by the same vy-gating design pattern the motion-model code uses elsewhere (the `isHolonomic()` flag gates all `vy`-axis handling in `predict()`/`predictPose()`, D4-S28), `vy_std` is presumed, but not directly confirmed in a downloaded source, to behave the same way. [D4-S27; D4-S28, lines 111, 143–158, 180–194]
- In the C++ source, `mppi::OmniMotionModel` and `mppi::DiffDriveMotionModel` are both small subclasses of a common `MotionModel` base; the only behaviorally distinguishing override shown is `isHolonomic()`, which `OmniMotionModel` returns `true` from (with an explicit code comment "Whether the motion model is holonomic, using Y axis") and `DiffDriveMotionModel` returns `false` from — i.e., at the motion-model level, omni support is a minimal, deliberately simple boolean flag that changes how the rest of the MPPI rollout code treats the lateral (vy) velocity axis, not a separate large block of omni-specific trajectory-generation logic. [D4-S28, lines 339–376]
- Nav2's DWB controller (the package `nav2_dwb_controller`) states in its own README that "the current trajectory generator plugins work for omnidirectional and differential drive robots, though an ackermann generator would be trivial to add," and separately documents a "Twirling" critic whose purpose is specifically to "prevent holonomic robots from spinning as they make their way to the goal" — i.e., DWB has carried omnidirectional-robot support, including at least one critic plugin reacting to holonomic-specific unwanted motion, independently of and prior to MPPI gaining its own dedicated Omni motion model. [D4-S29, line 11 and the Twirling critic entry]
- No documented maturity gap, open issue, or community report specific to mecanum/omni support being less mature on Humble specifically (versus Jazzy/Rolling) was found beyond the plain release-timeline fact above (the controller simply did not exist on Humble until the March 2025 backport); no GitHub issue or ROS Discourse thread describing a mecanum/omni-specific bug or limitation unique to the Humble backport was located in this research. [Open question — see below]
- (Gap-check addition, 2026-10-06) The top-level `ros2_controllers` changelog already cited (D4-S26a/b) only records meta-package version bumps and does not itself list `mecanum_drive_controller`-specific changes. The controller's **own per-package** changelog shows two things. First, even the initial Humble backport release (`2.43.0`, 2025-03-17) already bundled a same-release fix ("Fix Odometry Initialization," backport of rolling PR #1573) alongside the controller's first addition (PR #512) — i.e. a bug in the controller's odometry initialization was identified and fixed before or at the moment Humble users could first use it at all. Humble then received several further backported fixes through 2025–2026 (pose-covariance-matrix population, `2.49.0`, 2025-07-21; a missing `backward_ros` dependency declaration, `2.50.1`, 2025-10-02; a Windows build portability fix, `2.52.1`, 2026-03-11). [D4-S36]
- (Gap-check addition, 2026-10-06) Second, on the standing maturity-gap question: the Humble per-package changelog (pinned to commit `65db736`, the Humble branch's HEAD as of the access date) was checked directly for a `set_odometry` service, a velocity-limiting feature, and a "halt logic" safety fix — none of these three items appear anywhere in that file. This establishes only that the Humble-backported controller's own recorded changelog does not document having these specific items; **no `rolling`-branch changelog for this package was downloaded in this research**, so whether, when, or under what PR number any such change exists upstream on `rolling` cannot be confirmed from a source in this topic. ### 11. Net verdict literature: outdoor grass/dirt/competition suitability

- The clearest explicit position found is the IIT/MIT/TARDEC team's (Findings §5–§7): mecanum/omni roller wheels are stated as unsuitable for outdoor use specifically because "their slender rollers can easily become clogged with dirt and debris," and this is given as the design rationale for rejecting roller wheels altogether in favor of a conventional-wheel-based mechanism for an outdoor off-road UGV. [D4-S14, §1]
- The clearest field-evidence-based negative result from an actual IGVC AutoNav competition robot is Bluefield State's report that their team's prior mecanum-wheeled vehicle "had problems in grass fields, and the team had problems with the complications of a mecanum controller," leading the following year's team to explicitly adopt a "Keep It Simple, Small, and Light" design philosophy and move away from the mecanum approach. [D4-S20, p. 2]
- The clearest field-evidence-based positive (or at least not-disqualifying) result from an actual IGVC AutoNav competition robot is Buffalo's report that their custom, large-roller, in-house-built mecanum wheels "have no problems moving over the terrain presented in the competition," while still noting a design-level caveat that "larger rollers would improve the robot's mobility in rougher terrain" — i.e., the positive result is conditioned on a custom wheel built specifically with larger rollers and traction grooves added "for the competition's outdoor environment," not an off-the-shelf indoor-grade mecanum wheel. [D4-S19, p. 5]
- Oakland's Moonwalker report frames its own mecanum-on-grass choice explicitly as an open experiment rather than a settled engineering decision: "This year, Oakland University embarked on an experiment to see if it is possible to make a reliable Mecanum ODV platform that can drive on grass," concluding only that "it is believed that off-road omnidirectional vehicles would be very useful, provided enough research effort is devoted to their development" — a statement of aspiration and acknowledged uncertainty, not a validated result. [D4-S18, p. 16]
- Every rough-terrain-capable mecanum/omni redesign found in this research (the semicircular/elliptical double-wheel family reviewed in the Calgary thesis, the spring-loaded-fork vibration-mitigation design, the US Navy's internally-framed 39″ ODV wheel) is reported only as a prototype or a one-off organizational build with bench/simulation or limited field validation — none is documented as a currently available, catalog-purchasable off-the-shelf product; this contrasts with the catalog products independently verified in this research (AndyMark, Rotacaster), neither of which documents a validated sustained outdoor grass/dirt/mud performance claim for its standard wheel line. [D4-S16, pp. 12–16; D4-S23; D4-S24]

## Recommended practice

1. To reduce the straight-line efficiency loss intrinsic to a conventional mecanum wheel (Findings §2–§3), lock the peripheral rollers while driving straight — e.g. via an added mechanical brake-pin mechanism — and release them for sideways motion; alternatively, use a roller whose pivot axis dynamically re-orients toward the platform's current direction of travel. [D4-S07, §4]
2. To reduce mecanum vertical vibration, tune the wheel fork/suspension's equivalent stiffness toward the 200–250 N/mm range identified for the tested wheel geometry (achieved in that study via a fork fillet radius of about 2–3 mm), and favor a roller profile within the "3rd–7th curvature" band the same study found lowest-vibration. [D4-S11, §3–4]
3. To correct systematic mecanum/omni odometry error, drive a set of calibration trajectories that together trace a closed, flower-shaped path from a known start, compare each trajectory's odometry-estimated final pose against ground truth, and iteratively adjust the robot's effective kinematic parameters to close the gap — reported to recover most of the systematic error on one 3-wheel platform (82.14% average improvement). [D4-S12, Abstract]
4. When a mecanum/omni platform must cross a rigid obstacle outdoors, approach it at roughly 45° to the vehicle's longitudinal axis rather than straight-on or purely laterally — reported as the best-performing approach angle in the EADS Astrium outdoor mecanum obstacle-climbing test reviewed by the Calgary thesis. [D4-S16, p. 13]
5. Before treating an "outdoor-capable" mecanum/omni claim as general, check whether the specific ground and obstacles stay within the regime the cited rough-terrain study actually validated (e.g. obstacle height well under the ~40%-of-wheel-diameter failure point found on sand/loam/stone, since sinkage and lateral-motion slippage are separately reported to worsen on loose sand) rather than assuming it generalizes to all unpaved terrain. [D4-S16, pp. 12–13]

## Common mistakes

- Treating "omnidirectional" and "holonomic" as fully interchangeable without checking a platform's Campion-classification type: a swerve chassis is omnidirectional in the colloquial sense (can eventually reach any pose) but — reading the paper's type-(1,2) class (≥2 independently-oriented conventional wheels) as describing a swerve chassis, a mapping this research makes rather than one the paper itself states — is formally δm = 1, not the δm = 3 "omnimobile" type (3,0) that genuine mecanum/omni roller platforms achieve; conflating the two misstates what each architecture can do *instantaneously*, without first reorienting anything. [D4-S04, p. 742]
- Assuming a mecanum wheel's roller rotation is observable from the drive-wheel's own encoder: the encoder only measures the "driven" component of contact-point velocity, never the roller's free "sliding" component, so any slip in the sliding direction is structurally invisible to simple odometry integration unless a separate calibration or sensing method is added. [D4-S08, pp. 50–52; D4-S09, ch. 13, p. 516]
- Assuming a catalog indoor/competition-mat mecanum or omni wheel will perform like the custom, larger-roller, traction-modified wheels built by teams such as Buffalo's "Big Blue," which added rubber grooves and oversized rollers specifically "to provide greater traction for the competition's outdoor environment" and explicitly stated that COTS wheel rollers "are continuous and are meant for smooth, indoor surfaces." [D4-S19, p. 5]
- Citing Rotacaster's "All Terrain" marketing claim as evidence for a drive-wheel's outdoor performance without noting that the specific product it is attached to (the Rotatruck PRO hand truck) uses the Rotacaster omni wheel only as a steerable/maneuvering caster, with a separate large pneumatic-style wheel doing the actual rough-terrain rolling and load-bearing. [D4-S24]
- Assuming ROS 2 Humble has first-class `ros2_controllers` support for a mecanum base simply because later ROS 2 distributions do: the `mecanum_drive_controller` package did not exist in Humble until a March 2025 backport release, roughly three years after Humble's original 2022 release. [D4-S26a; D4-S26b]
- (Gap-check addition, 2026-10-06) Assuming a Mecanum wheel's roller surface is an ellipse, a circle, or a torus because that is the easiest shape to visualize and to manufacture: the field's own primary geometric analysis shows the torus is only an approximation (exact only in the degenerate 90°-roller omni-wheel case), and treating it as exact for a true 45°-roller mecanum wheel misstates the wheel's actual rolling-contact geometry. [D4-S31, Theorem 1]

## Disagreements between sources

- **Does an unmodified mecanum/omni wheel perform acceptably on grass specifically?** The hobby-scale Gugumuck & Paugger report found mecanum and simple rubber-tread wheels "performed similarly" on grass (i.e., grass was not where mecanum specifically struggled; LEGO bricks/stones were) [D4-S13]. In contrast, Bluefield State's IGVC report states their team's prior mecanum-wheeled competition vehicle directly "had problems in grass fields" [D4-S20, p. 2], and the general outdoor-rejection literature (IIT/MIT/TARDEC, Calgary thesis's literature review) treats unstructured/debris-laden ground broadly — which would include grass, dirt, and mud — as the problem case rather than isolating grass as uniquely benign or uniquely hostile [D4-S14; D4-S16]. These are not strictly contradictory (different wheel scales, different specific grass/soil conditions, different robots), but the Gugumuck & Paugger report is the only one of these that reports grass-specific test data rather than a general outdoor-vs-indoor framing, and it is the lowest-evidence-level source in this set (a non-peer-reviewed student competition-preparation report).
- **Is a custom, traction-modified mecanum wheel a viable fix, or is the mechanism fundamentally unsuited to rough terrain?** Buffalo's "Big Blue" reports a positive outcome from a custom wheel redesign (larger rollers, added traction grooves) with no competition-terrain problems reported [D4-S19]. The IIT/MIT/TARDEC team and the Calgary thesis's literature review instead treat the underlying roller-based contact mechanism itself as the limiting factor regardless of specific wheel redesign, and both groups' own design response was to abandon roller wheels for a conventional-wheel-based mechanism rather than iterate on roller geometry [D4-S14; D4-S16, pp. 12–16]. No source in this research directly tests the Buffalo-style "larger rollers" fix against the EADS Astrium/US Navy-documented failure modes (sinkage, >40%-of-diameter obstacle failure) on the same rig, so this disagreement is not resolved by head-to-head evidence.

## Open questions

- No source found gives a quantified wear life, mean-time-between-failures, or field-replacement-rate figure for mecanum/omni roller bearings, pins, or tires at a scale comparable to this topic's ~0.8 m-radius reference vehicle; the component-count argument (Findings §6) is this research's own inference from documented roller counts, not a sourced reliability study.
- No direct, controlled, same-platform study comparing a mecanum/omni drivetrain against skid-steer or tracks specifically on grass/dirt was found (the closest are single-drivetrain field reports and the small-scale Gugumuck & Paugger comparison against a non-omnidirectional wheel, not against skid-steer or tracks) — this gap, flagged already at the SCOPE.md mapping stage, remained open after a further research pass.
- VEX Robotics' and SuperDroid/Nexus's own product pages could not be accessed directly (bot-check / JavaScript-only catalog pages blocked automated retrieval in this research), so their wheel/kit specifications and prices are not independently verified beyond what search-engine result snippets reported; a researcher with normal browser access should re-verify these before relying on them.
- Whether any mecanum- or omni-*roller*-wheeled (as opposed to swerve-driven) agricultural/greenhouse robot has been built and field-tested on unstructured outdoor terrain remains unanswered: the one candidate found (AGRIMARO.Q, Politecnico di Torino) is access-restricted at its only located repository copy (institutional single-sign-on wall), and unverified search-result summaries suggest it may use swerve-drive units rather than mecanum/omni rollers, which would place it outside this topic's scope rather than answering the question.
- The West & Asada ball-wheel mechanism's MIT thesis (D4-S06) could not be retrieved despite multiple access attempts (direct link, cookie-primed request, Wayback Machine); no finding in this topic is drawn from reading that thesis's content, only from its citation and from how other sources (Diegel et al., the Jurnal Teknologi review) describe it secondhand.
- No source found documents an industrial AGV operator's maintenance/reliability field experience with mecanum wheels specifically in an *outdoor* (non-warehouse) logistics deployment; the closest found (D4-S34, gap check 2026-10-06) is a heavy-duty industrial robot tested on warehouse-adjacent asphalt as well as indoor concrete, which narrows but does not close this question — whether any mecanum-wheeled AGV has been deployed on genuinely outdoor/unpaved ground, and if so how it fared, remains unanswered.
