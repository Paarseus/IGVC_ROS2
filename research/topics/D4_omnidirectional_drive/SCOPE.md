## Overview

Omnidirectional wheeled drive gets a vehicle three independent degrees of freedom (vx, vy, ω) from standard rotary motors by replacing a conventional tyre's continuous rolling-contact line with a wheel built from many small passive rollers, so the contact patch itself can slide sideways relative to the direction the hub is pointed. The two dominant mechanisms are the **mecanum wheel** (rollers canted 45° to the hub axis, four wheels in an X arrangement, invented by Bengt Ilon of the Swedish firm Mecanum AB and patented in 1972/1975 — US3,876,255) and the plain **omni wheel** (rollers at 90° to the hub axis, most often used three-per-robot at 120° spacing, the "Kiwi drive"). A third family avoids small discrete rollers altogether: Killough & Pin's **orthogonal wheel-pair** platform (1992 ICRA / 1994 IEEE T-RA) switches between two conventional wheels mounted at right angles in a powered cage, and West & Asada's **ball-wheel** (1995 ICRA / 1997 ASME J. Mech. Design) drives a single ball with friction rollers so the contact point is a true sphere rather than a sequence of roller facets. All of these trade the wheeled mobile robot's usual single non-holonomic rolling constraint for a fully holonomic (or in some treatments omnidirectional-but-not-fully-holonomic) kinematic model, first placed in a general wheeled-mobile-robot framework by Agulló, Cardona & Vivancos (1987, "vehicles with directional sliding wheels") and by Muir & Neuman's (1987) wheel-Jacobian formulation covering "omnidirectional and ball wheels" together, and later organized into the standard five-class kinematic/dynamic taxonomy of Campion, Bastin & D'Andrea-Novel (1996) that recurs across every drivetrain topic in this research set. The control-theoretic promise — any (vx, vy, ω) at any instant, zero-radius turns with no heading change required first — is counter-balanced by mechanical precision costs that are the central engineering literature on the topic: the roller's own profile curvature is not simply an ellipsoid or sphere (exact-rolling tracing the hub circle requires a specific non-elliptical generatrix), consecutive roller-to-roller handoff makes the "continuous" ground contact actually discontinuous at the roller-facet level, producing unavoidable vertical and lateral vibration that several papers model and try to damp; and because each roller is small and narrow, an uneven, loose, or debris-laden surface readily breaks contact with one or more rollers, collapsing the traction and position-control guarantees that hold on a smooth hard floor. This is the crux of the outdoor question: essentially every source that discusses mecanum/omni wheels outside a flat warehouse or competition-mat floor — rough-terrain redesign papers, an Illinois Institute of Technology omnidirectional UGV project that deliberately chose conventional wheels over mecanum/omni specifically because "small, slender rollers... can easily become obstructed with debris and dirt," and multiple IGVC AutoNav teams' own design reports (Oakland University "Moonwalker," University at Buffalo "Big Blue," UBC Snowbots "Avalanche," Bluefield State "ARCHON") — treats smooth, hard, level ground as mecanum/omni's native habitat and grass/mud/sand/gravel as a departure from it serious enough to warrant either a wheel redesign or a different drivetrain choice. On the software side, the holonomic kinematic model is supported as a first-class citizen in the modern ROS 2 stack: `ros2_controllers` ships a dedicated `mecanum_drive_controller` (merged into the main `ros2_controllers` line in late 2024, backported to the Humble distribution in March 2025 — i.e., not present in Humble's original 2022 release set) and `nav2_mppi_controller` ships an explicit `OmniMotionModel` alongside its differential-drive and Ackermann motion models, while older Nav2 components (DWB's trajectory generators, costmap/planner layers) are also described as working for omnidirectional robots, though with less documentation depth than the differential-drive case that dominates most Nav2 tutorials and defaults.
