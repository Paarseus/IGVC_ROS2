## Summary
- Ideal (non-slip) differential drive is a textbook special case — two fixed wheels on a common axle, degree of mobility δm = 2 — and both Siegwart & Nourbakhsh and Lynch & Park explicitly exclude skid-steering from that no-slip model: Lynch & Park state it verbatim ("tanks and skid-steered vehicles are excluded"), while Siegwart & Nourbakhsh exclude the equivalent case by describing it as a *degenerate* "slip-skid" configuration outside their no-slip framework, without using that exact phrase; a wheeled skid-steer robot must break the no-sideslip assumption to turn at all, which is why it needs a separate kinematic treatment. [D2-S03, chapter 13 introduction, p. 513] [D2-S02, section 3.3.1, p. 61]
- Mandow et al.'s 2007 ICR model — built and validated on a wheeled platform (Pioneer P3-AT, not a tracked vehicle) — treats each side's effective rotation centre as displaced outward from the wheel contact line by an amount that depends on terrain and tyre pressure; on asphalt with 20 psi pneumatic tyres the fitted model put the two side-ICRs at xICRr ≈ 0.31 m, xICRl ≈ −0.26 m against a physical half-separation of 0.20 m (track centreline separation L = 0.40 m), a ≈1.4× effective-width ratio (1.31–1.44× across all six tyre/terrain conditions they tested), and the same shape of correction — one multiplier applied to an otherwise-rigid differential-drive model — is what `ros2_controllers`' `diff_drive_controller` exposes today as `wheel_separation_multiplier`, applied identically to the command path and the odometry path. [D2-S05, section IV.C, Tables I–II] [D2-S20, lines 143, 298] [D2-S09, "wheel_separation_multiplier" parameter]
- Reported effective-width ratios for wheeled platforms span a wide range depending on vehicle and surface — about 1.31–1.44 for the 23.6 kg Pioneer P3-AT [D2-S05, Tables I–II], about 1.33–1.54 for an independently-designed 330 kg four-wheel UGV [D2-S14, Table 1, Table 2], and about 2.6–3.7 for a 590 kg Clearpath Warthog on concrete versus snow [D2-S12, Table I] — so wheeled studies do not show a smaller or more tightly clustered correction than what is reported for tracked platforms in companion research; the dominant pattern across both is that the ratio is surface- and vehicle-dependent, not a universal constant, which several of these same sources note explicitly.
- `ros2_controllers`' `diff_drive_controller` ships one unified velocity controller that covers both ideal differential drive and wheeled skid-steer through `wheel_separation_multiplier` (and per-side radius multipliers), explicitly built with Husky-style "two wheels per side, one control signal" platforms in mind, and Clearpath's own shipped configs use it (Husky A200: 1.875; Jackal J100: 1.5; Warthog W200: 1.125) — though one official Husky repository config ships the un-corrected default of 1.0, showing the multiplier is not applied consistently even across a single vendor's own configs. [D2-S09, "wheel_separation_multiplier" parameter, "wheels_per_side" parameter] [D2-S24] [D2-S25] [D2-S26] [D2-S27]
- Grass-specific wheeled data exists but is thin and somewhat inconsistent: a six-wheeled skid-steer UGV (Crusher) driving over "rough, grassy terrain" had its no-slip pose-prediction mean error cut from 1.8 m to a few centimetres by online slip calibration [D2-S17, section 6.1, p. 28]; a Gaussian-process slip model on a Clearpath Jackal reduced mean angular-position error on grass from 18.9 % to 5.6 % and linear error from 6.2 % to 5.7 %, with the baseline model's linear error actually *lower* on grass than on asphalt (6.2 % vs. 14.2 %) [D2-S13, Table I]; and a four-wheel robot tested on a grass field, ceramic tile, concrete and gravel found terrain type had no significant effect on the ICR-correction's effectiveness, with turning radius mattering far more. [D2-S16, "Results and Discussion", "Conclusions"]
- Off-the-shelf wheeled skid-steer platforms range from research robots (Clearpath Husky A300: 80 kg, 2.0 m/s, 100 kg payload; Jackal: 17 kg, 2.0 m/s; Warthog: 280–590 kg, 18 km/h, amphibious, optional track conversion) to competition-robotics kits (AndyMark AM14U6: 6-wheel drop-center chassis, ToughBox Mini S gearboxes, $940) to independent IGVC-competition builds using custom gearboxes and chain drives. [D2-S28] [D2-S29] [D2-S30] [D2-S31] [D2-S32] [D2-S33]

## Findings

### 1. Ideal (non-slip) wheeled differential-drive kinematics
- Siegwart and Nourbakhsh model each standard wheel with a rolling constraint (it must roll when moving along its own plane) and a sliding constraint (no lateral slippage orthogonal to that plane); this analysis assumes pure rolling at a single contact point per wheel. [D2-S02, section 3.2.3, pp. 45–46]
- The robot's instantaneous centre of rotation (ICR) must lie on the "zero motion line" through every standard wheel's axle; a differential drive has two fixed wheels sharing one axle, giving degree of mobility δm = 2 (it directly controls forward speed and turn rate). [D2-S02, section 3.3.1, pp. 58–59, 61]
- Siegwart and Nourbakhsh explicitly flag that a four-wheel "slip-skid" steering configuration (fixed wheels on more than one axle) is a *degenerate* case outside this no-slip framework — useful on loose soil and sand, but at the cost that "dead-reckoning based on odometry becomes less accurate and power efficiency is reduced dramatically". [D2-S02, section 3.3.1, p. 61]
- Campion, Bastin and d'Andréa-Novel's five non-degenerate wheeled-robot structures all assume every wheel rolls without slipping (velocity of the ground-contact point is zero both along and across the wheel plane); a differential drive is their type (2,0), requiring fixed wheels confined to a single common axle — a skid-steer vehicle with fixed wheels on more than one axle falls outside all five types. [D2-S01, section II.B–II.C, pp. 736, 741–742]
- Lynch and Park's wheeled-mobile-robot chapter assumes rolling "without skidding (i.e., tanks and skid-steered vehicles are excluded)" — the standard differential-drive equations φ̇ = (r/2d)(uR − uL), forward speed (r/2)(uL + uR), with inverse uL = (v − ωd)/r, uR = (v + ωd)/r, are derived under that assumption. [D2-S03, chapter 13 introduction, p. 513; section 13.3.1.2, eq. 13.14–13.15, pp. 523–524]
- Lynch and Park note that odometry errors for even the ideal model accumulate "due to unexpected slipping and skidding of the wheels and to numerical integration error", and recommend correcting odometry with other sensors (e.g. GPS) via a Kalman or particle filter. [D2-S03, section 13.4]
- An unpowered caster wheel does not turn a differential-drive robot into a skid-steer vehicle: Siegwart and Nourbakhsh state plainly that "such wheels do not impose any real constraints on the kinematics of a robot chassis", and their own worked example of a differential-drive robot (Fig. 3.13a, captioned "Differential Drive Robot with two individually motorized wheels and a castor wheel, e.g. the Pygmalion robot at EPFL") is a two-driven-wheel-plus-castor design — the castor is a stabiliser, not a third kinematic constraint, so this configuration remains the ideal δm = 2 differential-drive case covered above, not a skid-steer case. *(Added in gap check, 2026-10-06, answering subtopic 11(b)/13: this is also the common mistake of conflating a caster-stabilised diff-drive with a true multi-wheel skid-steer — the two are kinematically different classes even though both are sometimes called "skid-steer" informally.)* [D2-S02, section 3.2.3.3, p. 50; Fig. 3.13a, p. 59]

### 2. Wheeled skid-steer kinematics: the ICR model and the effective-width correction
- Mandow et al. state the physical distinction plainly: "when differential drive is applied to control a pair of pure rolling wheels, no slippage occurs and each tread's contact is limited to a single point... this assumption is usually adequate for non-ideal wheels with a relatively small contact patch," whereas tracked and wheeled skid-steer vehicles both require slippage to turn because "each side's tread consists of several contact patches that correspond to mechanically linked wheels." [D2-S05, section II, p. 2]
- Their model places each side's ICR using geometric relations xICRl = (αl·Vl − vy)/ωz, xICRr = (αr·Vr − vy)/ωz (with αl, αr correction factors for tyre inflation/transmission effects), and shows the resulting kinematic matrix A is algebraically identical in form to an ideal differential-drive model whose wheel contact points sit at the (bounded) tread-ICR positions instead of the physical wheel centres. [D2-S05, section II, eq. 3–8, pp. 2–3]
- Fitted on a Pioneer P3-AT (physical track separation L = 0.40 m) with 20 psi pneumatic tyres on asphalt, the model gave xICRr ≈ 0.307 m, xICRl ≈ −0.255 m (effective separation ≈ 0.56 m, about 1.4× physical) and α ≈ 0.90–0.93; on solid rubber tyres the fitted α values were, on average, slightly lower than for 20 psi pneumatic tyres, though not uniformly — one of the four tread/terrain comparisons (asphalt, right tread) goes the other way (solid α = 0.9128 > 20 psi α = 0.9049). [D2-S05, section IV.B–IV.C, Table I]
- Mandow et al. define a steering-efficiency index χ = L/(xICRr − xICRl) (1 when no slippage occurs) and report χ ≈ 0.69–0.76 across their asphalt and concrete test conditions, with "the loss of thrust power while turning... greater in asphalt than in concrete due to greater friction," and compact (solid rubber) wheels showing better steering efficiency than pneumatic tyres, "but less traction." [D2-S05, section IV.C, eq. 9, Tables I–II]
- Table III reports mean-squared dead-reckoning position error, over a validation path checked against differential GPS, for three models: (0.00010, 0.00011) m² for the optimised asymmetric model, (0.00183, 0.00468) m² for the as-shipped factory P3-AT model (α = 1, fixed xICR = 0.3 m), and (0.00162, 0.00455) m² for a separate, experimentally-fitted *symmetric* model (α ≈ 0.91, fixed xICR = 0.275 m) — the asymmetric model outperforms both alternatives by roughly an order of magnitude or more on each error component. [D2-S05, section IV.D, Table III]
- Kozłowski and Pazderski's 4-wheel skid-steer model states that if the ICR's projection along the vehicle's longitudinal axis grows too large, "the vehicle can lose motion stability as a result of skidding," a phenomenon "not important for traditional vehicles if only the no-slip assumption is satisfied"; Caracciolo et al.'s operational fix is to constrain that projection to stay within the wheelbase. [D2-S06, section 1, p. 477–478]
- Wang et al.'s laser-scanner-based follow-on introduces a dimensionless path-curvature variable λ and an ICR coefficient χ, derives an empirical function relating them from Pioneer P3-AT data, and reports the resulting model improves dead-reckoning to a position error within 0.03 m and an angle error within 0.1 rad relative to the robot's default (uncorrected) model, under acceleration < 3 m/s² and speed < 0.5 m/s. [D2-S07, section 4 "Conclusions/Outlook", p. 9700]
- Rabiee and Biswas's friction-based kinematic model, trained and tested on a public ≥6 km Clearpath Jackal dataset spanning tile, asphalt and grass, "outperforms the state-of-the-art in terms of both translational and rotational prediction error," with its advantage over simpler kinematic models standing out specifically "when the robot has accelerated motion." [D2-S08, Abstract; section V (starts p. 4), quote at p. 5]
- On the same Jackal, increasing commanded angular acceleration measurably *decreases* the observed angular velocity for a fixed pair of commanded wheel velocities — i.e. the effective correction is not speed/acceleration-invariant even on a single wheeled platform and surface. [D2-S08, Fig. 5 and surrounding text]
- Baril et al., comparing five kinematic-model parameterisations on a 590 kg wheeled Warthog, found the extended differential-drive model's virtual width b̂ was 4.46 m on concrete versus 3.08 m on snow (physical wheel separation b = 1.2 m — ratios ≈3.7× and ≈2.6×), and that for commanded angular displacements over 30°, "the curve for resulting angular displacement grows faster when the robot is operated on snow than on concrete," with a visible drop in measured angular displacement at the highest commanded values on snow — a nonlinearity the authors say "could be hard to describe with linear kinematic models." [D2-S12, Table I; section V, "Trained concrete"/"Trained snow" results]
- Counter-intuitively, Baril et al. also report that the *ideal*, uncorrected differential-drive model "performs better on snow that on concrete" [sic — the source's own text reads "that," evidently a typo for "than"], i.e. wheeled skid-steer motion on snow was closer to the no-slip model than motion on concrete was, for their platform. [D2-S12, section V, results discussion]
- Zhou et al., modelling an independently designed 330 kg four-wheel skid-steer UGV (physical width 1.45 m), found that naively computing an effective width B from raw wheel-velocity differences gave B ≈ 1.93–2.23 m (about 1.33–1.54× physical) and conclude this occurs because slippage makes "the measured difference between the velocities of the outer and inner wheels... larger than those of the actual values," i.e. directly-measured wheel speeds overstate the true velocity difference across the vehicle. [D2-S14, Table 1, Table 2, Results]

### 3. Contact-patch physics: why wheeled slip differs from tracked slip
- The core physical distinction that both wheeled- and tracked-skid-steer researchers draw on is contact-patch shape: an ideal wheel is assumed to touch the ground at a point or small patch, while each side's "tread" in a multi-wheel skid-steer vehicle is actually "several contact patches that correspond to mechanically linked wheels" rather than one continuous footprint as in a track. [D2-S05, section II, p. 2, Fig. 1]
- Jia, Smith and Peng's wheel–terrain interaction model is built specifically for the "cylindrical wheel-soil contact area" of a single rolling/steered wheel in soft soil, modelling stress distributions (normal pressure, shear stress) along that contact region and integrating them to get reaction forces and torques — a modelling unit that, by construction, is per-wheel rather than per-track. [D2-S18, section 3 (starts p. 3), quote at p. 4]
- The published finding most directly comparing wheeled and tracked turning resistance under the same framework comes from Li, Yin, Zhang and Yuan's model, built "with reference to the steering theory of tracked vehicles" but accounting for lateral nonlinear slip and uneven tyre load distribution specific to wheels; the paper's own abstract states that "the turning resistance coefficient of skid steer wheeled vehicles is smaller than that of tracked vehicles." This is reported secondhand from the paper's abstract on its institutional repository page — no open full-text copy was found, so the derivation behind the claim could not be checked. [D2-S19, abstract text, not downloaded]
- A separate, downloadable finding pointing the same direction: Mandow et al.'s own citation list includes Wong and Huang's "Wheels vs. tracks: A fundamental evaluation from the traction perspective" as the standard reference for this comparison, but no open copy of that paper could be located, so its content is not used here beyond noting its existence and topic. [D2-S05, references, ref. 6]
- *(Added in gap check, 2026-10-06, subtopic 16.)* A second, overlapping-author paper from the same Beijing Institute of Technology group as D2-S19 gives a specific mechanism for wheeled skid-steering resistance: using a discrete-element-method (DEM) simulation with a micromechanical tyre-ground contact model, Li, Zhang, Hu and Yuan's abstract states that "the fitting formula of skid-steering resistance coefficient is given" and concludes that "bulldozing resistance between tire and ground is much higher than friction force" — i.e. the lateral resistance a wheel generates while skidding is reported as dominated by soil being pushed/bulldozed aside, not by Coulomb friction alone. This is reported secondhand from the paper's abstract only (no open full-text copy found), so the derivation behind it could not be checked, the same limitation as D2-S19. [D2-S39, abstract text, not downloaded]

### 4. Magnitude and predictability of the correction: wheels vs. tracks
- Across the wheeled sources gathered here, the effective-width (or virtual-width) ratio ranges from about 1.31–1.44 on a 23.6 kg Pioneer P3-AT [D2-S05, Tables I–II], to about 1.33–1.54 on an independently designed 330 kg four-wheel UGV [D2-S14, Table 1, Table 2], to about 2.6–3.7 on a 590 kg Clearpath Warthog [D2-S12, Table I] — almost a three-fold spread (1.31× to 3.7×) across published wheeled platforms, which does not support a claim that wheels give a smaller or tighter-clustered correction than tracks; if anything the single heaviest wheeled platform here (Warthog) has the largest ratio of any platform in this topic or in `C2_drive_kinematics`'s tracked-vehicle data.
- Clearpath's own shipped `ros2_controllers` configs for three wheeled skid-steer platforms show the same spread: `wheel_separation_multiplier` = 1.875 for Husky A200 (physical wheel separation 0.555 m → effective ≈1.041 m), 1.5 for Jackal J100 (physical 0.376 m → effective ≈0.563 m), and 1.125 for Warthog W200 (physical 1.5 m → effective ≈1.688 m). [D2-S24] [D2-S25] [D2-S26]
- Against the same `diff_drive_controller` default of 1.0 (no correction), one of Clearpath's own Husky repositories (the `humble-devel` branch of the standalone `husky` repo, as opposed to the `clearpath_common` repo's A200 config) ships `wheel_separation_multiplier: 1.0` — the two official Clearpath configs for essentially the same robot disagree on whether any skid correction is applied at all, and they also disagree on the underlying `wheel_separation` value itself (0.512 m in `husky` `humble-devel` vs. 0.555 m in `clearpath_common`'s A200 config), so this is not simply two different multipliers applied to one agreed physical geometry. [D2-S27] [D2-S24]
- Wanichratanagul et al.'s four-wheel robot explicitly concludes that "the terrain type is insignificant to the skid behavior in low dynamic [conditions]," based on tests across grass, ceramic tile, concrete and gravel at two turning radii, while turning radius itself had a large, consistent effect — "the larger radius generates a smaller slip." [D2-S16, "Conclusions"]
- Baril et al.'s own framing states this directly for the heavier end of the wheeled-platform range: validating kinematic models "above 120 kg" was, to their knowledge, previously unstudied [D2-S12, section I, p. 1]; their own Table I and §V/§VI results (already cited above) show the same five models transfer from light wheeled platforms but with parameter *magnitudes* that are platform- and surface-specific, not portable constants [D2-S12, Table I, sections V–VI].

### 5. Odometry and state estimation specific to wheeled skid-steer
- Borenstein and Feng's UMBmark method isolates the two dominant systematic odometry-error sources in a non-skid differential-drive robot — Type A error (wheelbase uncertainty, which changes total rotation the same way in both the clockwise and counter-clockwise direction of a bidirectional square path) and Type B error (unequal wheel diameters, which changes rotation oppositely in the two directions) — and reports "a consistent improvement of at least one order of magnitude in odometric accuracy" after calibration. [D2-S04, Abstract; section 4.1, pp. 13–14]
- This UMBmark decomposition addresses only the non-skid error sources; wheeled skid-steer calibration (Mandow's ICR method and its follow-ons) is a separate, additional step that targets the slip-driven effective-width and longitudinal-slip parameters UMBmark does not model. [D2-S05, section III] [D2-S04, Abstract]
- Okawara et al.'s tightly-coupled LiDAR-IMU-wheel odometry learns a neural wheel kinematic model online inside a factor-graph optimiser specifically because, in their framing, a fixed wheel model "must be maintained online" as terrain changes, and a linear skid-steer model cannot capture large-slip nonlinearity; in their tests, online learning ran in about 0.03 s per step, faster than their 0.1 s LiDAR period. [D2-S15, section 1, pp. 1–2; section 4.2.2, p. 14]
- Okawara et al. tested their wheeled platform on eight terrain types including grass, and found that learned network parameters for grass ("uneven and soft") were clearly separated from those for flat hard terrains (indoor floor, wood tiles), and that training on an indoor flat floor and testing on grass produced significantly worse prediction than training/testing on similar hard terrains. [D2-S15, section 4.1, Table 1, p. 10, Case 2 results]
- Seegmiller et al.'s integrated prediction error minimisation (IPEM) calibrates wheel-slip parameters online on Crusher, a six-wheeled skid-steer UGV, over "rough, grassy terrain" at Camp Roberts, California, including a dirt road and tall dry grass in the same run; online calibration reduced mean pose-prediction error from 1.8 m to a few centimetres and cut the standard deviation of along-track error by 72 %, cross-track error by 83 %, and heading error by 90 %, relative to assuming no slip. [D2-S17, section 6, pp. 27–28]
- Seegmiller et al. also report that Crusher's "effective turn rate is only a third of the commanded rate," attributing this "partly... to the fact that four of six wheels are slipping sideways and resisting motion in a tight turn," with a further loss of traction and effective turning ability when turning on a hillside because the uphill-side wheels carry reduced load. [D2-S17, section 6.1, p. 31]

### 6. ROS 2 / `ros2_controllers` / Nav2 support maturity
- `ros2_controllers`' `diff_drive_controller` is documented as taking body velocity commands and publishing odometry for "mobile robots with differential drive," and explicitly directs readers to its general "Wheeled Mobile Robot Kinematics" background page; its parameter `wheels_per_side` is described with the Husky specifically as the example: "Husky has two wheels on each side, but they use one control signal, in this case '1' is the correct value of the parameter." [D2-S09, controller description; "wheels_per_side" parameter] [D2-S21, "wheels_per_side" field]
- The same controller exposes `wheel_separation_multiplier` (default 1.0) as a "correction factor when the actual wheel separation differs from the nominal value," plus independent `left_wheel_radius_multiplier` / `right_wheel_radius_multiplier`; the controller's source applies `wheel_separation_multiplier * wheel_separation` identically in both the command/update path and the odometry path, so one parameter corrects both directions together rather than needing separate forward/inverse tuning. [D2-S09, "wheel_separation_multiplier" parameter] [D2-S21, "wheel_separation_multiplier" field] [D2-S20, lines 143, 298]

### 8. Terrain traction and precision: grass, dirt, gravel
- Seegmiller et al.'s Crusher test at Camp Roberts, California traversed "both a dirt road and tall dry grass" on the same run, with roll angles from −28° to 29° and pitch from −22° to 17° on steep slopes; lateral accelerations reached up to 0.5 g from slopes and 0.3 g from aggressive manoeuvres. [D2-S17, section 6.1, Fig. 8]
- Trivedi et al.'s Gaussian-process model, trained and tested on a Clearpath Jackal across asphalt, grass and tile, reports mean percentage errors for the baseline five-parameter extended differential-drive model of: angular error 17.6 % (asphalt), 18.9 % (grass), 21.1 % (tile); linear error 14.2 % (asphalt), 6.2 % (grass), 11.6 % (tile). Their GP model reduced these to 5.7 %/5.6 %/10.9 % (angular) and 5.8 %/5.7 %/5.0 % (linear) respectively. Notably the *baseline* model's linear error on grass (6.2 %) was lower than on asphalt (14.2 %) in this dataset. [D2-S13, Table I]
- Rabiee and Biswas's released Jackal dataset includes "more than 2.3 km" of long-distance driving across "tile, asphalt, and grass" collected under normal human-joystick operation, intended as a benchmark for everyday mixed-terrain use rather than an extreme off-road test. [D2-S08, section IV, "Long Distance Dataset"]
- Wanichratanagul et al. tested a four-wheel robot through 180° U-turns at two radii (1 m, 2 m) on a grass field, ceramic floor, concrete road, and gravel passageway, using a stereo-camera V-SLAM system (factory-calibrated ZED2i) as ground truth; a zero-radius turning calibration run on the gravel passageway gave a fitted parameter y0 = 0.93 m, and the gravel surface was flagged in discussion as "suspected to be the most friction[al] terrain," extending the robot's angular-acceleration period relative to the other three surfaces. [D2-S16, "Results and Discussion"]
- Okawara et al.'s wheeled-robot grass tests found prediction trained on an indoor flat hard floor transferred poorly to grass specifically because "indoor flat floor was more similar to another flat and hard terrain (wood tiles) than to grass (i.e. rough [and soft])" — i.e. the grass–hard-floor domain gap was larger than the hard-floor–hard-floor gap in their data. [D2-S15, section 4.1, p. 10]
- No full-text source identified in this search gives a direct, controlled wheeled-vs-tracked traction or precision comparison specifically on grass, dirt or gravel at a mid-size-robot scale; the comparative framing available (Bruzzone & Quaglia's survey, subtopic 10 below) is qualitative and at the category level, not a grass-specific field measurement.

### 9. Testing and validation methods for wheeled skid-steer accuracy
- Borenstein and Feng's UMBmark uses a bidirectional square path (their worked example uses a 4 m side length): the robot is driven around the same square clockwise and counter-clockwise, and the resulting end-point scatter is decomposed into Type A (wheelbase) and Type B (wheel-diameter) error contributions through a closed-form geometric model. [D2-S04, section 4.1, Figs. 7–8]
- Mandow et al.'s ICR identification uses representative joystick-driven paths with varied rotational/translational speeds, logging internal encoder ticks at high rate alongside a lower-rate external ground truth (differential GPS, <1 cm precision in their case, via RTCM/RTK corrections at 5 Hz), and fits the ICR/slip parameters with a genetic-algorithm search that minimises the sum of squared pose-increment errors between segments. [D2-S05, section III; section IV.C]
- Wang et al. use laser-scanner-based localisation as ground truth to fit the relationship between their ICR coefficient χ and a path-curvature variable, then validate on a held-out sample path. [D2-S07, Abstract, Conclusions]
- Wanichratanagul et al. use a factory-calibrated stereo-camera visual-SLAM unit (ZED2i) as ground truth for 180° U-turn trajectories at two fixed radii, and additionally cross-check against a MATLAB SimScape dynamic simulation of the same manoeuvre (friction coefficient 0.9 chosen as it produced no significant difference across the range tested) — the simulation was found to replicate the qualitative skid behaviour but not the precise slip magnitude, which the authors attribute to the simulation's friction model not accounting for wheel deformation. [D2-S16, "Dynamic simulation", "Results and Discussion"]
- Seegmiller et al. use a high-end IMU and differential GPS as ground truth for online model calibration on Crusher [D2-S17, section 6, Introduction], while Baril et al. use a Robosense RS-32 LiDAR plus ICP scan-registration pipeline (not GPS) as ground truth for an offline, per-terrain parameter fit on the Warthog [D2-S12, section IV "Experimental Setup"] — both are multi-kilometre field datasets rather than short lab manoeuvres, but their ground-truth sensing and calibration approach (online vs. offline) differ.
- Rabiee and Biswas built and released a purpose-made ≥6 km benchmark dataset (constant-velocity segments plus a long-distance mixed-terrain run) specifically so that other kinematic/slip models for wheeled skid-steer robots could be compared on the same data, rather than each paper collecting its own incomparable dataset. [D2-S08, Abstract; section IV]

### 10. Lessons from other domains using wheeled skid-steer / diff-drive
- Bruzzone and Quaglia's survey states that wheeled robots "can reach high speeds with low power consumption, and can be guided by controlling a few active degrees of freedom... but their ability to overcome obstacles is generally limited," and that tracked robots "are well suited to move on uneven and soft terrains and overcome obstacles, thanks to their large ground contact surface, but they move more slowly and consume more energy than wheeled robots" and are "subject to vibrations as the lateral track profile is a polygon with moving vertices," which "limits maximum speed and reduces mechanical efficiency." [D2-S11, section 3.1, p. 51; section 3.2]
- Their synthetic comparison table rates wheeled locomotion as "high" on maximum speed and energy efficiency and "low" on obstacle-crossing and soft/uneven-terrain capability, but "low/medium" (not pure "low") on slope-climbing capability (all normalised for robot size); tracked locomotion is rated "medium/high" on maximum speed, "medium/high" to "high" on obstacle-crossing and slope-climbing, "high" on soft-terrain capability, but only "medium" on energy efficiency. Both wheeled and tracked locomotion are rated "low" (i.e. simplest) on mechanical complexity and control complexity — the lowest rating in the table on control complexity — though one hybrid category (wheeled-tracked/WT-hybrid) shares that same "low/medium" floor on mechanical complexity rather than being rated strictly higher on every cell. [D2-S11, Table 2]
- Bruzzone and Quaglia note that most wheeled robots built for unstructured outdoor environments use 4, 6 or 8 wheels rather than 3, because 3-wheel designs — adequate for flat structured floors such as hospitals (their example) — are "not suitable for unstructured environments because of... poor stability in the presence of uneven and sloping surfaces," and that 4+ wheel layouts are mechanically "hyperstatic," requiring an articulated frame or suspension to keep every wheel in ground contact. [D2-S11, section 3.1, pp. 51–52]
- The Crusher platform (Carnegie Mellon / DARPA) is explicitly built and tested as a military-relevant off-road autonomous UGV, and its reported behaviour — effective turn rate only a third of commanded, due to four of its six wheels slipping sideways in a tight turn, with further traction loss on side-slopes — is a reasonable general lesson for other six-wheel skid-steer vehicles under similar load/terrain conditions, not a quirk unique to Crusher, though the source itself does not use the word "any" at this location; this generalisation is this topic's own inference from Crusher's reported mechanism. [D2-S17, section 6.1, p. 31]
- The LandTamer (skid-steered, hydraulic-drive) and RecBot (Ackermann-steered, electric) platforms are included in Seegmiller et al.'s validation specifically "to show applicability to other platforms" beyond the six-wheeled Crusher, and the IPEM calibration method is reported to work on all three without architecture-specific changes. [D2-S17, section 6, Introduction]
- *(Added in gap check, 2026-10-06, subtopic 14 — planetary-rover domain.)* Shamah's 1999 CMU thesis, built around Nomad, a four-wheel testbed that had traversed 223 km in Chile's Atacama Desert the prior year as a planetary-exploration-analog field trial, directly compares wheeled skid steering (differential wheel velocity) against explicit steering (changing wheel heading, like Ackermann) on the *same* vehicle over the same steady-state turn radii. For a point turn, "the power for skid steering is approximately double that for an explicit point turn," and power/torque for both methods converge to equal values only at infinite radius (straight driving); the paper frames this as a general trade-off for "slow but capable locomotors characteristic of planetary robotic vehicles," not a quirk of one machine. [D2-S34, Abstract; Chapter 3 "Nomad", p. 25 (Atacama Desert field trial); Chapter 4 "Results", section 4.1, p. 27]
- The same Nomad experiments show skid-steer scrub load is not shared evenly across the vehicle's four wheels: torque "is split in the same diagonal fashion," with the rear-outer and front-inner wheels together carrying noticeably more torque than the front-outer and rear-inner pair, and for a 4 m-radius skid-steer turn the single highest-loaded wheel's share of total torque ranged from about 49% to 52% across repeated trials, versus about 5–8% for the lowest-loaded wheel in the same trials; the authors found "the rear outer wheel has a consistently higher torque value than the other wheels," independent of turn direction. [D2-S34, Chapter 4, section 4.3.3 "Wheel Torque", p. 35]
- *(Added in gap check, 2026-10-06, subtopic 14 — agricultural domain.)* Botta et al.'s peer-reviewed, open-access study of Agri.Q — an eight-wheel articulated wheeled skid-steer robot built for precision-agriculture monitoring on "hilly or mountainous crops" — reports that simulated steady-state circular manoeuvres reproduced and extended "previous results obtained on similar robotic architectures, which suffer from significant understeer behaviour due to significant lateral wheel slip during turning." [D2-S35, Abstract, p. 103]
- Only Agri.Q's front module is actively skid-steered: "the rear motors are wired in such a way that they can only spin in the same direction and at the same speed," so "the rear module is not skid-steered" and its motors are normally disengaged, activated only by a "Traction Allocation Control Strategy" when the front module needs extra traction power (e.g. climbing a hard slope, facing an obstacle, or "negotiating extremely tight curves") — a deliberate electrical/mechanical choice to prevent the rear module from fighting the front module's steering. At standstill the four front wheels carry about 60% of the robot's total weight (112 kg) versus 40% on the rear wheels, and this static split shifts further under the rocker-tilting torques generated by uneven ground reaction forces as the robot moves. [D2-S35, section 3 (traction-motor wiring and Traction Allocation Control Strategy), p. 109; section 5.3 "Contact Forces Analysis", p. 121]

### Failure modes specific to wheeled skid-steer
- Loss of directional stability from excessive ICR displacement: Kozłowski and Pazderski identify that if the vehicle-ICR's projection onto the longitudinal axis grows too large, "the vehicle can lose motion stability as a result of skidding" — a failure mode with no equivalent in a true no-slip vehicle. [D2-S06, section 1, pp. 477–478]
- Reduced turn authority on slopes from load transfer: on Crusher, "when turning on a hill, the wheels on the high side carry reduced load and are therefore unable to generate as much traction as on level ground," to the point that "turning becomes impossible and turn commands cause more or less translational motion perpendicular to the terrain gradient" instead. [D2-S17, section 6.1, p. 31]
- Direct wheel-speed differencing overstates true velocity difference: Zhou et al.'s initial attempt to compute an effective track width directly from raw wheel-encoder speed differences gave a value "significantly... larger than the actual width of the UGV," because slippage means the measured velocity difference between outer and inner wheels is larger than the vehicle's actual velocity difference — a naive implementation trap for anyone implementing effective-width correction straight from encoder data without external ground truth. [D2-S14, Results, p. 5 of extracted text]
- Treating the effective-width/slip correction as constant across conditions: Baril et al.'s own result — a 2.6× ratio on snow versus a 3.7× ratio on concrete for the same 590 kg Warthog, plus a measurable nonlinearity above 30° commanded turns on snow — directly contradicts treating the multiplier as a single surface-independent constant, even for one vehicle. [D2-S12, Table I, section V]

## Recommended practice
1. Fit the wheeled skid-steer ICR/effective-width model (not the ideal differential-drive model) whenever the platform has more than two fixed-axle wheels or any mechanically linked multi-wheel side drive, because the ideal model's no-sideslip assumption is violated by construction. [D2-S05, section II] [D2-S02, section 3.3.1, p. 61]
2. Calibrate the correction empirically against an external ground-truth sensor (differential GPS, laser scanner, visual SLAM, or a high-end IMU+GPS pair) rather than assuming a textbook or vendor-default value, because every wheeled source in this topic that measured the ratio on more than one surface found it changed with surface. (Note: not every wheeled source agrees that *terrain* is the dominant driver of slip at all — D2-S16 tested four surfaces and found terrain type insignificant compared to turning radius, so it is a counter-example on this specific point rather than supporting evidence; see Findings §4 and Disagreements.) [D2-S05, Tables I–II] [D2-S12, Table I]
3. If the platform or its duty cycle crosses multiple terrains with materially different friction (e.g. concrete and snow, or hard floor and grass), either recalibrate per terrain or use an online/adaptive estimator rather than a single fixed multiplier, following the online EKF/factor-graph/learned approaches reported for wheeled platforms. [D2-S12, section V] [D2-S15, section 1] [D2-S17, section 6]
4. Run a non-skid odometry calibration (UMBmark-style bidirectional square path) first to remove wheelbase-uncertainty and wheel-diameter error, before layering a skid/ICR correction on top, since the two target different, additive error sources. [D2-S04, Abstract, section 4.1] [D2-S05, section III]
5. When configuring `ros2_controllers`' `diff_drive_controller` for a multi-wheel-per-side platform, set `wheels_per_side` to the number of independent control signals (not the physical wheel count) and set `wheel_separation_multiplier` from a measured value rather than leaving the un-corrected default of 1.0, since at least one official vendor configuration ships that uncorrected default. [D2-S09, "wheels_per_side" parameter] [D2-S27]

## Common mistakes
- Assuming the ideal (no-slip) differential-drive equations apply to any multi-wheel-per-side robot: both standard textbooks used here explicitly exclude skid-steered vehicles from that model, and applying it anyway is the single largest source of the dead-reckoning error the rest of this topic's calibration methods exist to correct. [D2-S02, section 3.3.1, p. 61] [D2-S03, chapter 13 introduction, p. 513]
- Computing an effective track width directly from simultaneous outer/inner wheel-speed measurements without external ground truth: this systematically overstates the true width, because slippage inflates the measured speed difference between sides. [D2-S14, Results]
- Treating a fitted effective-width multiplier (or `wheel_separation_multiplier`) as a universal constant for a given robot: every wheeled source here that tested more than one surface (asphalt vs. concrete; concrete vs. snow) found the fitted value changed, sometimes with a visible nonlinearity at larger commanded turns. [D2-S05, Tables I–II] [D2-S12, Table I, section V]
- Assuming turning resistance/scrub scales the same way with turning radius as a Coulomb-friction model predicts: Yu et al.'s result cited in `C2_drive_kinematics` (a tracked-vehicle finding, carried over here only as a caution) shows pure Coulomb friction predicts the same resistance torque at every radius, which contradicts measured motor-torque data that falls as commanded radius grows — a shear-displacement-based model is needed instead. This is a tracked-vehicle finding and is flagged here only as a caution that the same nonlinearity should not be assumed away for wheels without checking.
- Relying on a vendor's shipped control config as evidence that skid correction has been applied: at least one official Clearpath Husky repository ships the uncorrected `wheel_separation_multiplier` default of 1.0 while a separate official Clearpath repository for the same robot family ships 1.875 — the presence of a config file is not itself evidence of a calibrated value. [D2-S27] [D2-S24]
- *(Added in gap check, 2026-10-06, subtopic 13.)* Conflating a caster-stabilised 2-wheel differential drive with a true multi-wheel skid-steer vehicle: the two are different kinematic classes even though both sometimes get called "skid-steer" informally — a caster wheel adds no kinematic constraint (Siegwart & Nourbakhsh's own worked differential-drive example has one), so a design with two driven wheels plus one or more unpowered casters should still be analysed with the ideal no-slip model, not the ICR/effective-width correction this topic is otherwise about. [D2-S02, section 3.2.3.3, p. 50; Fig. 3.13a, p. 59]

## Disagreements between sources
- Whether wheels give a smaller/more predictable correction than tracks: SCOPE.md's working hypothesis (from one secondhand BIT-paper abstract) is that wheeled turning resistance is smaller than tracked turning resistance [D2-S19, abstract, not downloaded], but the actual *magnitude spread* of the effective-width correction found across wheeled sources in this topic (≈1.31× to ≈3.7×) is as wide as, or wider than, the spread reported for tracked platforms in `C2_drive_kinematics` (≈1.2× to ≈1.4× for a small tracked robot, up to ≈1.36× for a 16 t tracked vehicle) — the two claims (smaller turning-resistance *coefficient* vs. smaller/more predictable *effective-width correction*) are not the same quantity, and this topic found no single source that measures both for a direct comparison.
- Whether grass is "harder" or "easier" terrain for wheeled skid-steer than hard surfaces: Baril et al. found the uncorrected ideal model fit *better* on snow than on concrete for their Warthog [D2-S12, section V], and Trivedi et al. found the uncorrected baseline model's *linear* error was lower on grass than on asphalt for their Jackal [D2-S13, Table I] — yet Okawara et al. found grass was the terrain their indoor-trained model generalised to *worst* [D2-S15, section 4.1, p. 10]. These are not strictly contradictory (different error quantities, different baselines, different robots), but they mean "grass is harder to model than pavement" is not a safe generalisation from this evidence alone.
- Whether Clearpath's own configuration defaults represent a calibrated or an uncorrected value: the `husky_control/config/control.yaml` in the standalone `husky` repository (`humble-devel` branch) ships `wheel_separation_multiplier: 1.0` and `wheel_separation: 0.512`, while the `clearpath_common` repository's A200 config ships `wheel_separation_multiplier: 1.875` and `wheel_separation: 0.555` — both are official Clearpath repositories for the same robot family, but they disagree on both the multiplier *and* the underlying physical wheel-separation value, so this is not simply two correction factors applied to one agreed geometry; neither config file states which (if either) reflects a measured value. [D2-S27] [D2-S24]

## Open questions
- No open-access, full-text source was found that directly compares turning resistance, scrub torque, or effective-width correction magnitude between a wheeled and a tracked skid-steer vehicle under matched load/terrain/speed conditions on the same test rig; the closest finding (Li et al.'s abstract-only claim that wheeled turning resistance is smaller than tracked) could not be verified against the paper's full derivation. [D2-S19, not downloaded]
- No grass-, dirt-, or gravel-specific wheeled-vs-tracked precision/repeatability comparison at the roughly 0.8 m-radius, sub-100 kg robot scale was found; the available grass/dirt data (Crusher, Jackal, the four-wheel ICR robot) is all wheeled-only, and the available wheeled-vs-tracked comparison (Bruzzone & Quaglia) is a qualitative, category-level survey rather than a terrain-specific field measurement.
- How the effective-width correction should be expected to vary with vehicle mass was not resolved: the three wheeled mass points gathered here (23.6 kg, 330 kg, 590 kg) do not show a monotonic relationship between mass and correction ratio (1.3–1.5×, 1.33–1.54×, 2.6–3.7× respectively), and no source in this topic studied mass as an isolated variable.
- Whether any current `ros2_controllers`-based wheeled skid-steer deployment uses an online/adaptive `wheel_separation_multiplier` (rather than a fixed value set at launch) was not established; all the official vendor configs found here use a single static value.
- No VEX Robotics EDR product page could be retrieved (vexrobotics.com, kb.vex.com and the robotshop.com/idesignsol.com reseller mirrors all returned HTTP 403 to automated requests during this search), so the VEX side of subtopic 7b's "AndyMark/VEX" comparison rests on AndyMark alone; this is a research-tooling gap, not a finding that VEX lacks such kits. *(Retried in the 2026-10-06 gap check with the same result — a second, independent attempt confirms this is a tooling/access barrier, not a one-off search failure.)*
- *(Added in gap check, 2026-10-06.)* No current, official purchase price for Husky, Jackal or Warthog could be found — Clearpath's own materials use a "request a quote" model; only one dated (2014), launch-era, secondhand estimate for Jackal was located (D2-S38), and it should not be read as current pricing.
- *(Added in gap check, 2026-10-06.)* No source meeting this topic's acceptance checklist (peer review, official documentation, or named authorship at a traceable, reputable venue) was found specifically on skid-steer or differential-drive kinematics for powered wheelchairs, lawn-care robots, or industrial AGVs — three domains the research context names as relevant comparisons. Searches surfaced only patents, an encyclopedia article, and brief survey mentions (e.g. that most industrial AGVs use Ackermann or simple two-wheel differential drive rather than multi-wheel skid-steer), none of which could support a cited finding here.
