## Summary
- Textbook wheeled-robot kinematics assumes every standard wheel rolls without sliding sideways; a skid-steer or tracked vehicle can only turn by breaking that sliding constraint, so the ideal differential-drive (no-slip) model does not predict its motion, and texts either treat skid steering as a "degenerate" configuration or exclude it. Each track instead behaves as if it rotates about its own ICR (the point about which that track is instantaneously rotating), and these track ICRs lie *outside* the track centrelines. [C2-S21, p. 61] [C2-S22, p. 513] [C2-S01, section II]
- For a symmetric vehicle this reduces to a differential-drive model with a wider "virtual" track width plus a longitudinal slip factor; the width ratio (effective / physical) is the same idea as the `wheel_separation_multiplier` in ros_controllers and ros2_controllers, which apply one multiplier to both the command and the odometry path (Clearpath ships 1.875 for Husky and 1.5 for Jackal). [C2-S03, section III.B, eq. (8)–(9)] [C2-S10, lines 142–145, 257–260, 298–302] [C2-S15] [C2-S16]
- The effective width is not a physical constant: published identified ratios range from about 1.3–1.5 (the 23.6 kg wheeled Pioneer 3-AT, plus a 15.8 kg sensor structure in Mandow's tests) to about 2.6–3.7 (590 kg wheeled Warthog), about 1.22–1.40 for a 40 kg rubber-tracked robot (equivalent track 1.159–1.329 m against a 0.95 m nominal track, our arithmetic), and change with surface, tyres, load distribution / centre of mass, vehicle inertia (in simulations of a 258 kg tracked robot the track-ICR spread changed with the inertial force F_i = −M ω × v_CM) and slope, and turn radius, and (by inference from Rabiee and Biswas's result that measured turn rate for the same wheel speeds falls as angular acceleration rises) with acceleration; if the ICR's longitudinal position leaves the wheelbase the vehicle skids sideways and loses stability. [C2-S01, Tables I–II, sections II, IV.A, IV.C] [C2-S02, p. 484] [C2-S04, Table 3] [C2-S03, Table I, section IV] [C2-S05, section V, Fig. 5] [C2-S29, section 2.1, Table 1] [C2-S37, section III, Fig. 7(c)] [C2-S38, section II.B]
- Terramechanics explains why: track–ground shear stress builds up with shear displacement (τ = τ_max(1 − e^(−j/K))) instead of jumping to its Coulomb maximum, so turning resistance and motor torque fall as the turn radius grows, and the parameters (friction, shear modulus K, rolling resistance) are terrain-dependent. [C2-S23, PDF pp. 8, 10, 20] [C2-S32, pp. 5–6]
- The standard calibration is: drive varied manoeuvres while an external reference (RTK GPS, laser, total station, motion capture, lidar ICP) records the true motion, then fit the model by minimising the error of predicted versus measured pose over a chosen horizon; online alternatives estimate slip or track ICRs from track speeds plus GNSS/IMU with an EKF, or from a gyro and an empirical slip relation, and improved odometry or path tracking by roughly 44–90 % in field tests. [C2-S01, section III] [C2-S03, sections IV–V] [C2-S26, section 7] [C2-S28, Abstract, section 6.1] [C2-S29, section 3.2] [C2-S30, section 6.2] [C2-S31, Abstract] [C2-S25, sections II.D, III]
- Dynamic and power models (including the closed-loop wheel-speed controllers and motor limits) are recommended for motion planning in environments with elevation changes, varied terrain or frequent accelerations, and where motor power limits matter; and they show that in a turn the outer motor always consumes power while the inner motor can switch to generating power. [C2-S24, Abstract] [C2-S23, PDF pp. 1, 5, 18]

## Findings
General principles and methods come first (textbook kinematics, terramechanics, dynamics, online estimation, validation practice on other platforms, then the skid-steer ICR literature); material specific to ROS controllers and vendor configurations is in the last sub-section.

### Classification of wheeled robots and nonholonomic constraints (textbook view)
- Siegwart and Nourbakhsh model each wheel with two constraints: a rolling constraint (the wheel must roll when motion occurs along its plane) and a sliding constraint (no lateral slippage orthogonal to the wheel plane); the analysis assumes pure rolling at a single contact point. [C2-S21, section 3.2.3, pp. 45–46]
- Each standard wheel defines a "zero motion line" through its axle; the robot's instantaneous centre of rotation (ICR) must lie on every such line, so mobility depends on the number of independent constraints, not the number of wheels. [C2-S21, section 3.3.1, pp. 58–59]
- The degree of mobility is δm = dim N[C1(βs)] = 3 − rank[C1(βs)], where C1 stacks the sliding constraints of all standard wheels; it counts the chassis degrees of freedom that can be changed directly through wheel speeds and ranges from 0 to 3. [C2-S21, section 3.3.1, eq. (3.40), p. 61]
- A differential drive has two fixed wheels on one axle, so rank C1 = 1 and δm = 2 (it controls forward speed and turn rate); a bicycle has δm = 1; a robot with only omnidirectional wheels has δm = 3. [C2-S21, section 3.3.1, pp. 61–62] [C2-S21, Fig. 3.14, p. 63]
- If rank C1f > 1 (fixed wheels that do not share an axle), the vehicle can at best move on a circle or a straight line; the book calls the four-wheel "slip-skid" steering system such a degenerate configuration that is still useful on loose soil and sand, at the price that "dead-reckoning based on odometry becomes less accurate and power efficiency is reduced dramatically". [C2-S21, section 3.3.1, p. 61]
- Campion, Bastin and d'Andréa-Novel assume that every conventional wheel rolls without slipping: the velocity of the wheel point touching the ground is zero, both along the wheel plane and orthogonal to it. [C2-S35, section II.B, p. 736]
- From this they show that only five non-degenerate wheeled-robot structures exist, labelled by the pair (degree of mobility δm, degree of steerability δs): (3,0), (2,0), (2,1), (1,1) and (1,2), with 1 ≤ δm ≤ 3, 0 ≤ δs ≤ 2 and 2 ≤ δm + δs ≤ 3. [C2-S35, section II.C, eq. (16)–(18), p. 741]
- A type (2,0) robot (e.g. a differential drive) has either one fixed wheel or several fixed wheels on a single common axle; otherwise rank C1f would exceed 1 (HILARE is the example given). Combining this with Siegwart and Nourbakhsh's statement that rank C1f > 1 gives a degenerate "slip-skid" configuration, a skid-steer vehicle with fixed wheels on more than one axle falls outside the five no-slip types (our inference from the two sources). [C2-S35, section II.C, p. 742] [C2-S21, section 3.3.1, p. 61]
- Lynch and Park classify wheeled mobile robots as omnidirectional (no equality constraint on chassis velocity) or nonholonomic (one Pfaffian velocity constraint A(q)q̇ = 0 that cannot be integrated into a configuration constraint, e.g. a car cannot move directly sideways yet can reach any pose). [C2-S22, section 13.1, p. 514]
- Their chapter assumes rolling on hard, flat, horizontal ground "without skidding (i.e., tanks and skid-steered vehicles are excluded)". [C2-S22, chapter 13 introduction, p. 513]
- Diff-drive kinematics: with wheel radius r and wheel spacing 2d, φ̇ = (r/2d)(u_R − u_L) and forward speed (r/2)(u_L + u_R); the inverse is u_L = (v − ωd)/r, u_R = (v + ωd)/r, which turns unicycle, diff-drive and car into one "canonical" (v, ω) model differing only in the allowed control set. [C2-S22, section 13.3.1.2, eq. (13.14)–(13.15), pp. 523–524] [C2-S22, section 13.3.1.4, p. 525]
- No linear (and no continuous time-invariant) feedback law can stabilise the full pose of a nonholonomic robot to a point, although the robot is controllable. [C2-S22, section 13.3.2, p. 528]
- Odometry errors accumulate "due to unexpected slipping and skidding of the wheels and to numerical integration error", so odometry should be corrected by, or fused in a Kalman or particle filter with, other sensors such as GPS. [C2-S22, section 13.4]

### Terramechanics: slip, shear displacement and turning resistance
- Two definitions of wheel/track slip are used: "slipping" when the speed computed from wheel rotation exceeds the actual ground speed, and "skidding" when it is smaller. [C2-S23, PDF p. 3] [C2-S32, p. 6, eq. (12)]
- Terramechanics models of wheel–soil and track–soil contact rest on two relations: pressure–sinkage (usually the Bekker equation) and shear stress–shear displacement. [C2-S34, section 3.2, p. 494] [C2-S32, p. 5]
- The Janosi–Hanamoto relation gives shear stress τ = τ_max(1 − e^(−j/K)), with j the shear displacement, K the shear deformation modulus and τ_max = c + p·tan φ (Mohr–Coulomb: c cohesion, p normal pressure, φ internal friction angle). [C2-S34, section 3.4, eq. (22)–(23), p. 496] [C2-S32, eq. (10)–(11), pp. 5–6]
- For soils whose shear curve has a "hump" (certain muskegs, dry sands and snows), Wong's modified shear formula with a residual-stress ratio K_r is used instead. [C2-S34, section 3.4, eq. (24), p. 496]
- Along a track the shear displacement grows with distance from the front of the contact patch: j = i·x, where i is the slip ratio. [C2-S32, p. 6, eq. (12)]
- For firm ground, Yu et al. apply Wong and Chiang's version τ_ss = pμ(1 − e^(−j/K)) (p normal pressure, μ friction coefficient, K a terrain-dependent shear deformation modulus) to a skid-steered wheeled vehicle, instead of assuming the full Coulomb shear stress pμ appears as soon as there is any relative motion. [C2-S23, PDF pp. 7–8, eq. (9)]
- With pure Coulomb friction, the predicted resistance torque is the same for all turning radii, which contradicts experiments on tracked (Wong) and wheeled vehicles; with the shear-displacement model, measured left and right motor torque magnitudes fall as the commanded turning radius increases. [C2-S23, PDF p. 10, eq. (24); PDF p. 20, section 5.1]
- The skid-steer "expansion factor" α (effective width / physical width) varies with terrain: for a Pioneer 3-AT α = 1.5 on a vinyl lab surface and α > 2 on concrete, and "the larger the rolling resistance, the larger the expansion factor". [C2-S23, PDF p. 6] [C2-S24, section II, Table II]
- The slip-ratio (Wong) kinematic model and the expansion-factor model become identical when i_l/i_r = −ω_r/ω_l and α = 1/(1 − 2 i_l i_r/(i_l + i_r)); the authors knew of no experiment verifying the first condition. [C2-S23, PDF pp. 6–7, eq. (3)–(5)]
- On a 40 kg rubber-tracked skid-steer robot (nominal track width 0.95 m), the equivalent track estimated online during turns was 1.269 m on sand, 1.252 m on gravel, 1.329 m on mud (highest, "wet surface generates the highest slipping effect") and 1.159 m on asphalt (lowest). [C2-S29, section 2.1, p. 2; section 4.2, Table 1, p. 7, text p. 8]
- Drive-motor currents were higher on sand and mud than on asphalt and gravel, which the authors attribute to these terrains being more flexible and giving a larger track contact area that requires more tractive effort. [C2-S29, section 4.2, p. 8]
- For a small tracked vehicle, the ratio of left to right slip ratios followed |a_l/a_r| = |v_r/v_l|^n with n = 0.4811 on p-tile, 0.6213 on plywood and 0.5094 on artificial turf, i.e. the slower track slips more, and n "does not change significantly due to the surface". [C2-S25, section II.D, eq. (7); section III.B, p. 2873]
- A multibody simulation of an agricultural rubber-tracked crawler (Bekker + Janosi–Hanamoto soil forces) gave steady slip ratios of about 9.8 % (dry sand), 8.7 % (sandy loam) and 6.2 % (clay) at 7 km/h without load, and about 12.45 %, 11 % and 15 % with a 13 kN planter draft; slip reached 20 % when climbing (a 20° slope was simulated). [C2-S32, Abstract; p. 11; p. 15]
- Rubber crawler tracks can have high internal motion resistance (from rollers and belt), and belt tension affects both resistance and wear. [C2-S32, p. 1]
- For lugged wheels on soft soil, grouser height strongly affects traction, and the preferable operating slip range cited is 10 % to 45 %. [C2-S34, section 4.1, p. 499]

### Dynamic skid-steer models and the power cost of turning
- Dynamic and power models are needed for motion planning in "unstructured, outdoor environments that have substantial changes in elevation, consist of a variety of terrain surfaces, and/or require frequent accelerations and decelerations", e.g. for time-optimal or energy-efficient planning, fewer replans, or planning with a faulty motor. [C2-S23, PDF p. 1] [C2-S24, section I]
- Yu et al.'s dynamic model is characterised by three terrain-dependent parameters — coefficient of rolling resistance, coefficient of friction and shear deformation modulus — and includes motor saturation and power limits, which let it predict velocities correctly when climbing hills. [C2-S24, Abstract]
- Including the closed-loop PID wheel-speed controllers in the model predicted vehicle linear and angular velocities "much better" than the open-loop dynamic model, so the closed-loop model is recommended for planning. [C2-S24, Abstract]
- Identified parameters for a Pioneer 3-AT on vinyl: K = 0.00054 m, rolling resistance 0.0371, friction coefficients 0.4437 and 0.3093, α = 1.5; rolling resistance on asphalt 0.051. [C2-S24, Table II, p. 6]
- Few experiments are needed: fitting K and the friction coefficients from N = 2 up to N = 31 steady-state circles changed them only modestly (16.7 % for K, 9.9 % and 7.0 % for the friction coefficients). [C2-S24, section V, p. 6]
- Compared with Ackermann and differential steering, the wheels or tracks of a skid-steered vehicle roll and slide at the same time in general curvilinear motion, which makes accurate kinematic and dynamic models difficult to develop; the source lists as other disadvantages that skid steering tends to be energy-inefficient, difficult to control and (for wheels) to wear tyres faster. [C2-S23, PDF p. 2]
- In a turn the outer motor always consumes power, but as the turning radius decreases from infinity the inner motor first consumes power, then generates power (the outer side drags the inner side backwards through the body), then consumes power again; the two transition radii can be predicted from the model and were seen in experiments. [C2-S23, PDF p. 5; PDF p. 18; PDF p. 20]
- Earlier power models based on Coulomb sliding friction "can lead to incorrect predictions for larger turning radii" and, being written from the terrain's side, cannot give per-motor power. [C2-S23, PDF p. 4]
- Seegmiller et al. note that, for a skid-steered hydraulic-drive vehicle, a first-order powertrain model (time constant and delay, estimated online with an EKF) predicted wheel speeds that closely matched encoder data during deceleration. [C2-S28, section 4, Fig. 5, p. 21]

### Online and learned estimation of slip and effective track width
- Endo et al. estimate both track slip ratios online from two track encoders plus a gyro: the gyro-measured yaw rate gives one equation (θ̇ = [v_r(1−a_r) − v_l(1−a_l)]/2d) and an empirical slip-ratio relation gives the second; they call this slip-compensated odometry with gyro (SCOG). [C2-S25, sections II.B–II.D, eq. (1)–(7)]
- With the slip ratios known, the inverse kinematics gives the track speeds needed for a desired (V, Ω), so path-following laws developed for wheeled robots can be used on the tracked vehicle. [C2-S25, section IV.A, eq. (11)]
- Helmick et al. (Mars rover research platform Rocky 8) fuse IMU and visual odometry in a Kalman filter, compare it with the wheel-kinematic estimate, and treat the difference as a slip vector that the path follower compensates (heading loop plus lateral "crab" loop). [C2-S26, Abstract; section 6.2, eq. (24)–(25)]
- They accept kinematic odometry into the filter only if the Mahalanobis distance of its residual passes a χ² test (5 degrees of freedom, threshold 11.07 at 95 %); otherwise the measurement is discarded and slip is declared. [C2-S26, section 5, eq. (22), p. 13]
- Galati and Reina estimate an "equivalent track" B_s online with a scalar EKF from the left–right track speed difference (encoders) and the yaw rate (IMU), using ω_z = ΔV/B_s; the filter is switched off during straight driving "due to the lack of excitation". [C2-S29, section 1; section 3.2, eq. (10)–(16), p. 5]
- Çiloğlu and Kutluay estimate the left and right track ICR positions online with an EKF (states: position, yaw and the ICR coordinates, modelled as constants with random-walk noise) from GPS and IMU, following Pentzer et al., and feed a variable artificial track width to a pure-pursuit controller. [C2-S30, sections 1–2]
- On their 16 t tracked vehicle (track width 2.17 m) the estimated track ICRs clustered around +1.7 m and −1.75 m, and the dominant effective width of about 3 m is a 36 % amplification over the ~2.2 m physical width; the authors state that the majority of the ICR estimates lay within the predicted bounds of −3 m to 3 m. [C2-S30, section 5, Table 4; section 6.2]
- That filter "needs logical initial conditions to converge quickly", obtained from a multibody model, and its process noise was tuned by trial and error. [C2-S30, section 7]
- Liu et al. (tracked agricultural sprayer) estimate left and right slip ratios with an EKF whose state is [x, y, θ, s_L, s_R], fusing RTK–IMU pose with track speeds; the slip ratios are treated as slowly varying and updated only in the measurement step. [C2-S31, section 3.2.1, eq. (11), p. 7]
- Liu et al. note that "true slip ground-truth is not directly measurable in field conditions"; they judged the estimator by smooth estimates and physically plausible behaviour (the inner track slipped more in each turn direction). [C2-S31, section 4.2, p. 12]
- Seegmiller et al. calibrate slip models by integrated prediction error minimisation (IPEM): instead of fitting the differential equation, they fit the model's integrated prediction over a chosen horizon, which needs only low-frequency state observations and gives parameters that optimise simulation accuracy for that horizon; an online EKF form is also given. [C2-S28, Abstract; section 1, pp. 3–4; section 2.4.2]
- On the six-wheel skid-steered Crusher, learned slip surfaces showed forward slip decreasing with |angular velocity|, lateral slip depending mainly on centripetal acceleration, and angular slip "predominantly a linear function of angular velocity"; slip also depended on the gravity vector (slopes). [C2-S28, section 6.1, p. 30]
- Angelova et al. propose a framework in which terrain type is recognised from appearance and a nonlinear slip model is then learned per terrain type; this paper addresses only the second part, learning slip as a nonlinear function of terrain slope for a known terrain type (soil, sand, gravel, asphalt), with a prediction error of about 15 %, comparable to the slip measurement error. [C2-S27, Abstract; section III]
- Okawara et al. argue that the differential-drive model ignores lateral motion and slip, that slip depends on terrain so the wheel model "must be maintained online", that a linear skid-steer model cannot capture large-slip nonlinearity, and that an offline-trained network cannot adapt to terrain changes; they train a neural kinematic model online inside a LiDAR–IMU–wheel factor graph. [C2-S33, section 1, pp. 1–2]
- In their tests the learned network parameters for grass ("uneven and soft") were clearly separated from those for flat hard terrains, and the online learning ran in about 0.03 s per step, faster than the 0.1 s LiDAR period. [C2-S33, section 4.2.2, Fig. 9, p. 14]

### How other platforms calibrate and validate motion models
- Endo et al. (tracked CV-04: tread 500 mm, track contact length 400 mm, 25 kg) identified the slip exponent n with a stereo motion-capture system (9 mm horizontal accuracy, 30 fps) on p-tile, plywood and artificial turf, by least squares on log |a_l/a_r| vs log |v_r/v_l|. [C2-S25, section III, Table I, p. 2873]
- On a path of straight lines and 15–30 cm radius arcs at 112.8 cm/s, wheeled-type odometry ended at (22 cm, 188 cm, 2.02 rad), gyro odometry at (13 cm, −6 cm, 3.15 rad) and SCOG at (2 cm, −3 cm, 3.15 rad) from the goal pose; gyro-only odometry fixed heading but the vehicle still ran inside the target path in turns. [C2-S25, section V.C, p. 2875]
- Helmick et al. used a Leica total station (±2 mm, ±0.2°) on four prisms as ground truth in a Mars yard and the Mojave Desert (sandy slopes up to 25°), and a tilted sandbox; visual odometry stayed within 2.5 % of distance travelled. [C2-S26, section 7, pp. 16–17]
- On a 10° sand slope, continuous slip compensation reduced the mean square path offset error from 0.54 m² to 0.05 m². [C2-S26, section 7.3, p. 18]
- Seegmiller et al. validated slip models with a high-end IMU and differential GPS, scoring pose prediction error over overlapping 2 s intervals (and, in a separate test, on holdout data); on Crusher (dirt road and tall dry grass on steep slopes, lateral accelerations up to 0.5 g from slopes) online calibration cut mean prediction error from 1.8 m to a few centimetres and reduced the standard deviation of along-track, cross-track and heading error by 72 %, 83 % and 90 %. [C2-S28, section 6, p. 26; section 6.1, pp. 27–28; Fig. 12, p. 30]
- Crusher's "effective turn rate is only a third of the commanded rate", partly because four of six wheels slip sideways in a tight turn; on a hill the high-side wheels lose load so turn commands can produce sideways translation instead. [C2-S28, section 6.1, p. 31]
- For odometry calibration of a differential-drive robot, 28 trajectories between surveyed points (2 mm repeatability) reduced systematic error by about 75 % in the worst case. [C2-S28, section 3, p. 19]
- Çiloğlu and Kutluay first validated a multibody model against field data, then tested on a stabilised dirt road with about 7 % grade: pure-pursuit mean absolute path error fell from 17.1 cm to 9.6 cm (about 44 %) with ICR estimation; in simulation maximum error fell from 94 cm to 36 cm. [C2-S30, sections 6.1–6.2, 7]
- Liu et al. tested on cultivated soft farmland on a loop with 2.5–5 m turn radii at 0.35 and 0.75 m/s, three repeats each, with an RTK–IMU module (its 2–3 cm position accuracy was used to set the filter's measurement noise); slip-aware tracking cut maximum lateral deviation by 78.1 % and average deviation by 50.6 % (0.35 m/s), and by 63.1 % and 57.6 % at 0.75 m/s, compared with fuzzy pure pursuit. [C2-S31, Abstract; p. 8; section 4.2, p. 12]
- Yu et al. validated their dynamic model with steady-state circles at 0.2 m/s over commanded radii from 10^−0.7 m to 10^4 m, comparing measured and simulated wheel speeds, torques and power, plus a partial lemniscate path. [C2-S23, PDF pp. 19–22, section 5] [C2-S24, section V]
- Galati and Reina drove turning manoeuvres on sand, gravel, mud and asphalt, logging motor currents, sprocket speeds and vertical acceleration at 120 Hz in ROS, and used equivalent track, currents and vibration spectra as a terrain "fingerprint"; mud was detected with about 72 % success. [C2-S29, section 4, pp. 7–10]

### How a skid-steer / tracked vehicle moves (question 1)
- Because all wheels (or the tracks) are aligned with the vehicle's long axis, turning requires wheel slippage; the no-slip, pure-rolling assumptions of ordinary wheeled-robot kinematics do not apply. [C2-S01, section I]
- Mandow et al. describe the motion with three ICRs: one for the whole vehicle and one for each tread. The two tread ICRs and the vehicle ICR lie on one line parallel to the vehicle's lateral axis, and every tread has the same angular velocity as the vehicle. [C2-S01, section II, Fig. 2]
- The tread ICR positions are given by x_ICRl = (α_l·V_l − v_y)/ω_z and x_ICRr = (α_r·V_r − v_y)/ω_z, and the shared longitudinal coordinate by y_ICR = v_x/ω_z (Mandow's frame: Y is forward). α_l and α_r are correction factors for effects such as tyre inflation or belt tension. [C2-S01, section II, eq. (2)–(5)]
- The vehicle ICR goes to infinity when driving straight, but the tread ICRs stay bounded (finite) near straight-line motion; this boundedness holds for "kinematic" motion where centrifugal effects are neglected and slip comes only from steering. [C2-S01, section II]
- Knowing the two tread ICRs (plus α_l, α_r) gives a 3×2 matrix mapping the two track speeds to (v_x, v_y, ω_z); for a symmetric vehicle the matrix is identical to an ideal differential drive whose "wheels" sit at the tread ICRs. [C2-S01, section II, eq. (6)–(8), Fig. 3]
- The difference from real wheels is that tread ICRs are "dynamics-dependent and always lie outside of the tread centerlines because of slippage"; less slippage puts them closer to the vehicle. [C2-S01, section II]
- The matrix has no inverse (3 outputs, 2 inputs), which expresses the non-holonomic restriction: lateral velocity cannot be commanded directly. [C2-S01, section II] [C2-S01, section IV.B.2]
- Kozlowski and Pazderski write the lateral constraint as v_y + x_ICR·θ̇ = 0 (their frame: x forward), a non-integrable (non-holonomic) constraint; wheels are tangent to the path only when ω = 0. [C2-S02, section 2.1, eq. (6), (12)]
- Caracciolo, De Luca and Iannitti (four-wheel skid-steer ATRV-2) point out that to follow a curved path the wheels must skid laterally and cannot be tangent to the path, and that the ICR may move out of the wheelbase, causing loss of motion stability; they therefore argue for a dynamic rather than a kinematic model for control design. [C2-S36, section 1, p. 2632]
- Their model assumes a rigid vehicle on a horizontal plane, speed below 10 km/h, no longitudinal wheel slip, and a tyre lateral force that depends on the wheel's vertical load. [C2-S36, section 2, p. 2633]
- The vertical load on each wheel is set by the centre-of-mass position: front wheels carry (b/(a+b))·mg/2 and rear wheels (a/(a+b))·mg/2, where a and b are the distances from the centre of mass to the front and rear axles. [C2-S36, section 2.2, p. 2633]
- With Coulomb friction and a uniform contact pressure on hard ground, the lateral wheel forces are μ·F_zi·sgn(ẏ_i), and the resistive yaw moment is M_r = μ·(abmg/(a+b))·(sgn(ẏ1) − sgn(ẏ3)) + f_r·(tmg/2)·(sgn(ẋ2) − sgn(ẋ1)), i.e. proportional to vehicle weight and dependent on the centre-of-mass position (t: half the track width, f_r: rolling-resistance coefficient); lateral load transfer from centrifugal forces is neglected at low speed. [C2-S36, section 2.2, eq. (3)–(5), p. 2634]
- To keep the ICR inside the wheelbase they add an "operative" nonholonomic constraint ẏ + d0·θ̇ = 0 with 0 < d0 < a, which fixes the ICR's longitudinal position. [C2-S36, section 3.1, eq. (7), p. 2634]
- The same ICR-based model is used for wheeled skid-steer robots (Pioneer P3-AT, Clearpath Jackal) and tracked vehicles, with left/right track or wheel-side speeds as inputs. [C2-S01, section I] [C2-S07, section 3, eq. (1)–(3)]
- On flat, uniform ground with Coulomb friction, a power-dissipation (least-energy) analysis of a tracked vehicle predicts zero lateral velocity and the differential-drive forward velocity ẋ (which does not vary with track length L), with θ̇ = C(S_r − S_l) where C depends on track geometry; when L = 0, C = 1/(2W) and the vehicle behaves like a differential-drive robot. [C2-S06, section III.A, Remark, Fig. 2]
- For a Rover Robotics Flipper tracked robot (track length 0.42 m, track-centre spacing 0.27 m) the power-dissipation model gives θ̇ ≈ 1.5(S_r − S_l) for continuous track contact and θ̇ ≈ 1.27(S_r − S_l) with grouser-tip contact on hard ground; the manufacturer's identified model uses 1.37 and the paper's "differential drive" comparison model uses 2.38. [C2-S06, Table I, section IV.A–B, eq. (6), section V.A]
- When turning, measured forward velocity was a little lower than every model predicted, which the authors attribute to track–ground slippage and track deformation. [C2-S06, section V.A]
- Shamah gives the textbook similar-triangles turn radius R = (B/2)(v_o + v_i)/(v_o − v_i) and yaw rate, and a slip-corrected version where each side's speed is multiplied by (1 − i), i being that side's longitudinal slip; without a slip model, wheel speeds and turn radius "can only be assumed to be estimates". [C2-S09, pp. 9–10, eq. (7)–(9)]
- Wheel-level slip is defined as slip ratio s = 1 − V_wx/(rω) (longitudinal) and slip angle α = arctan(V_wy/V_wx) (sideways). [C2-S05, section II, eq. (4)]
- Rabiee and Biswas extend the differential-drive model with per-side slip ratios and a longitudinal ICR offset x_ICR, which are solved each time step from a friction model and the commanded wheel accelerations. [C2-S05, section III, eq. (8)–(12)]
- Skid-steer turning costs more power than steered turning: in a point turn, skid-steer power was about double that of an explicitly steered point turn, and power and torque rise as turn radius shrinks. [C2-S09, Abstract]

### Effective track width and how it varies (question 2)
- Mandow defines a steering efficiency χ = L/(x_ICRr − x_ICRl), with L the distance between tread centrelines; χ = 1 means no slippage (ideal differential drive), and χ is between 0 and 1. [C2-S01, section II, eq. (9)]
- Wang et al. and Rabiee and Biswas use the inverse ratio, the "ICR coefficient" χ = (y_l − y_r)/B = 2y_0/B ≥ 1, i.e. effective width divided by physical width; this is the quantity a width multiplier represents. [C2-S04, section 2.1, eq. (15)] [C2-S05, section II, eq. (1)]
- Baril et al. show the symmetric extended differential-drive model is the ideal model with the physical width b replaced by an estimated "virtual width" b̂ = 2y_0, plus one slip factor α; it models longitudinal slip and turning losses but still assumes no lateral skidding (v_y = 0). [C2-S03, section III.B, eq. (8)–(9)]
- Pioneer P3-AT, tread centreline spacing L = 0.4 m: identified steering efficiency χ on asphalt was 0.6949 (pneumatic 50 psi), 0.7112 (pneumatic 20 psi) and 0.7612 (solid rubber); on smooth concrete 0.7098, 0.7053 and 0.7460. [C2-S01, section IV.A, Tables I–II]
- The loss of thrust while turning was larger on asphalt than on concrete "due to greater friction", seen as a lower χ; solid wheels turned more efficiently than pneumatic tyres. [C2-S01, section IV.C]
- The P3-AT factory model assumes x_ICR = 0.3 m for pneumatic tyres, whereas a no-slip model would use 0.2 m (half of L = 0.4 m). [C2-S01, section IV.B.1]
- Wang et al. (P3-AT on tile, B = 0.40 m) measured χ = 1.4662 at λ = 0 down to 1.4115 at λ = 7, where λ = (v_l + v_r)/(v_r − v_l) is 0 for a spin in place and grows as the turn widens; they fitted χ(λ) = 1 + 0.4728/(1 + 0.0538·|λ|^½) for λ in [0, 10]. [C2-S04, section 2.1, eq. (13); section 3.4, Table 3, eq. (44)–(45)]
- In the same experiments χ "remains almost constant" as speed rises from 0.1 to 0.5 m/s at a given λ. [C2-S04, section 3.4, Fig. 12b]
- The same authors report the P3-AT's default model uses a constant χ = 1.5. [C2-S04, section 3.5]
- Clearpath Warthog (590 kg, wheel radius 0.3 m, physical width b = 1.2 m in the model): trained virtual width b̂ = 4.46 m on concrete and 3.08 m on snow; slip factor α = 0.94 (concrete) and 0.86 (snow). [C2-S03, Table I]
- The ideal differential-drive model performed better on snow than on concrete, i.e. motion on the slippery surface was closer to ideal differential drive; on hard concrete the turning motion comes from wheel deformation, on snow from terrain deformation. [C2-S03, section IV; section V, Fig. 5]
- For the same command, the Warthog rotated more on snow than on concrete, and above about 30° of commanded rotation per metre the snow curve grows faster than the concrete curve. [C2-S03, section V, Fig. 7]
- Angular prediction error of the (linear) symmetric model was correlated with the wheel commands and peaked where one side was commanded about twice the speed of the other, which the authors read as a non-linearity no linear model captures. [C2-S03, section V, Fig. 8]
- For a Jackal, a higher angular acceleration gave a lower measured angular velocity for the same pair of wheel speeds, which implies (our inference, not the paper's framing) that the effective width also depends on acceleration, not only on terrain. [C2-S05, section V, Fig. 5]
- Shamah (Nomad rover, width 1.97 m): to actually drive 4 m, 8 m and 12 m circles, the inner/outer wheel speeds had to be changed by trial from the kinematic values (e.g. 4 m: theory 0.11/0.19 m/s, used 0.08/0.21 m/s, giving a 4.2 m radius). [C2-S09, p. 15, Table 1; p. 32, Table 3]
- In the DRIVE study, the transfer functions for the Warthog on ice and asphalt (Fig. 9) show angular slip depending "almost uniquely on the angular speed command"; there, the maximum achieved angular speed was above half of what the ideal differential-drive model predicts. [C2-S08, p. 12, section V]
- On the Husky, median angular slip was similar across mud (0.735 rad/s), grass (0.708 rad/s) and asphalt (0.690 rad/s), all within each other's interquartile ranges. [C2-S08, p. 11]
- Power-dissipation analysis predicts that the turning coefficient depends on track length and width (geometry), not only on friction, when ground pressure and friction are uniform. [C2-S06, section III.A, Remark]
- Grousers change the result: on hard ground where only grouser tips touch, the Flipper's predicted coefficient drops from 1.5 to 1.27. [C2-S06, section IV.A–B]

### Load, mass distribution, inertia and slope (what else moves the ICRs)
- Martínez et al. describe the constant-ICR (horizontal) model as valid on hard horizontal terrain "under low inertia": there the tread ICRs keep almost constant local coordinates outside the tread contact lines, and a symmetric mass distribution about the longitudinal axis gives x̄_ICRl = −x̄_ICRr, and about the lateral axis ȳ_ICR = 0. [C2-S38, section II.A, p. 2]
- In steady-state dynamic simulations of the 258 kg tracked robot Auriga-α (track centreline distance 0.42 m, centre of mass 0.015 m to the side of and 0.04 m ahead of the frame origin; 441 track-speed pairs from −1 to 1 m/s), track ICR positions varied considerably; averaging gave constant asymmetric ICRs x_ICRl = −0.458 m, x_ICRr = 0.630 m, y_ICR = 0.0482 m. [C2-S37, section II, Table I, Fig. 3, p. 2]
- They attribute the variation to the inertial force F_i = −M ω × v_CM (M vehicle mass, ω yaw rate, v_CM centre-of-mass velocity), which is near zero only in straight motion and in on-spot turning; because the centre of mass is off-centre, on-spot turning needed V_l = −0.67134·V_r rather than V_l = −V_r. [C2-S37, section III.A, eq. (4)–(13), pp. 2–3]
- Their steering-efficiency index η = (x_ICRr − x_ICRl)/L (1 for an ideal differential drive, above 1 when slippage resists turning — the same quantity as a width multiplier) rose from roughly 2.5 to roughly 4 as the signed inertial-force term grew (read from Fig. 7(c)); the fitted inertia-based model halved velocity errors against constant ICRs (ω_z RMSE 0.0637 vs 0.1433 rad/s). This is a simulation result; experimental identification is left as future work. [C2-S37, section III.B, eq. (19); Fig. 7(c); Table II, p. 5; section V, pp. 5–6]
- On firm slopes the tread ICRs change continuously while turning; changes depend mainly on roll, pitch and the turn direction, with |V_r − V_l| having little influence, and on inclines the outer tread's traction improves when heading downhill and worsens when heading uphill, so the vehicle slides down "even on small inclines". [C2-S38, section II.B, p. 2]
- For the four-wheel robot Lazaro (contact rectangle half-width 0.2 m) turning on the spot on a tilted board (0°–7.6°), the tread ICRs expanded from their level values (x̄_ICRl = −0.44 m, x̄_ICRr = 0.42 m) with tilt; the distance between tread ICRs stayed relatively constant except in the "aggravated sliding down" cases, where it grew. [C2-S38, section III.A, Fig. 2, p. 3]
- Their slope model adds two parameters (λ1, λ2) to the horizontal ICR model, driven by measured roll and pitch; on the 1090 kg, eight-wheel J8 rover (5 psi tyres, maximum payload 567 kg, contact rectangle half-width 0.6 m) on smooth concrete the level parameters were x̄_ICRl = −0.980 m, x̄_ICRr = 1.073 m, ȳ_ICR = 0.206 m. [C2-S38, section III; section IV, Table I, pp. 4–5]
- In the J8 on-spot turn on a ≈3.6° slope, the level (horizontal) model's mean squared position error along the global y axis was 0.957 against 0.006 for the slope model. [C2-S38, section IV.C, Table II, p. 5]
- A distributed-parameter tracked-vehicle simulator that includes non-uniform track loading (downhill part of the track more loaded, uphill part unloaded; centre-of-mass height changes the load transfer) gave a cumulative trajectory difference of 25.6 % (4 m over 15.6 m) against the uniform-load assumption on a randomly generated sloped terrain, while each simulation loop took 11 ms instead of 0.49 ms. [C2-S40, section 8.1, p. 18]
- In the same simulations, at 0.8 m/s gravity dominated the loading and centrifugal load transfer to the outer track was not visible. [C2-S40, section 8.1, p. 18]
- Payload can also change the rolling radius: the Warthog's very low tyre pressure varies with extra payloads and operating temperature, and the manufacturer's wheel radius overestimated the real radius, saturating the reachable wheel speed. [C2-S08, section V, pp. 9–10]
- On a 330 kg, 1.45 m-wide four-wheel skid-steer UGV on concrete, the track width back-calculated from wheel speeds and yaw during steady turns was 1.9270–2.2259 m, "significantly much larger than the actual width" (about 1.33–1.54 times, our arithmetic); slip ratios of the four wheels differed more when the turn radius was below 20 m. [C2-S39, Table 1, p. 6; Table 2, p. 8; p. 14]

### Calibration experiments (question 3)
- Simple symmetric calibration (from Pedraza et al., used by Mandow): command equal and opposite track speeds so the vehicle spins about its centre, measure the actual rotated angle φ, and compute x_ICR ≈ (∫V_r dt − ∫V_l dt)/(2φ). [C2-S01, section III, eq. (11)]
- The longitudinal factor α is found by measuring the actual distance d travelled in a straight line: α ≈ 2d/(∫V_r dt + ∫V_l dt). [C2-S01, section III, eq. (12)]
- This simple method does not capture centre-of-mass asymmetry or mechanical misalignment. [C2-S01, section III]
- Full asymmetric calibration: log internal readings (encoders/inertial) and an external reference (laser scanner or differential GPS), split the path into N short segments at the external sensor rate, and choose the five parameters {x_ICRl, x_ICRr, y_ICRv, α_r, α_l} that minimise the summed squared error in (Δx, Δy, Δφ); Mandow used a genetic algorithm. [C2-S01, section III, eq. (13)]
- Mandow's P3-AT ground truth was an RTK/DGPS receiver (precision under 1 cm, 5 Hz); encoder ticks were logged every 10 ms from several joystick-driven paths. [C2-S01, section IV.C]
- Wang et al. drove constant-velocity circles on 8 path curvatures × 5 speeds (40 experiments) and measured heading with a fixed SICK LMS400 laser scanner looking at a plate on the robot; y_0 per run is (∫ω_r r dt − ∫ω_l r dt)/(2φ). [C2-S04, section 3.1, eq. (25); section 3.2]
- The laser method's estimated ICR error was Δy_0 = 0.007 m and the maximum relative error of χ was under 1%. [C2-S04, sections 3.3–3.4]
- Baril et al. used lidar ICP (point cloud registration) as ground truth, driving trajectories planned to maximise the range and coverage of commanded wheel velocities, with separate training and evaluation trajectories. [C2-S03, section IV]
- Their loss is a sum of squared Mahalanobis distances between model-predicted and ground-truth pose over N segments; they split data by distance travelled (spatial horizon) rather than time, which made it easy to drop zero-command outliers. [C2-S03, section IV, eq. (13)]
- Models trained on short horizons did better at short evaluation horizons and worse at long ones; 2 m or 5 m training horizons converged quickly, and a 2 m evaluation horizon was used. [C2-S03, section V, Fig. 4]
- The DRIVE protocol automatically sends random step commands covering the vehicle's whole true command space, each held for six seconds, and splits each step into one transient window and two steady-state windows (commands at 20 Hz). [C2-S08, section I; p. 8, Fig. 5]
- DRIVE first finds the true command space with an "unloaded calibration": lift the vehicle or disengage the wheels, command maximum longitudinal speed, measure maximum wheel speed, compute maximum angular speed from the measured base width and wheel radius via the ideal model, then check that commanded speeds match encoder speeds over the whole space. [C2-S08, p. 15, section VI.B]
- Minimum test area: at least one dimension 1.5 × the distance travelled at maximum speed for six seconds (20 × 45 m for the Warthog, 9 × 6 m for the Husky). [C2-S08, p. 15, section VI.B]
- Dixit and Burdick measured a tracked robot following a 2 m diameter circle (0.5 m/s, 0.5 rad/s) open-loop with an OptiTrack motion-capture system at 120 Hz. [C2-S06, section V.A]
- Online calibration is an alternative: Zuo et al. estimate the ICR parameters (X_v, Y_l, Y_r, α_l, α_r) continuously inside a visual-inertial estimator, because they vary with track–terrain interaction and roughness; parameters converged quickly from deliberately wrong initial values. [C2-S07, Abstract; section 3; section 6.1]
- Those parameters are identifiable (with camera + IMU + wheel odometry) except when: one wheel's speed stays zero; angular velocity stays zero; wheel speeds and angular velocity are all constant; or the left speed is always proportional to the right. [C2-S07, section 5.2, Lemma 2]
- Pentzer et al. (2014) tracked the track ICRs online with an EKF using position and heading measurements on a 118 kg skid-steer robot (reported by Baril et al.; not downloaded). [C2-S03, section II]

- Focchi et al. identified tracked-vehicle slip on a slippery parquet floor with motion capture at 200 Hz: left/right sprocket speeds were sampled uniformly in [−10, 10] rad/s in 0.65 rad/s steps, each pair held for 2 s to reach steady state, and the steady slips were averaged and fitted with a regressor (decision trees chosen over neural networks and RBF interpolation). [C2-S40, sections 4.1–4.2, p. 8]
- They parametrise longitudinal slip by the two sprocket speeds rather than by turn radius, because the earlier radius-based definition (Moosavian et al.) has a singularity when the turn radius equals half the track separation and the inner track speed is zero. [C2-S40, section 4.2, p. 8]
- Martínez et al. identify their slope model in two stages: first on level ground by manual driving at different speeds and curvatures (as in Mandow), then one on-spot turn on an incline; ground truth came from 3D LiDAR SLAM (lidarslam_ros2, about every 100 ms) and parameters minimise the summed squared position and heading errors. [C2-S38, section III.B, eq. (13), p. 4; section IV, pp. 4–5]
- Zhou et al. found that wheel-speed-based turning geometry overestimated the width until they used a high-accuracy INS velocity as reference, from which they computed each wheel's slip ratio and the true turning radius. [C2-S39, Abstract; p. 8]

### Calibration and model accuracy on grass
- Rabiee and Biswas's public skid-steer dataset (Clearpath Jackal, 16 kg) includes grass alongside tile and asphalt: training driving (977 s, 764.1 m), open-loop constant-velocity turning at several radii (2127 s, 771.1 m) and long-distance runs (685 s, 561.5 m) on grass, with mean wheel slip velocities of 0.08–0.13 m/s. [C2-S05, section IV, Table I, p. 4]
- On that dataset, the best of five kinematic models (extended differential drive with five parameters, EDD5) had a mean angular position error of 18.9 % on grass (17.6 % asphalt, 21.1 % tile) and a linear error of 6.2 % on grass (14.2 % asphalt, 11.6 % tile) over 1 s prediction horizons; a Gaussian-process correction brought the grass figures to 5.6 % and 5.7 %. [C2-S41, section IV.D, Table I]
- The DRIVE protocol was run on grass with both a 75 kg Husky (200 steps, 0.63 km, 23.4 min) and a 470 kg Warthog (200 steps, 2.14 km, 36.2 min), using ICP lidar localisation for linear velocity and an Xsens IMU for yaw rate. [C2-S08, section IV, Table 1, Fig. 6, pp. 9–10]
- Okawara et al. (Rover Robotics Rover mini) found that reusing kinematic-model parameters learned on an indoor flat floor when driving on grass ("uneven and soft") gave translational relative trajectory errors about six times worse than parameters learned for the terrain (average 0.158 m vs 0.028 m), while reusing floor parameters on wood tiles cost only about a factor of two. [C2-S33, section 4.1, Table 2, p. 10]
- Seegmiller et al. calibrated Crusher's slip models on dirt roads and tall dry grass on steep slopes (see "How other platforms calibrate and validate motion models"). [C2-S28, section 6.1, pp. 27–28]

### Rotation centre relative to the chassis (question 4)
- For the P3-AT the vehicle ICR's longitudinal coordinate (y_ICRv) was always about 1 cm behind the frame origin for every terrain and tyre, which the authors say confirms it depends only on the centre of mass. [C2-S01, section IV.C, Tables I–II]
- If the centre of mass is closer to one side, that side slips less and its tread ICR moves closer to the vehicle while the other moves farther out; a centre of mass towards the front or rear concentrates pressure on part of the tread, pulls the tread ICRs closer and shifts y_ICRv; tyre pressure also moves the ICRs. [C2-S01, section II]
- The eccentricity index e = (x_ICRr + x_ICRl)/(x_ICRr − x_ICRl) measures left/right asymmetry and was close to zero (−0.0213 to 0.0921) for the P3-AT. [C2-S01, section II, eq. (10); Tables I–II]
- Kozlowski and Pazderski: if the ICR's longitudinal coordinate x_ICR "goes out of the robot wheelbase, then the robot skids into a lateral direction and loses its stability"; controllers therefore impose a fixed x_0 inside the wheelbase, x_0 ∈ (−a, b). [C2-S02, p. 484, section 3.1, eq. (60)–(61)]
- Choosing x_0 = 0 makes the skid-steer model look like a standard two-wheel robot; x_0 is a tuning coefficient, not a structural property. [C2-S02, p. 484, section 3.1]
- Earlier, Kozlowski and Pazderski note that controlling the lateral velocity is impossible without knowing the ICR's longitudinal projection. [C2-S02, section 2.1]
- Rabiee and Biswas model x_ICR as the longitudinal offset of the ICR from the geometric centre, solved each step from the dynamics (centre of gravity, forces), i.e. it moves during manoeuvres. [C2-S05, section III, Fig. 1]
- Baril's trained asymmetric model for the Warthog placed the vehicle ICR longitudinal offset at x_v = −2.57 m (concrete) and −2.71 m (snow); the authors note their near-symmetric vehicle made the symmetric model a good fit. [C2-S03, Table I; section V]
- Zuo et al.'s ICR model includes the vehicle ICR's longitudinal position X_v as a parameter estimated online (Eq. (2)); the ideal case is X_v = 0. [C2-S07, section 3, eq. (2)–(3)]
- On flat uniform ground, a symmetric tracked vehicle has zero lateral velocity for any track speeds (i.e. it rotates about a point on its lateral axis). [C2-S06, section III.A, Remark]
- Nav2's setup guide describes `base_link` as attached "typically at its main chassis and its rotational center". [C2-S19, section on REP 105]
- Nav2's `footprint` polygon is defined with `base_link` as origin, and `robot_radius` generates a circle centred at `base_link`. [C2-S20, first section]
- Odometry and control both depend on the ICR: dead-reckoning is computed from the ICR matrix, and wheel commands are obtained from V_l = (v_y + x_ICRl·ω_z)/α_l and V_r = (v_y + x_ICRr·ω_z)/α_r. [C2-S01, section IV.B.2, eq. (16)–(17)]

### Accuracy of the models
- Trained models (extended differential drive, ROC-based, full linear) all performed "vastly better" than the ideal differential-drive model on the Warthog. [C2-S03, section V, Fig. 5]
- The asymmetric extended and full linear models gave the best translation predictions (they include lateral motion); the symmetric two-parameter model gave the best rotation predictions and is less prone to overfitting. [C2-S03, section V, Fig. 6]
- Estimating the ICR parameters online reduced final drift by roughly an order of magnitude in almost half of 20 test sequences (e.g. 632.64 m run: 1.93 m vs 28.91 m without). [C2-S07, section 6, Table 1]
- On the P3-AT, the optimised asymmetric model gave mean squared Δx and Δy errors of 0.00010 and 0.00011 versus 0.00183 and 0.00468 for the factory model. [C2-S01, section IV.D, Table III]
- The friction-based model beat the extended differential-drive, full linear, ROC-based and enhanced kinematic models on long-distance runs and matched them on constant-velocity runs; its advantage shows under acceleration. [C2-S05, section V]

- On a small LIMO tracked robot (4.36 kg) following a chicane on parquet and on a soapy plastic sheet, a controller using the identified slip model cut the average Cartesian tracking error by 48 % (7.2 cm to 3.8 cm) and the average orientation error by 24 % (4.2° to 3.2°) on parquet, and by 55 % and 22 % on the slippery surface, compared with a standard unicycle controller. [C2-S40, Table 6, p. 13; section 7.2, p. 14]
- Focchi et al. report that, contrary to Moosavian et al.'s claim that track slip is almost independent of speed at limited speeds, their data showed a dependency of slip on the vehicle's absolute velocity as well as on turn radius. [C2-S40, section 4.2, p. 8]

### Reference controllers and configs — ROS 2 / ROS 1 controllers and Clearpath (product-specific)
- ros2_controllers `diff_drive_controller` (Humble) computes `wheel_separation = wheel_separation_multiplier * wheel_separation` and per-side radii the same way, in both `update()` and `on_configure()`. [C2-S10, lines 142–145, 298–300]
- Wheel commands use the corrected separation: `velocity_left = (linear − angular·wheel_separation/2)/left_wheel_radius`, right with a plus sign. [C2-S10, lines 257–260]
- Odometry uses the same corrected separation (`odometry_.setWheelParams(wheel_separation, ...)`) and computes angular velocity as `(right_vel − left_vel)/wheel_separation_`. [C2-S10, line 302] [C2-S12, lines 81–84]
- The parameter is documented only as a "Correction factor when the actual wheel separation differs from the nominal value", default 1.0; `wheel_separation` is described as the "Shortest distance between the left and right wheels. If this parameter is wrong, the robot will not behave correctly in curves." [C2-S11, lines 18–24, 39–43]
- `wheels_per_side` is described as "important to take the wheels slip into account when multiple wheels on each side are present"; it should equal the number of control signals per side (Husky: 1). [C2-S11, lines 26–30]
- ROS 1 `ros_controllers` (Noetic) uses the same scheme: `ws = wheel_separation_multiplier_ * wheel_separation_` for odometry and for `vel_left/right = (lin ∓ ang·ws/2)/radius`. [C2-S14, lines 296–299, 492–493]
- The ros2_controllers kinematics page covers only the no-slip differential-drive model (v = (v_r + v_l)/2, ω = (v_r − v_l)/w); it contains no skid-steer or ICR-offset model for differential drive. [C2-S13, section "Differential Drive Robot"]
- No lateral velocity or ICR offset term exists in the controller; only three scalars (separation multiplier, left and right radius multipliers) are available. [C2-S10] [C2-S11, lines 39–53]
- Clearpath's current Husky (A200) config: `wheel_separation: 0.555`, `wheel_separation_multiplier: 1.875`, `wheels_per_side: 1 # actually 2, but both are controlled by 1 signal`. [C2-S15, lines 16–20]
- Clearpath's Jackal (J100) config: `wheel_separation: 0.37559`, `wheel_separation_multiplier: 1.5`. [C2-S16, lines 16–20]
- The ROS 1 Noetic Husky config also uses `wheel_separation_multiplier: 1.875`. [C2-S18, line 26]
- The older `husky` repository's Humble branch uses `wheel_separation: 0.512` with `wheel_separation_multiplier: 1.0`. [C2-S17, lines 16–20]
- Mandow's analysis of the P3-AT's manufacturer model shows the same idea in vendor firmware: a symmetric model set by two integers, Ticksmm (containing α) and Revcount (ticks per full turn in place, containing x_ICR), used for both odometry and speed commands. [C2-S01, section IV.B.1, eq. (14)–(15)]

## Recommended practice
What the sources recommend, in order.
1. Do not use the ideal differential-drive model unmodified for a skid-steer vehicle; at minimum use an effective width and a longitudinal slip factor. [C2-S03, section V, Fig. 5] [C2-S07, section 3]
2. Start with the symmetric two-parameter model (slip factor α and virtual width b̂): it gave the best rotation prediction and has fewer parameters to overfit. [C2-S03, section V]
3. For a first estimate, spin in place with equal and opposite track commands and measure the true rotation (for the width), and drive straight and measure the true distance (for α). [C2-S01, section III, eq. (11)–(12)]
4. Measure the true motion with an external reference (RTK/DGPS, fixed laser scanner, lidar ICP or motion capture), not with the wheel encoders being calibrated. [C2-S01, section IV.C] [C2-S03, section IV] [C2-S04, section 3.2] [C2-S06, section V]
5. For a full (asymmetric) model, drive varied paths with a range of turning and forward speeds, split the log into short segments, and fit parameters by minimising the pose-increment error; use separate training and evaluation paths. [C2-S01, section III, eq. (13)] [C2-S03, section IV]
6. Choose the training horizon to match how the model will be used (controllers use short horizons). [C2-S03, section V]
7. Make sure the data include turning with changing, non-proportional wheel speeds; otherwise the parameters cannot be identified. [C2-S07, section 5.2, Lemma 2]
8. Identify parameters per terrain (and tyre/track condition); constant parameters are optimised "for a specific robotic task, according to typical path motions, particular soil types, and speed ranges". [C2-S01, section III] [C2-S03, Table I]
9. If the turn radius varies widely, consider a radius-dependent width (ROC model), or estimate parameters online. [C2-S04, section 3.4, eq. (44)] [C2-S07, section 6.1]
10. Before systematic testing, verify commanded vs encoder wheel speeds with the wheels off the ground, and limit acceleration to avoid drivetrain and tyre damage. [C2-S08, p. 15, sections VI.A–B]
11. When predicting motion for planning or control, model the closed-loop wheel-speed controllers (and motor saturation/power limits) rather than only the open-loop vehicle; the closed-loop model predicted velocities much better. [C2-S24, Abstract]
12. Use a shear-displacement friction model rather than pure Coulomb friction when turning resistance, torque or power across turn radii matters. [C2-S23, PDF pp. 10, 20]
13. Calibrate by minimising integrated prediction error over the horizon the planner or estimator will use; this needs only low-rate position observations. [C2-S28, Abstract; pp. 3–4]
14. Where slip changes with terrain, estimate it online (slip ratios, track ICRs or equivalent track) with an EKF from GNSS/IMU plus track speeds [C2-S29] [C2-S30] [C2-S31], or from a gyro and an empirical slip relation [C2-S25], and update the width-type parameter only while the vehicle is turning (straight driving gives no excitation). [C2-S25, section II.D] [C2-S29, section 3.2] [C2-S30, section 2] [C2-S31, section 3.2.1]
15. Give an online ICR/slip filter sensible initial values (e.g. from a prior model or calibration run) so it converges quickly. [C2-S30, section 7]
16. Before trusting wheel odometry in a fused estimator, test it against an independent estimate (IMU + visual odometry) with a Mahalanobis/χ² gate and treat rejections as slip. [C2-S26, section 5, eq. (22)]
17. Treat constant-ICR (effective-width) parameters as valid only for hard, level ground at low inertia; on slopes, add roll/pitch-dependent terms identified with a separate on-incline turn. [C2-S38, sections II.A, III.B]
18. Re-identify slip parameters on each operating surface rather than reusing those from a different terrain; parameters learned on a flat floor gave about six times larger translational error on grass. [C2-S33, section 4.1, Table 2, p. 10] [C2-S40, section 7.2]

## Common mistakes
- Using the ideal differential-drive model on a skid-steer vehicle: residual errors are "much higher than for any of the trained models". [C2-S03, section V, Fig. 5]
- Feeding skid-steer wheel odometry into a localiser as if it were a differential drive: in almost half of 20 sequences the drift was about ten times larger than when the ICR parameters were modelled. [C2-S07, section 6, Table 1]
- Calibrating only with a spin-in-place test: it gives a symmetric model and misses centre-of-mass asymmetry and mechanical misalignments. [C2-S01, section III]
- Treating the effective width as a constant across surfaces or turn radii: identified values differ between asphalt and concrete, concrete and snow, and with path curvature. [C2-S01, Tables I–II] [C2-S03, Table I] [C2-S04, Table 3]
- Calibrating with motions that make parameters unobservable (straight lines only, constant speeds, or left speed always proportional to right). [C2-S07, section 5.2, Lemma 2]
- Choosing a training horizon unrelated to the use: short-horizon models degrade at long horizons and vice versa. [C2-S03, section V, Fig. 4]
- Letting the ICR's longitudinal position leave the wheelbase (e.g. through the control law): the robot skids sideways and loses stability. [C2-S02, p. 484]
- Running aggressive, high-traction calibration without acceleration limits: after 13 km at 4 m/s², the Warthog showed gear skipping, wheels bent inward by 30° on asphalt, and tread wear from 12 mm to 2 mm. [C2-S08, p. 15, section VI.A]
- Setting `wheel_separation` wrong in diff_drive_controller: "the robot will not behave correctly in curves". [C2-S11, lines 18–24]
- Modelling track/wheel–ground friction as pure Coulomb friction: it predicts the same resistance torque at every turning radius, which contradicts experiments, and Coulomb-based power models "can lead to incorrect predictions for larger turning radii". [C2-S23, PDF pp. 4, 10]
- Predicting vehicle motion with an open-loop dynamic model that leaves out the wheel-speed controllers: the closed-loop model predicted velocities much better. [C2-S24, Abstract]
- Using wheeled-type (no-slip) odometry for path following on a tracked vehicle: heading errors built up in turns and the vehicle stopped far from the goal (22 cm, 188 cm, 2.02 rad) versus (2 cm, −3 cm) with slip-compensated odometry. [C2-S25, section V.C]
- Fixing only heading with a gyro and ignoring track slip in the translational estimate: heading was right but the vehicle still ran inside the target path in turns. [C2-S25, section V.C]
- Updating an effective-track estimate during straight driving, where there is no excitation; the filter is switched off then. [C2-S29, section 3.2]
- Accepting wheel odometry into a fused estimate without a consistency check during slip; rover work gates it with a χ² test and discards it when slipping. [C2-S26, section 5]
- Using an offline-trained learned motion model on changing terrain: it cannot adapt to terrain-dependent slip. [C2-S33, section 1]
- Treating the slip estimate as validated when no slip ground truth exists; field work can only check it indirectly (plausibility, path-tracking error). [C2-S31, section 4.2]
- Using constant (level-ground) tread ICRs on slopes: the model cannot predict the vehicle sliding down while turning (J8 mean squared position error along the global y axis 0.957 vs 0.006 with a slope model). [C2-S38, section IV.C, Table II]
- Assuming the ICR spread is independent of inertia: in simulation it changed with the inertial force, and constant ICRs doubled velocity errors compared with an inertia-based model. [C2-S37, section IV, Table II]
- Back-calculating track width from wheel speeds without an external velocity reference: slip makes the computed width much larger than the real one. [C2-S39, p. 8]
- Parametrising slip by turn radius alone: the formulation becomes singular when the inner track stops (turn radius equal to half the track separation). [C2-S40, section 4.2, p. 8]
- Assuming a manufacturer wheel radius holds under load: low-pressure tyre radius changes with payload and temperature. [C2-S08, pp. 9–10]

## Disagreements between sources
- **Is a constant width enough?** Mandow et al. and Baril et al. use constant, terrain-specific ICR parameters [C2-S01, section II] [C2-S03, section III.B]; Wang et al. find χ varies with turn radius and fit a function of λ [C2-S04, section 3.4]; Rabiee and Biswas find that the achieved angular velocity for the same wheel speeds varies with acceleration [C2-S05, section V]; Zuo et al. say the parameters "cannot remain constant due to different motions and terrains" and estimate them online [C2-S07, section 3].
- **ROC-based model value:** Wang et al. report their radius-of-curvature model improves dead reckoning [C2-S04, section 3.5], while in Baril et al.'s Warthog tests the ROC-based model was the one trained model that did not perform like the others [C2-S03, section V, Fig. 6].
- **Identification vs. first principles:** most sources require experimental identification per terrain [C2-S01, section III] [C2-S03, section IV]; Dixit and Burdick derive the turning coefficient from track geometry and friction assumptions "without requiring experimental identification" and report accuracy similar to a vendor-tuned model [C2-S06, section I; section V.A].
- **Speed dependence:** Wang et al. found χ almost constant with speed (0.1–0.5 m/s) [C2-S04, section 3.4]; Baril et al. found angular prediction errors correlated with wheel-speed commands [C2-S03, Fig. 8]; Mandow notes ICR boundedness holds only when centrifugal effects are negligible [C2-S01, section II]. Focchi et al. report that Moosavian et al. treated tracked-vehicle slip as almost speed-independent at limited speeds, whereas their own data showed a dependence on absolute velocity [C2-S40, section 4.2, p. 8].
- **Husky multiplier:** Clearpath's `clearpath_common` (A200) and the Noetic `husky` config use 1.875 [C2-S15, line 20] [C2-S18, line 26], while the `husky` repo's humble-devel branch uses 1.0 with a different nominal separation (0.512 m vs 0.555 m) [C2-S17, lines 16–20].
- **Symmetric vs asymmetric model:** Baril et al. found the symmetric model best for rotation and the asymmetric/full linear models best for translation [C2-S03, section V]; Mandow found the asymmetric model clearly better in position than the symmetric one on the P3-AT [C2-S01, Table III].
- **Coulomb vs shear-displacement friction:** Yu et al. state that Caracciolo et al. and Kozlowski and Pazderski model sliding friction and moment of resistance with Coulomb friction (maximum shear as soon as there is relative motion) [C2-S23, PDF pp. 4, 7], while Yu et al., following Wong and Chiang, use a shear stress that grows with shear displacement and show measured torques falling with turn radius [C2-S23, PDF pp. 8, 20].
- **Does surface change the slip behaviour?** Endo et al. found their slip-ratio exponent n "does not change significantly due to the surface" (0.48–0.62 on tile, plywood, artificial turf) [C2-S25, section III.B]; Galati and Reina found the equivalent track clearly terrain-dependent (1.159 m asphalt to 1.329 m mud) and use it to classify terrain [C2-S29, Table 1]; Okawara et al. found learned parameters on grass separated from hard terrains [C2-S33, Fig. 9]. (The quantities differ: n describes the left/right slip split, not the width.)
- **Slip parameters as constants or functions:** Liu et al. and Çiloğlu and Kutluay treat slip ratios / ICRs as slowly varying random-walk states [C2-S31, section 3.2.1] [C2-S30, section 2]; Seegmiller et al. model slip as a function of commanded velocities and gravity [C2-S28, section 6.1]; Okawara et al. argue a linear model cannot capture high-slip nonlinearity and use a neural network [C2-S33, section 1].
- **Kinematic or dynamic model for skid-steer control?** Caracciolo et al. argue that the ICR can leave the wheelbase and that a dynamic model is therefore needed for control design [C2-S36, section 1, p. 2632]; Martínez et al. argue that ICR kinematics can "implicitly take into account some dynamical effects" and extend it with roll/pitch (slopes) or inertia terms instead of full dynamics [C2-S38, section I, p. 1] [C2-S37, section V].
- **Is the ICR spread constant for a given terrain?** Mandow et al. and Martínez et al. (level ground, low inertia) treat it as constant [C2-S01, section II] [C2-S38, section II.A]; Martínez et al.'s own tracked-robot simulations show it varying with inertial force [C2-S37, Fig. 7(c)], and on slopes the distance between tread ICRs stayed roughly constant except when the vehicle slid downhill [C2-S38, section III.A].
- **Treatment of skid steering in textbooks:** Siegwart and Nourbakhsh include slip-skid steering as a degenerate but useful configuration [C2-S21, p. 61]; Lynch and Park exclude tanks and skid-steered vehicles from their wheeled-robot kinematics altogether [C2-S22, p. 513].

## Open questions
- No open-access source gave identified ICR/effective-width values for a **rubber-belt tracked** robot on **natural grass**; the tracked-vehicle sources found are the Flipper on hard floor [C2-S06], the CV-04 on tile, plywood and artificial turf (slip-ratio split only) [C2-S25], a 40 kg rubber-tracked robot on sand, gravel, mud and asphalt [C2-S29], a 16 t tracked vehicle on a dirt road [C2-S30], tracked agricultural vehicles [C2-S31] [C2-S32], the Auriga-α tracked robot in simulation [C2-S37], and tracked robots on parquet and slippery plastic [C2-S40]. Martínez et al. 2005 (IJRR 24(10):867–878, ICR kinematics of tracked robots) and Moosavian and Kalantari 2008 were not downloaded.
- Wong's *Theory of Ground Vehicles* and Wong and Chiang (2001) skid-steer theory are cited by [C2-S04], [C2-S05], [C2-S23] and [C2-S25] but no open copy was found; their shear stress–shear displacement result is summarised here only as applied by Yu et al. [C2-S23, PDF pp. 7–8], and their numerical results (e.g. effect of turning radius and centre-of-gravity shift on track slip) are not summarised.
- None of the sources state whether the effective-width correction should be applied equally to the command (inverse) path and to the odometry (forward) path; ros_controllers / ros2_controllers apply it to both by design [C2-S10] [C2-S14], and no source evaluates applying it to only one.
- No source quantified how the rotation centre should be chosen for Nav2 `base_link`/footprint on a vehicle whose ICR moves with load and manoeuvre; Nav2 only says `base_link` is "typically" at the rotational centre [C2-S19].
- How the effective width varies with speed above about 0.5 m/s on light robots was not tested by [C2-S04]; Baril et al. note higher concrete speeds were not reached for safety [C2-S03, section V].
- No source explains how Clearpath derived 1.875 (Husky) and 1.5 (Jackal).
- Of the new tracked-vehicle sources, only Endo et al. tested on artificial turf (slip-ratio exponent, not effective width) [C2-S25], and Galati and Reina reported equivalent track on sand, gravel, mud and asphalt but not grass [C2-S29]; no source gave an identified effective width for a rubber-belt tracked robot on natural grass.
- The terramechanics sources give the shear-displacement law and slip for straight-line traction [C2-S32] [C2-S34] and a firm-ground turning model for wheels [C2-S23]; no downloaded source gave shear-modulus K or friction values for turf, and Wong and Chiang (2001) and Ding et al. (2020, turning resistance on soft terrain) were not downloaded (paywalled or blocked).
- Online estimators were validated only indirectly for slip itself: Liu et al. note that true slip ground truth is not measurable in the field [C2-S31, section 4.2]; Çiloğlu and Kutluay compared estimated ICRs to values computed from GPS/IMU [C2-S30, section 6.2].
- Pentzer et al. (2014, EKF track-ICR estimation, J. Field Robotics 31(3)) and Martínez et al. (2005, IJRR 24(10)) remain paywalled and are known here only through [C2-S03] and [C2-S30].
- **Load / payload sweep (gap check 2026-09-28):** no source found measured the effective width of the *same* vehicle at several payload masses. What exists is indirect: Coulomb models make the resistive yaw moment proportional to weight and dependent on centre-of-mass position [C2-S36, eq. (5)]; a simulation shows the ICR spread changing with the inertial force −M ω × v_CM [C2-S37]; payload changes low-pressure tyre radius [C2-S08, pp. 9–10]; and non-uniform loading matters on slopes [C2-S38] [C2-S40, section 8.1]. Searches (direct: "skid steer payload effective track width", "added mass ICR"; general: "load transfer skid steering", "centre of gravity shift tracked vehicle steering", "parameter uncertainty skid-steer model"; neighbouring: agricultural tracked tractors, military tracked-vehicle steering) found only paywalled terramechanics papers (e.g. Wong & Chiang 2001; J. Terramechanics studies of pressure distribution and CoG on steering).
- **Grass (gap check 2026-09-28):** grass data now cover wheeled skid-steer robots (Jackal dataset [C2-S05] [C2-S41], Husky and Warthog DRIVE runs [C2-S08], Rover mini [C2-S33], Crusher [C2-S28]), but none reports an identified effective width (width multiplier) on grass for a rubber-tracked robot.
- Kitano and Kuma (1977) on tracked-vehicle turning dynamics is known here only as a reference in [C2-S06]; no open primary copy was found.
- **Track tension and contact-length / track-gauge ratio (source audit 2026-09-28):** no downloaded source measured how track tension or the ratio of track contact length to track gauge changes the effective width of a tracked robot.
- **Benchmark protocol (source audit 2026-09-28):** the sources use different accuracy metrics and manoeuvre sets (see *How it is tested*); no downloaded source proposes a single agreed benchmark protocol for comparing skid-steer or tracked kinematic models.
- Huskić et al., "High-speed path following control of skid-steered vehicles", *IJRR* 38(9):1124–1148, 2019 (speed-dependent kinematic parameters at higher speeds) was not downloaded: the HAL copy (hal-02994317) is behind a bot check.
- The 2023 survey "A Survey of Off-Road Mobile Robots: Slippage Estimation, Robot Control, and Sensing Technology" (*J. Intelligent & Robotic Systems*, 2023, doi:10.1007/s10846-023-01968-2) was found but the publisher PDF could not be downloaded (bot check); it is not cited.
